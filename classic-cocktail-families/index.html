Classic Cocktail Families: Balance, Specs and Bar Basics

In short

Almost every classic cocktail balances four elements: strong (the spirit), weak (dilution and mixers), sweet, and sour and/or bitter. Change one ingredient in a balanced recipe and you get a new drink in the same family: spirit-forward drinks, short sours, highballs and long sours (Collins, sling and sparkling sours).

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

Classic cocktails can look like an endless list of recipes. In fact they fall into a few families that share the same structure. Learn the structure, and you can explain why a Daiquiri, a Margarita and a Sidecar are close relatives, or why a Tom Collins is simply a sour made long.

This page covers the families studied in the spirits courses, with spec tables organised by role (strong, sour, sweet, bitter) instead of recipe lists. It also covers the techniques, tools and glassware every bartender and candidate should know.

Balance: the four points of every cocktail

A balanced cocktail is one where no element dominates. Bartenders work with two scales: strong vs weak, and sweet vs sour and/or bitter.

ElementWhat it doesTypical ingredients
StrongThe base and centre of the flavourA spirit, usually 40% abv or more
WeakLowers strength, lengthens and refreshesMelted ice, juices, soda, tonic, cola, ginger beer, dairy
SweetRounds and softensSugar, simple syrup, flavoured syrups, liqueurs
Sour and/or bitterGives freshness and structure; stops the drink being cloyingFresh citrus juice, cocktail bitters, bitter aperitifs

One ingredient can play more than one role. Vermouth brings sweetness, bitterness and some acidity. A liqueur is both strong and sweet. Ice deserves special attention: in a stirred Martini, the water from the ice is the main weak element, so stirring time is part of the recipe.

Short drinks and long drinks

The families split into two groups. Short drinks are small, with few ingredients, and get their weak element mainly from ice melted while shaking or stirring. Long drinks are larger and take their weak element from a mixer such as soda, tonic or juice.

Spirit-forward cocktails

Spirit-forward cocktails are the strongest family: a base spirit softened with something sweet and seasoned with something bitter. The simplest is spirit, sugar and bitters; more complex versions use vermouth or a liqueur. They are stirred or built so they stay clear and silky.

Learn the variations as small changes to the base recipe:

  • Martini: less vermouth makes it drier, more makes it wetter; olive brine makes it dirty; a Vodka Martini swaps the base.
  • Manhattan: dry vermouth makes a Dry Manhattan, equal sweet and dry a Perfect Manhattan; Scotch instead of rye makes a Rob Roy.
  • Old Fashioned: swap the whiskey for aged rum and you have a rum Old Fashioned.
  • Add cream and you move into the Alexander family, such as the Brandy Alexander.

Short sours

The sour is the most productive family in the bar: spirit, fresh citrus and sweetener, shaken hard with ice and strained. Shaking chills and dilutes the drink and also gives it a light, airy texture. Some sours add egg white (or a vegan foaming agent such as aquafaba) for a silky foam; in a Pisco Sour it is essential.

A useful starting spec for a short sour is roughly two parts spirit, one part fresh citrus and between half and one part syrup, depending on how rich your syrup is. Taste, then adjust: if a sour is too sharp, add sweetness in small steps rather than more spirit.

Highballs

A highball is the simplest mixed drink: a spirit lengthened with a mixer and served over plenty of cubed ice. Gin and tonic, vodka and tonic, and whisky and soda are all highballs. A glass packed with ice keeps the drink cold without melting too fast; a few cubes melt quickly and water it down.

Long sours: Collins, sling and sparkling sours

A long sour is a short sour lengthened with something fizzy. The Collins family tops the sour with soda water and serves it over ice. Slings add extra layers such as liqueurs and bitters. Sparkling sours replace soda with sparkling wine.

In exams, the quickest way to place a drink is to ask two questions: is it short or long, and does it contain citrus? Short with citrus is a sour; long with citrus and soda is a Collins-style long sour; long without citrus is usually a highball.

From one recipe to many: how families grow

Once you see a cocktail as four roles, inventing or recognising variations becomes simple. Keep the structure and change one element. Swap the strong part and a Tom Collins becomes a Vodka Collins or a Pierre Collins. Swap the sweet part for a liqueur and a Daiquiri-style sour becomes a Sidecar or Margarita. Swap the weak part, soda for sparkling wine, and you move from a Collins to a French 75. This is exactly how exam questions are built, so practise naming the family before the drink.

Techniques: build, stir, shake

  • Build: pour the ingredients straight into an ice-filled glass. Used for highballs and many long drinks.
  • Stir: stir with cubed ice in a mixing glass using a bar spoon, then strain with a Julep strainer. Gives a clear, smooth drink with controlled dilution.
  • Shake: shake hard with cubed ice in a shaker, then strain with a Hawthorne strainer, often through a fine sieve as well (double straining) to catch ice shards.
  • Muddle: crush mint or citrus gently in the glass or shaker to release juice and oils.
  • Blend: mix with crushed ice in a blender for a smooth, frozen texture.

Ingredients, tools and glassware

Four ingredients matter in every bar. Ice must be cold and dry: cubed ice for shaking, stirring and most serves, crushed ice for drinks like the Mojito. Simple syrup is sugar dissolved in water, often two parts sugar to one part water. Citrus juice should be squeezed fresh and kept chilled, because it loses freshness within hours. Cocktail bitters are a seasoning, used by the dash, never as a main ingredient.

Flavoured syrups add complexity without extra alcohol; orgeat, made with almonds and floral water, is a well-known example. Garnishes should add something, usually an aroma that echoes or lifts the drink, such as a citrus twist over a Martini or mint on a Mojito. Cut fresh garnishes on the day and throw them away at the end of service.

Common mistakes

  • Putting the Mojito with the short sours: it is a long drink topped with soda.
  • Shaking a Martini or Manhattan when the recipe calls for stirring: shaking clouds the drink and changes the texture.
  • Forgetting that dilution is an ingredient: under-stirred drinks taste hot and harsh, over-stirred ones taste thin.
  • Using bottled citrus juice: the drink loses freshness and balance.
  • Treating vermouth as only sweet: it also brings bitterness and acidity.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main classic cocktail families?

The families studied in spirits courses are spirit-forward cocktails (like the Old Fashioned, Manhattan, Martini and Negroni), short sours (Daiquiri, Whiskey Sour, Pisco Sour, plus variations such as the Margarita and Sidecar), highballs (Cuba Libre, Moscow Mule) and long sours, which include the Collins, sling and sparkling sour sub-families, such as the Tom Collins, Mojito, Paloma and French 75.

What makes a cocktail balanced?

Balance comes from two scales. The first is strong versus weak: the spirit against the dilution from ice and any mixers. The second is sweet versus sour and/or bitter: syrups and liqueurs against citrus, bitters or vermouth. A balanced drink lets you taste the spirit without harshness, and is neither cloying nor sharp.

What is the difference between a sour and a Collins?

A sour is a short drink of spirit, fresh citrus and sweetener, shaken and strained, with no long mixer. A Collins takes the same three ingredients, serves them over ice in a tall glass and tops them with soda water. In other words, a Collins is a sour made long, which is why the Tom Collins is often called a long gin sour.

When should a cocktail be shaken and when stirred?

Shake drinks that contain citrus juice, egg white, cream or other cloudy ingredients: shaking mixes them thoroughly and adds texture and air. Stir drinks made only of clear ingredients, such as spirits, vermouth and bitters, as in the Martini, Manhattan or Negroni: stirring chills and dilutes them while keeping them clear and silky.

Is a Mojito a sour or a highball?

It belongs to the long sours, in the Collins family. A Mojito has the classic sour structure, with rum, lime juice and sugar, and adds mint leaves before being lengthened with soda water and served over crushed ice. A highball, by contrast, is simply a spirit and a mixer, often without any citrus juice.

Which cocktails do I need to know for the WSET Spirits exams?

At Levels 1 and 2 you should know the main families and their best-known examples: Old Fashioned, Manhattan, Martini, Negroni and Sazerac; Daiquiri, Whiskey Sour, Pisco Sour, Margarita, Sidecar and Cosmopolitan; Cuba Libre and Moscow Mule; Tom Collins, Mojito and Paloma. Focus on the family and the role of each ingredient rather than exact measures.

What is double straining?

Double straining means pouring a shaken drink through a Hawthorne strainer and a fine sieve at the same time. Shaking breaks the ice into small shards, which would float in the drink and dilute it as they melt. The fine sieve catches them, giving a cleaner look and a smoother texture, especially in drinks served without ice.
