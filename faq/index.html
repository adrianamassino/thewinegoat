WSET Preparation: Frequently Asked Questions

In short

Most learners start at Wine or Spirits Level 1 or 2; Levels 1 and 2 are multiple-choice only, while Wine Level 3 adds 4 short written answers and a blind tasting. Plan roughly 6, 28 and 84 hours of study for Levels 1, 2 and 3, practise actively, and remember that The Wine Goat is an independent study app, not WSET.

These are the questions we hear most often from people preparing a WSET wine or spirits exam: which level to choose, how each exam works, how long to study, how to practise tasting, and how The Wine Goat app fits in. Each answer starts with the short version, then adds the detail that matters.

Choosing the right WSET level

How many WSET wine and spirits levels are there?

Wine has four levels: Level 1, Level 2, Level 3 and the Level 4 Diploma. Spirits has three: Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3. Each level adds depth and a new kind of assessment. Levels 1 and 2 are tested with multiple-choice questions only, while Level 3 adds short written answers and a blind tasting, and the Diploma is a multi-unit programme for professionals.

Do I have to take Level 1 before Level 2?

No. Level 2, in both wine and spirits, is designed for people with little or no prior knowledge, so many learners start there. Level 1 is still a good choice if you want a gentle introduction or a quick certificate for a new job in hospitality. Level 3 is a different matter: Level 2 or equivalent knowledge is recommended before you enrol.

Which WSET level do I need to work in wine?

Level 2 is a solid foundation for restaurant service, retail and wholesale, because it covers the grapes, regions and label terms you meet every day. Level 3 is widely seen as the professional standard for buyers, sommeliers, educators and sales roles, since it trains you to explain why wines taste as they do. The Diploma is for those aiming at senior or expert positions.

Should I study wine or spirits first?

Start with the drinks you work with or enjoy most, because motivation and real-life examples make studying much easier. The two pathways are independent: you do not need a wine qualification to take a spirits course, or the reverse. Some topics overlap, such as tasting technique, oak and service, so the second pathway usually feels lighter once you have completed a level of the first.

Exam format and study time

What is the format of the WSET Level 1 exam?

Both Wine Level 1 and Spirits Level 1 are tested with 30 multiple-choice questions in 45 minutes. You need 70%, which means 21 correct answers, to pass, and there are no higher grades. Each question has four options and one correct answer. The pass mark is high because the questions are straightforward, so careless mistakes matter more than difficult ones.

What is the format of the WSET Level 2 exam?

Wine Level 2 and Spirits Level 2 each have 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes. A pass needs 55% (28 correct answers), a merit 70% and a distinction 85%. Questions often test precise details such as label terms, minimum ageing periods or the grape behind a regional name, so a broad overview gives a pass while the higher grades need detail.

What is the format of the WSET Level 3 wine exam?

Wine Level 3 has two units. The theory paper combines 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written answers, and the tasting unit asks you to assess two wines blind using the Systematic Approach to Tasting. You need at least 55% in each unit, and a strong theory score cannot make up for a weak tasting. Higher results earn a merit or a distinction.

How long does it take to prepare for each level?

WSET's guideline study time, including class hours, is about 6 hours for Level 1, about 28 hours for Wine Level 2 (about 26 for Spirits Level 2) and about 84 hours for Level 3. In practice, most learners prepare Level 1 in one or two weeks, Level 2 in four to six weeks and Level 3 in ten to sixteen weeks of regular daily study.

Do wrong answers cost marks in the multiple-choice papers?

No. Each correct answer earns one mark and a wrong answer simply earns nothing, so you should never leave a question blank. If you are unsure, eliminate the options you know are wrong and choose among the rest. Practising with timed questions helps you manage this calmly: at Level 2 you have about 72 seconds per question, which is usually enough.

Preparing effectively

What is the best way to prepare for a WSET exam?

Use active methods rather than rereading. Learn each chapter, then test yourself straight away with practice questions; build key facts into flashcards reviewed with spaced repetition; review every mistake; and take timed mock exams in the final weeks. Short daily sessions work better than long weekend ones. For Level 3, practise written answers and tasting notes from the very start.

How can I practise blind tasting for Level 3?

Taste regularly and write a full note on every wine using the Systematic Approach to Tasting: appearance, nose, palate and conclusions on quality. Use the exam's exact terms, because synonyms do not score. Ask someone to pour wines for you without showing the bottle, compare classic styles side by side, and justify quality with balance, length, intensity and complexity.

Are practice questions online the same as the real exam questions?

No. Real exam papers are confidential, and no app or website can offer them. Good practice questions, like those in The Wine Goat, are written to reflect the published syllabus and the exam format, so they train the same knowledge and the same skill of choosing between plausible options. Treat them as training, not as a preview of your paper.

How many mock exams should I take?

Two to four full mocks is a good target for Level 2, and at least three for Level 3, spread over the final third of your preparation. Take the first one early enough to act on the result. Run each mock under real conditions, with the timer on and no notes, then spend more time reviewing your mistakes than you spent on the mock itself.

Tasting and practical skills

Do I need to taste a lot of wine to pass Level 2?

No. Wine Level 2 is assessed only by multiple-choice questions, so you can pass without tasting widely. Tasting still helps memory: a grape or region you have tasted becomes a real flavour rather than a name on a list. A few classic examples of the principal grapes, tasted side by side with a short structured note, are often enough to make the theory stick. Sharing bottles with other candidates keeps it affordable and adds discussion.

How many wines should I taste before the Level 3 tasting exam?

There is no magic number, but regular practice matters more than volume. Aim to write a full note with the systematic approach for two or three wines a week from the start of your preparation, and more in the final month. Include the classic styles of the syllabus and taste some wines blind, poured by someone else, so you learn to describe what is in the glass instead of what the label tells you.

Do I have to identify the wine in the Level 3 tasting?

No. You describe each wine with the systematic approach and reach conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking, but you are not asked to name the grape, region or vintage. Marks come from accurate descriptions of appearance, nose and palate and from a quality assessment justified with balance, length, intensity and complexity. Guessing the wine earns nothing, so focus on precise, consistent observations.

Spirits preparation

What does the WSET Spirits Level 2 exam cover?

Spirits Level 2 has 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes. Fourteen questions cover production, 23 the principal spirits such as whiskies, brandies, rum, Tequila and vodka, 7 flavoured spirits and aromatised wines, and 6 service and cocktails. Because almost half the paper is on the principal spirits, their legal rules, ageing requirements and label terms deserve the largest share of your study time.

Is spirits harder than wine at the same level?

Neither is harder overall; they are different. Spirits involves more production detail, such as stills and maturation, and many legal rules and ageing minimums. Wine involves more geography, with dozens of regions linked to grapes. Candidates with a bar background often find spirits easier to picture, while those who work in restaurants or shops may feel at home with wine. Choose the one that fits your work and interest.

Can I prepare for Spirits Level 3 in the app?

Not yet. Spirits Level 3, like the Wine Diploma, is in preparation in The Wine Goat. Today the app covers Spirits Level 1, which is free with about 210 questions, and Spirits Level 2, with about 1,000 questions, alongside lessons, flashcards, mock exams and a study plan. Spirits Level 3 adds written answers and a blind tasting of two spirits, so a solid Level 2 foundation is the best preparation in the meantime.

Exam day and after

What happens if I fail a WSET exam?

You can usually resit the exam, or at Level 3 only the unit you did not pass, through your course provider, which sets the dates and conditions. Use your result as a diagnosis: sit a full mock to see what is still secure, then build a shorter plan around the chapters or the unit that cost you marks. Most candidates who resit with a targeted plan improve clearly on the second attempt.

Can I take a WSET exam online?

Some course providers offer certain exams online with remote supervision, while others hold all exams in person. Ask your provider which formats are available for your level and what the technical requirements are. If you sit online, rehearse your mocks on the same device and in the same room, so the set-up feels familiar and nothing distracts you on the day.

How should I use my time in the exam room?

Work through the paper once at a steady pace, answering the questions you are sure about and flagging doubtful ones. Check the clock at the halfway point, then return to your flagged questions with the time left. Change an answer only if you find a concrete reason. At Level 3, keep enough time for the written answers and write in full sentences, one explained idea per sentence.

Studying in practice

Is self-study with an app enough without a study group?

Many candidates prepare alone successfully, because practice questions, flashcards and mock exams give the feedback a group would. A study partner adds something different: shared tastings, explaining topics to each other and keeping motivation up. If you study alone, use the explanations in each question as your second opinion and consider at least a few tasting sessions with other people, especially at Level 3.

Can I prepare on my phone only?

Yes, most daily preparation works well on a phone: lessons, questions, flashcards and games are designed for short sessions. For mock exams and Level 3 written answers, a larger screen or a quiet desk helps you concentrate and type comfortably. A good mix is phone for the daily goal and a computer or tablet for timed mocks.
