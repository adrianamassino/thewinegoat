used during maturation. None of the key techniques, the so/era system, biological ageing
or oxidative ageing, are unique to this region, but it is in Jerez that they are exploited to their fullest
The vineyard area surrounds the southern Spanish town
of Jerez de la Frontera that gives the region its name.
Jerez is the centre of the Sherry industry and the wines
must be matured either within the city limits or in one of
the two smaller seaside towns of Sanlucar de Barrameda
and El Puerto de Santa Marfa.

Jerez has a hot, sunny Mediterranean climate although
the vineyards that are nearer the coast are on average a
little cooler and benefit more from the prevailing cool
humid westerly wind known as the poniente.On
occasions the temperature can be sent soaring by the hot
and drying Jevante,which blows from the east. This extra
heat can seriously stress the vines and damage grapes.
Being a coastal region the annual rainfall is high
compared with many other Spanish regions, but rainfall
in the growing season is limited and it is the albariza soil
in the region that ensures the vine can cope.

During the autumn the wines are classified into two
types: those that will be sent for biological ageing and
those that will be sent for oxidative ageing. These
decisions are based on the flavour characteristics of the
wines. Typically, lighter paler wines with more 'finesse'
are selected for biological ageing and darker, richer
'heavier' wines are selected for oxidative ageing. Note
that at this stage the film-forming yeast known as f/or
will have started to grow on the surface of all the wines.
The exact criteria for classification will vary according
to the house style of the producer but this is not a part of
the process that is simply left to chance. Producers need
certain volumes of new wines in particular styles in order
to meet the sales demands of their brands and classify
wines with this in mind. As a simple guide, wines made
from grapes grown in the relatively cooler coastal
vineyards and/or fermented at lower temperatures are
more likely to be suitable for biological ageing . On the
other hand, wines made from grapes grown in the warmer
inland vineyards and/or fermented at higher temperatures
are more likely to be suitable for oxidative ageing .

MATURATION IN THE SOLERA SYSTEM
Sherry has to be aged in 600-litre oak barrels called
butts. The butts are often used to mature unfortified
wines before they are used to mature Sherry so that any
oak flavour is eliminated . The oak is needed to allow
oxygen to reach the wine , and this is further encouraged
by the fact the butts are only ever five-sixths full.
Keeping the maturation environment cool is a
particular challenge in Jerez. Traditional bodegas have
thick whitewashed walls, high ceilings and windows that
po int towards the cooling poniente winds helping to
maintain a relatively cool environment. The earth floors
of these buildings are kept damp to maintain the correct
level of humidity. Some are now air-conditioned.
Sherry is famous for its system of maturation called the
'so/erasystem'. It is a very versatile system that can be
used to sustain biological and oxidative ageing.
The so/erasystem is made up of a number of groups
of butts, referred to here as levels, which hold wines of
different average ages. These levels are called criaderas
and the maturation process involves moving wine
between them over time to achieve a blend of younger
and older wines. To avoid confusion, so/erasystem is
used here to refer to the maturation system as a whole
while so/erais used to refer to the final level in the

Flor and Biological Ageing
Biological ageing requires the presence of flor, which is
made up of a number of yeast strains. Together these
yeast form a thick layer on the surface of the wine. They
feed off the alcohol (and other nutrients) in the wine and
oxygen in the atmosphere to produce carbon dioxide
and acetaldehyde. It is acetaldehyde that gives
biologically aged Sherries their unique flavour.
In order to thrive, f/or needs precise levels of alcohol,
temperature and humidity. Its sensitivity to the ambient
conditions is such that the temperature and humidity of
an individual bodega and even a butt's position within it
can influence the style of the maturing Sherry. Flor
cannot thrive in a wine with an abv above 15.5%, and it
favours cool to moderate temperatures and high levels
of humidity. Consequently, f/or grows more vigorously in
the spring and autumn and dies back in winter and
The so/erasystem is able to sustain a permanent
culture of florin every butt. The butts are only part filled
so that the f/or has easy access to oxygen. Importantly,
this layer of flor also protects the wine from oxidation.
The f/or is also constantly consuming alcohol and other
nutrients and therefore regular additions of new wine
is needed to keep the f/or supplied with these nutrients.
In order to maintain a ready flow of nutrients between
the criaderas,wine is drawn off the so/eraat frequent and
regular periods throughout the year. This also best serves
the style of wine produced. Biologically aged Sherries,
such as Finos,quickly lose their freshness and ideally
need to be consumed as soon as possible after bottling.
Although it is possible to keep the flor culture alive
indefinitely, wine taken from these so/erasystems rarely
has an average age greater than three to four years. As
the average age of the wine increases the overall level of
nutrients in the oldest criadera and so/eradrops and by
the time the average age reaches seven years the levels
are sufficiently low that the f/or can start to fail and
oxidative characteristics develop.
O/oroso,PX and some Muscat Sherries are aged
oxidatively, without the presence off/or . Amontillado is
aged oxidatively after a period of biological ageing. The
impact of oxygen on these wines is greatly enhanced
by the air present in the part-filled butts. In these so/era
systems the introduction of young wine helps to
preserve the base character of the Sherry, which would
otherwise become overly oxidised.
Sherries can be matured oxidatively for upwards of
30 years although very few wines make it to this age.
With age, the alcohol level of these wines rises to
22% abv as the water content evaporates .
The advantage of the so/era system is that it produces a
wine of consistent style and quality. Some Sherries are
the product of only one so/erasystem but this is
relatively rare. Most Sherries, including many of the very
best, are blends of wines from several so/erasystems.

Fino and Manzanilla - These wines have only
undergone biological ageing. They are pale lemon in
colour and usually have aromas of citrus fruit, almonds
and herbs, together with bready notes derived from the
action off/or . The flavours are sometimes referred to as
tangy or salty. They do not improve in bottle and should
be consumed as fresh as possible.

Amontillado - These are wines that have undergone

Sherries with an Indication of Age
There are four categories of age-indicated Sherries. The
top categories are VORS(Vinum Optimum Rare Signatum/
Very Old Rare Sherry) and VOS (Vinum Optimum Signatum/
Very Old Sherry). These indicate that the average age of
the blend is at least 30 years old and 20 years old
respectively, and each batch must be tested to see
whether it complies . The lesser categories, 12 years old
and 15 years old, apply to a whole so/era system, so can
be used more flexibly. Only Amontillado, Palo Cortado,
0/oroso and PX can qualify for age indicated status.
