UNDERSTANDING WINES: EXPLAINING STYLE AND QUALITY

PortPorts are made by adding grape spirit to a fermenting juice to create an alcoholic sweet wine.
This is a technique that has been used in this region since the eighteenth century and it is now
used to make wines of a similar style throughout the world. Port is made in red, white and rose styles but
in this chapter we will focus on red Port.

LOCATIONS
The activities of the Port industry are divided between
the twin coastal cities of Porto and Vila Nova de Gaia
(which lie on opposite sides of the mouth of the river
Douro) and the vineyard area that starts 70 kilometres
upstream of these towns. These two areas are distinctly
different and will be considered separately.

INTHEVINEYARD
The vineyards area is divided into three sub-regions. The
Baixo Cargo produces the lightest wines; the Cima Cargo
is where the greatest number of the top vineyards are
found; and the Douro Superior which, although still
relatively sparsely planted, is also renowned as a source
of top-quality wine.

Growers have to contend with frosts in spring and

Topography
The vineyards in the Baixo and Cima Cargo are planted
on the very tall and very steep slopes that rise up from
the banks of the Douro and its tributaries. The
topography is very varied and this can be exploited by
the growers. For example, there can be clear temperature
differences between the top and the bottom of a slope

simply managing vineyards on such steep slopes is an

Traditionally, the vines were planted on narrow

walls are also expensive to maintain. Nevertheless this

Both of these systems are particularly prone to erosion.
This is particularly true of vinha ao alto, which is simply
not viable on the steepest slopes.

Grape Varieties
The vast majority of red Ports are complex blends of
many different varieties which, in the Douro, tend to be
thick-skinned and high in tannin with black fruit and
floral aromas. There are five varieties that are preferred in
premium Port production: Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz,
Tinta Barroca, Touriga Nacional and Tinto Cao.
IN THE WINERY
Fermentation
Port fermentation is stopped by fortification once the
alcohol level reaches 5-9% abv, to create a sweet wine.
This means the fermentation typically only lasts for
around 24 to 36 hours. Given the time restraints, the
normal extraction techniques used in the production
of red wines are simply not vigorous enough to produce
a wine that has the level of colour and tannin expected
of Port. Instead, other techniques are used.
Foot treading - This is the traditional method of
extraction in Port production and the benchmark
against which all other methods are judged. Large teams
of workers tread the grapes for three to four hours in
shallow granite troughs, or Jagares.Once fermentation
is underway, foot treading stops and the cap is then
regularly punched down to extract more colour and
tannin. This method is not widely used now because it is
so labour intensive but it is still used for some premium
wines.

used to automate extraction and autovinifers are still
widely used. Crushed grapes are put into sealed vats

Fortification
Fortification kills the yeast to create a stable sweet wine
with between 19% and 22% abv. However, although all
Port is sweet, some are sweeter than others. Each
producer has a particular style and the timing of the
fortification will depend on the initial must weight and
the required level of sweetness.
The spirit, or aguardente, that is used can be no
stronger than 77% abv. This, combined with the fact
that alcohol levels are low prior to fortification, means
that significant volumes of spirit have to be used. As an
average 20 per cent of the total volume of a bottle of
Port is spirit. By way of comparison, the amount of spirit
in a bottle of Fino Sherry is approximately 3.5 per cent
of the tota I volume.

Maturation
Ports are generally transported downstream to Vila Nova
de Gaia for maturation. The cooler coastal climate here is

well suited to the slow maturation of Port wines. Some
wines, notably wines destined for Tawny Port, have
always been stored up the Douro, where the higher
temperatures result in faster ageing and a loss in colour.
The advent of fully air-conditioned warehouses has
resulted in more wine being aged in the vineyard area.
Port maturation has traditionally always taken place in
old oak vessels, both small and very large (stainless steel
is now used too). New oak flavours are not a characteristic
that is desired in any Port. There are two broad styles
among red Ports that are a direct consequence of the
type of vessel used: ruby styles and tawny styles.
When bottled, ruby styles of Port have a predominant
primary fruit character. Producers look to minimise the
effect of oxygen on the flavour of these wines and
therefore they will only age these wines for a relatively
short period of time in very large oak vessels or stainless
steel tanks. This is how Ruby, Reserve Ruby, Late Bottled
Vintage (LBV) and Vintage Ports are aged.
Although quality and concentration will vary, these
wines will all be deeply coloured and have intense
primary fruit flavours when bottled. Ruby, Reserve Ruby
and some styles of LBV will be fined and filtered before
bottling and typically do not benefit from bottle ageing.
Some LBVsand all Vintage Ports are not filtered and can
benefit from long bottle ageing. After this extra period of
bottle maturation the wines have a garnet colour and
tertiary cooked fruit and vegetal (prune, leather, wet
leaves) flavours.

True Tawny Ports, on the other hand, undergo long
oxidative maturation in barrels called pipes. The wines
are made in exactly the same way as the ruby styles and
the differences in colour and flavour are entirely down to
the difference in maturation. The wines turn garnet and
finally tawny, although only the very oldest become
fully brown. With age the primary fruit fades, becoming
raisiny and is accompanied by flavours of walnuts, coffee,
chocolate and caramel. These wines throw their deposit
during wood ageing and require little extra treatment in
preparation for bottling. These are fully developed on
release and do not benefit from any extra bottle ageing.
The vast majority of Ports, even vintage styles, are
complex blends of different varieties grown in different
sites and typically blending is an ongoing process that
happens throughout the period of pre-bottle maturation.

blending in White Port, a period of hot maturation in the
Douro and/or heavy fining to remove colour.
Special Categories of Port
Reserve/Reserva - This is a labelling term that can be
applied to both Ruby and Tawny Ports that are of higher
quality than the most basic expressions. A wine's
suitability for this category is determined by an official
tasting panel. Importantly, a ReserveTawny must be
wood aged for a minimum of six years.

Inexpensive Ports
Ruby - These are blends of wines that are typically
between one and three years old. They lack the
concentration, complexity or tannins of more premium
ruby styles.

Late Bottled Vintage (LBV) - These are vintage wines
that have been aged for between four and six years
before they are bottled. This extra ageing, which
typically takes place in large oak vessels, helps to make
these wines more approachable on release, compared
with Vintage Ports. The vast majority of LBVsare fined
and filtered. These wines, which are similar in style to a
high-quality Reserve Ruby Port, are ready to drink on
release and rarely benefit from extra bottle ageing. A
relatively small number of LBVsare unfiltered. These
wines are more similar in style to Vintage Port and they
can often benefit from bottle ageing. Similar to Vintage
Port, unfiltered LBVsform a sediment in the bottle and
often need decanting before serving.

Vintage - Producers must register their intention to
release a Vintage Port in the second year after the
harvest and the wine must be bottled no later than the
third year. All ageing prior to bottling will take place
in either large oak vessels or stainless steel tanks and
the wines are unfined and unfiltered. On release these
are the most concentrated and tannic Ports. Some
consumers choose to enjoy them young but these
wines are capable of ageing in bottle for decades and
as they age they will throw a heavy sediment.
For most producers, a Vintage Port is their flagship
wine. They are only produced on average three times

a decade. They are typically a blend of the finest wines
from a producer's best vineyards. It is up to an individual
producer whether or not they'declare' a particular
vintage; there is not always a consensus. For example,
some houses declared 1991 as a vintage year, others
declared 1992 and some declared both. However, there
was general agreement that 2000, 2003, 2007 and 2011
should be declared.
Single Quinta Vintage Ports are the product of a single
estate, or quinta. The name of the quinta appears on the
label. Some small producers who only own one quinta
will only declare a vintage in the great years. The large
producers who own several quintas have a different
approach. In the best years they will use the pick of all
of their quintas' wines to make a Vintage Port. In years
that are not considered good enough for a vintage to
be declared they will release their best wines as Single
Quinta Vintage Ports. These are considered to be less
prestigious than their Vintage Port, but they are still
high-quality wines. It is quite common for these
producers to age their single quinta wines once they
have been bottled and release them when they are
ready to drink.

Muscat is widely used to make fort ified wine throughout
the world. There are a number of different grape varieties
that are called Muscat but they all have low to medium
acidity and display perfumed aromas of orange blossom,
rose and grape. These varieties can thrive in warm and
hot climates but the best vineyards typically benefit from
some cooling influences, which help to preserve the
grapes' acidity levels and aromas.
Muscats are made in two broad styles: youthful and
unaged or fully developed after an extended period of
ageing.

Youthful, Unaged Muscats
These wines are medium gold in colour, floral and
aromatic. The aim of the grape growing and winemaking
is to make sure that the varietal character is as pure as
possible. They are typically sweet but are rarely luscious.
This type of wine is made in a number of wine regions
throughout the world; one notable example is Muscat
de Beaumes-de-Venise that comes from the Southern
Rhone.
The grapes will be picked when they are ripe and
healthy. Raisining or drying the grapes after picking in
order to concentrate grape sugars is not typically
practised for this style. These techniques add extra dried
fruit characteristics that mask the pure varietal
characteristics.
Once crushed, the juice may be separated from the
skins but some skin contact is widely used to help
increase aromatic intensity and add some richness to
the palate. This can be further enhanced if the skin
contact continues during part or all of the fermentation.
Fermentation is cool and it is stopped by fortification
with 96% abv grape spirit to give a sweet wine. In order
to preserve the primary fruit aromas, the wines are stored

in inert vessels prior to bottling and protected from any
contact with oxygen.

Fully Developed, Aged Muscats
These w ines range in colour from amber to brown,
and can be sweet or luscious. Aged Muscats are also
made in many countries. Some of the most celebrated
examples made in the luscious style come from
Rutherglen in Australia . The style of these wines will
be determined by the timing of the harvest , when the
wines are fortified and the length and type of ageing
that they undergo.
In order to make a sweet aged wine the grapes will be
picked when they are ripe and healthy. However, in order
to make the more luscious style, the producer may want
to allow some raisining to happen prior to picking, but
care has to be taken . Raisining does add extra complexity
to the aromas, but excessive raisining will mean that the
grapes lose too much of their varietal aromas and the
resulting wines can lack an expressive Muscat character.
Fermentation will nearly always take place on the skins.
If a luscious style of wine is being made, the fortification
may take place when the fermentation has only reached
20/oabv.
These wines will then undergo a period of oxidative
ageing that can last for decades. Old wood is essential
(new oak aromas would clash with the grape aromas)
and large oak vessels are widely used. These wines are,
on occasion, aged in warm conditions. As they age, these
wines become amber and finally brown, and develop
pronounced oxidative aromas. Even when they are very
old these wines can still retain their Muscat aromas.
However, it is common for the oldest wines to be given
an aromatic lift by blending in a small amount of mo re
youthful wine prior to bottling.
