How Spirits Are Made: From Raw Material to Bottle (Pot Still vs Column Still)

In short

Every spirit goes through four stages: a raw material is turned into a sugary liquid, yeast ferment it to about 8-10% abv, a still concentrates the alcohol, and the new spirit is then adjusted through ageing, blending, colour, sugar and water. Pot stills work in batches and give lower-strength, more aromatic spirits; column stills run continuously and can reach 95-96% abv for neutral spirits.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

A spirit is an alcoholic drink whose strength has been raised by distillation. That one-line definition hides most of what makes a Cognac different from a vodka or a single malt different from a white rum. The differences come from choices made at each stage of production, and spirits exams test those choices again and again.

This guide follows a spirit from field to bottle. It explains how the two main types of still work and what happens after distillation, with comparison tables and the mistakes candidates make most often.

The four stages of spirits production

In short: raw material, fermentation, distillation, post-distillation. Each stage has a clear aim, and remembering the aim makes the details easy to place.

StageAimKey output
1. Processing the raw materialCreate a sugary liquidA liquid yeast can ferment
2. Alcoholic fermentationTurn sugar into alcoholAn alcoholic liquid of about 8-10% abv, plus flavour compounds
3. DistillationConcentrate the alcohol and select flavoursA colourless, dry new spirit, from about 60% up to 96% abv
4. Post-distillationFix colour, aroma, sweetness and strengthThe finished spirit, usually at around 40% abv

Stage 1: raw materials and sugar

A spirit can be made from any plant material that contains carbohydrates. The raw material gives the distiller two things: food for the yeast, and a set of aromas that can survive into the final spirit, such as the grassy note of cane juice or the peppery, earthy character of agave.

Some raw materials already contain simple sugars: grapes and other fruit, sugar cane juice and molasses. They only need crushing, pressing or diluting. Others store energy in complex carbohydrates that yeast cannot use. Grains and potatoes store starch, while agave stores inulin. These must be converted into sugar first.

  • Barley is malted: the grain is allowed to sprout so it produces enzymes, then dried with hot air. Crushed and mixed with warm water, the enzymes turn the starch into sugar.
  • Other grains are crushed and cooked, then mixed with some malted barley or with bought-in enzymes.
  • Agave hearts (piñas) are cooked, by steaming in ovens or roasting in pits, which turns inulin into sugar before crushing.
  • Potatoes are cooked until they break down, then treated with bought-in enzymes.

Stage 2: fermentation

Fermentation turns sugar into alcohol. Yeast feed on the sugar and produce ethanol, carbon dioxide (which escapes) and many minor compounds called congeners. One important group, the esters, smells fruity. Fermentation usually stops at about 8-10% abv.

This stage matters more than many candidates expect. Distillation can only select and concentrate what is already in the liquid: a congener that was not created in fermentation cannot appear in the spirit. That is why most distillers use carefully chosen yeast, and why some rum makers deliberately push fermentation to build intense tropical fruit aromas.

Stage 3: how distillation works

Distillation separates parts of a liquid by boiling and condensing. Ethanol boils at about 78°C and water at 100°C. When an alcoholic liquid boils, the vapour is therefore richer in alcohol than the liquid it came from. Collect that vapour, cool it in a condenser, and you have a stronger liquid.

Distillation does two jobs at once. It concentrates ethanol, and it selects congeners, keeping some and leaving others behind. The strength at which the spirit is collected decides how much flavour survives.

Distillation strengthAroma intensityTypical examples
Below 80% abvMedium to pronouncedCognac, malt whisky, Bourbon, Tequila, heavy rums
Around 90% abvLightLight rum marks, Scottish grain whisky
95-96% abvNeutralNeutral spirit used for vodka, gin and liqueurs

The rule to remember: the higher the distillation strength, the lighter the spirit. Neutral spirit is defined by law as at least 95% abv in the USA and at least 96% abv in the EU.

The pot still: parts and double distillation

A pot still is a batch still. It is filled, run, emptied and cleaned, and then the cycle starts again. Most are made of copper, which is easy to shape and conducts heat well. Learn the parts in the order the vapour travels:

  • Heat source: usually steam coils or a steam jacket; Cognac stills must be heated by direct flame.
  • Pot: holds the liquid to be distilled; a drain empties it afterwards.
  • Still head and neck: the vapour rises here; the height and shape influence the style.
  • Lyne arm (Scotland) or swan neck (Cognac): the pipe that carries vapour to the condenser.
  • Condenser: cold water turns vapour back into liquid, either in a shell and tube design or a traditional worm tub (required in Cognac).

One pass through a pot still is not enough to make a spirit, so most pot still spirits are distilled twice.

The first distillation mainly removes water. The fermented liquid, at about 10% abv, is boiled and the distiller collects everything until the flow is almost pure water. The result, called low wines, is only about 25-30% abv, because a lot of water comes over with the alcohol. The watery residue is discarded.

The second distillation selects flavour. The low wines go back into a still together with the heads and tails from the previous run, and the distiller makes two cuts:

  • Heads: the first few minutes of liquid, rich in very volatile compounds that smell of solvent. These are set aside.
  • Hearts: when the solvent smell fades, the flow is switched to the spirit receiver. This is the part that will be sold, usually about 70-75% abv overall.
  • Tails: after some hours, heavier compounds with cheesy or plastic notes appear. The flow is switched back and collected until it is almost water.

Heads and tails are recycled into the next batch, so no good alcohol or flavour is wasted. Because a pot still separates compounds only roughly, many congeners travel with the hearts. That gives pot still spirits a medium to pronounced aroma intensity.

The column still: plates and continuous distillation

A column still is a tall vertical still divided by horizontal plates. Every plate acts as a small still, so many distillations happen at once, one stacked on top of another. As long as the flow in equals the flow out, the column can run continuously.

  • Heat source: steam, usually through a heat exchanger, sometimes injected directly into the base.
  • Plates: each holds a layer of boiling liquid; the plates near the top have the highest strength.
  • Bubble caps: force rising vapour to bubble through the liquid on each plate.
  • Downcomers: carry overflowing liquid down to the plate below.

Hot fermented liquid enters near the base, vapour rises, and liquid falls. The most volatile compounds reach the top and are drawn off as heads. Heavier ones gather lower down and are taken off as tails. Watery waste leaves from the base. The hearts can be drawn from whichever plate gives the strength the distiller wants. A short column with few plates gives a flavourful, low-strength spirit, as in Armagnac or rhum agricole. A tall column with many plates can reach 95-96% abv.

A common exam trap: column stills do not only make neutral spirits. Bourbon and Armagnac are mostly column-distilled, yet both are full of character because they are collected at low strength.

Stage 4: what happens after distillation

Every new spirit is colourless and dry. Post-distillation decides the colour, aroma, sweetness and strength of the final product. The main operations, in their usual order, are maturation or storage, adding other flavours, blending and finishing.

Oak vs inert vessels

Oak barrels change a spirit in four ways. They add colour, which moves from lemon to gold, amber and, in rare cases, brown. They add aromas such as vanilla, coconut, cinnamon and clove. Over time, oxygen slowly enters the barrel and creates more complex notes like leather, dried fruit and mushroom, often called rancio. Finally, evaporation of water and alcohol (the angels' share) concentrates what remains, while the charred or toasted inner surface removes some harshness.

Three choices shape how much the oak gives:

  • Barrel age: a new barrel gives the most colour and flavour; each refill gives less.
  • Previous contents: barrels that held Bourbon, Sherry or other wines release some of that liquid back into the spirit.
  • Warehouse temperature: heat speeds up exchange with the wood, which is why spirits mature faster in a hot climate.

Inert vessels, usually stainless steel or glass, add no colour and no flavour. Most unaged spirits rest in them only briefly, so they stay close to the new spirit. Longer storage can make a spirit smoother and rounder while it stays colourless. Very old Cognacs are sometimes moved into glass demijohns to stop further change.

Blending, colour, sugar and dilution

Blending has two aims: consistency from one batch to the next, and complexity that no single barrel or spirit could give alone. The wider the range of spirits a blender can use (pot and column, young and old, different casks), the easier both aims become.

Finishing prepares the spirit for the bottle:

  • Colour: tiny amounts of caramel colour correct small differences between batches. Caramel adds colour, not sweetness.
  • Sugar: any sweetness you taste was added after distillation. It is optional for some spirits and required for liqueurs.
  • Filtration: chill-filtration stops haze when a spirit gets cold; activated charcoal can strip colour and flavour, as in some white rums and vodkas.
  • Dilution: pure water brings the spirit down to bottling strength. Legal minimums are usually 37.5% abv in the EU and 40% abv in the USA.

Common mistakes and study tips

  • Confusing sweet aromas with sweetness: vanilla in a Bourbon is an aroma; the spirit is dry unless sugar was added.
  • Thinking colour proves age: caramel can colour an unaged spirit, and charcoal can remove colour from an aged one.
  • Mixing up strengths: about 10% after fermentation, 25-30% for low wines, about 70-75% for pot still hearts, 95-96% for neutral spirit.
  • Forgetting that distillation cannot create flavour, it can only select what fermentation produced.

The best way to fix this topic is to draw both stills from memory, label each part and trace the vapour and liquid. Then explain the diagram aloud in two minutes. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a pot still and a column still?

A pot still works in batches: it is filled, run and emptied, and most spirits need two runs. It separates flavours roughly, so the spirit is lower in strength and richer in aroma. A column still runs continuously, with many distillations happening on its plates at the same time, and can reach 95-96% abv, the strength needed for neutral spirit.

Why are most pot still spirits distilled twice?

One pot still run cannot lift a fermented liquid of about 10% abv to spirit strength. The first run removes water and gives low wines of about 25-30% abv. The second run concentrates the alcohol further and lets the distiller separate heads, hearts and tails, keeping only the part with the right aromas, usually around 70-75% abv.

What are heads, hearts and tails?

They are the three parts of a distillation run. Heads come first and contain very volatile compounds with a solvent smell. Hearts are the clean middle part that becomes the spirit. Tails come last and carry heavy, oily or cheesy notes. In a pot still the distiller makes cuts between them; in a column still they are drawn from different heights.

Does a column still always make vodka-like spirits?

No. The style depends on the strength at which the spirit is drawn off. A short column or a low draw-off point gives a characterful spirit: Bourbon, Armagnac and rhum agricole are all column-distilled. Only tall columns with many plates, run to 95-96% abv, produce a truly neutral spirit for vodka or gin.

Where does the colour of a spirit come from?

Every new spirit is colourless. Colour comes from oak maturation, which moves a spirit through lemon, gold and amber over time, or from caramel colour added before bottling. Flavoured spirits and liqueurs can also be coloured with plant extracts or artificial colours. Activated charcoal filtration can remove colour, which is how some aged rums end up crystal clear.

Why are spirits diluted before bottling?

Straight from the still or cask, spirits are far too strong to enjoy: high alcohol numbs the palate and hides aromas. Distillers add pure water to bring them to bottling strength, most often around 40% abv. The water must be extremely clean, because any off-flavour would show, especially in vodka. Cask strength bottlings are the undiluted exception.

Is oak ageing always better than unaged?

No. Oak adds colour, vanilla and spice, and with time more complex notes, but it can also hide the character of the raw material. Spirits such as gin, most vodkas, Blanco Tequila, grappa and pisco are kept in inert vessels precisely to keep their pure fruit, botanical or agave aromas. Ageing is a style choice, not a quality guarantee.
