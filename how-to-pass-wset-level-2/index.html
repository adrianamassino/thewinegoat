How to pass WSET Level 2 first time

In short

To pass WSET Level 2 you need 55% in a 50-question multiple-choice exam. Study a little every day for about three weeks: learn the principal grapes with their regions and styles, understand the cause and effect of climate and winemaking, and finish with timed practice tests.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

Level 2 is very passable with steady preparation. The exam rewards understanding why wines taste as they do, not memorising lists.

The exam in brief

  • 50 multiple-choice questions in one hour.
  • Pass mark 55%; higher scores give Merit or Distinction.
  • No tasting exam and no written answers.

Common traps

  • Options that pair a real grape with the wrong region.
  • Confusing cool-climate and warm-climate styles of the same grape.
  • Label terms (Riserva, Reserva, Spätlese, Crianza) mixed up between countries.
  • Reading too fast: underline the key word (most, least, NOT).

Study notes: how to pass WSET Level 2 by understanding, not memorising

The fastest way to pass WSET Level 2 is to learn a small number of cause-and-effect rules and then attach the principal grapes and regions to them. The exam is 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes with a 55% pass mark, so you do not need to know everything: you need to recognise the right answer quickly and avoid the distractors that look almost right.

What the WSET Level 2 exam actually tests

WSET Level 2 questions fall into a few predictable families: how the vineyard and the winery shape a wine, which grapes grow where and what they taste like, how sparkling, sweet and fortified wines are made, how to read a label, and how food changes the way a wine tastes. Almost every question asks you to connect two of these things, for example a grape with a region, a climate with a style, or a technique with a flavour. That is why isolated lists are a weak way to revise: the exam rewards links.

The cause-and-effect core of WSET Level 2

Climate is the single most useful idea in WSET Level 2 because it explains most differences in style. A cool climate slows ripening, so grapes keep more acidity and develop less sugar; the wine is lighter in body, lower in alcohol and shows green or citrus fruit. A warm climate does the opposite: riper, sweeter grapes give wines with more alcohol, fuller body, lower acidity and riper fruit such as tropical fruit in whites or jammy black fruit in reds.

Winemaking choices that change the style

  • Oak: new oak adds vanilla, toast, smoke and sweet spice; old oak adds little flavour but lets a small amount of oxygen soften the wine.
  • Malolactic conversion: turns sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid and can add buttery flavours, typical of many oaked Chardonnays.
  • Lees contact: ageing on dead yeast adds body and bready, biscuity notes.
  • Temperature of fermentation: cool fermentation keeps fresh, fruity aromas in whites; warmer fermentation helps extract colour and tannin in reds.
  • Skin contact: red and rosé wines get their colour and tannin from the skins; white wines are usually pressed before fermentation.

A good habit is to say every rule as a sentence with because: this Chablis tastes of green apple and has high acidity because Chablis is a cool region. If you can produce that sentence, you can answer almost any question that combines climate, grape and style.

Principal grapes and regions for WSET Level 2

The WSET Level 2 grapes are best learned as triangles: grape, region, style. Start with the eight best-known international varieties, because they appear in many regions and generate the most questions. For each one, learn one cool-climate example, one warm-climate example and the key structural features.

Regional grapes that often appear in options

Beyond the big eight, keep a short list of grapes that are strongly linked to one place: Tempranillo for Rioja and Ribera del Duero, Sangiovese for Chianti, Nebbiolo for Barolo and Barbaresco, Chenin Blanc for Vouvray and South Africa, Malbec for Argentina, Gamay for Beaujolais, Albariño for Rías Baixas and Glera for Prosecco. Distractors often swap these pairings, so the best defence is to know each pair cold.

Label terms by country

  • France: AOC/AOP is the top category, IGP is a broader regional category with more freedom on grapes and yields.
  • Italy: DOCG sits above DOC; Riserva means a longer minimum ageing period than the standard wine of the same name.
  • Spain: Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva indicate increasing minimum ageing, in barrel and in bottle.
  • Germany: within Prädikatswein, Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein are based on the ripeness of the grapes at harvest; they do not tell you directly how sweet the finished wine is.
  • New World labels usually show the grape variety, while European labels more often show the place.

Sparkling, sweet and fortified wines: the quick wins

These topics are worth a lot of marks for little effort because each wine is defined by one method. Champagne and Cava use the traditional method: the second fermentation happens in the bottle and ageing on the lees gives toast, biscuit and brioche aromas. Prosecco, made mostly from Glera, uses the tank method, which keeps fresh pear and apple fruit. Asti is a sweet, low-alcohol sparkling Moscato also made in tanks.

Sweet wines are made by concentrating sugar or by stopping fermentation. Noble rot (Botrytis cinerea) concentrates sugar and acidity in Sauternes and Tokaji; freezing the grapes on the vine produces Eiswein and icewine; adding spirit during fermentation keeps natural sugar in Port. Sherry is different: it is fortified after fermentation, so the base wine is dry. Fino ages under a layer of yeast called flor and stays pale and fresh, while Oloroso ages in contact with oxygen and becomes brown and nutty.

Multiple-choice technique for WSET Level 2

  1. Read the question stem twice and underline the key word: most, least, NOT, best, usually.
  2. Cover the options and answer in your head first; then look for the option that matches.
  3. Eliminate options that are true statements but do not answer the question asked.
  4. Watch for a real grape paired with a wrong region, or a real region paired with the wrong style.
  5. Never leave a blank: there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so always choose the most likely option.
  6. Keep about ten minutes at the end to check flagged questions, but change an answer only if you find a clear reason.

The most common mistake at WSET Level 2 is mixing up climate effects for the same grape. When two options describe the same variety, ask yourself which one is the cool-climate version (more acid, lighter, greener fruit) and which one is the warm-climate version (riper, fuller, more alcohol).

How to revise WSET Level 2 in 15 minutes a day

Short daily sessions beat long weekend sessions because memory is built by retrieval, not by rereading. Fifteen focused minutes a day over three weeks gives you more than five hours of active practice, which is plenty for most candidates on top of the course.

  1. Minutes 1 to 3: recall yesterday's topic without notes and say the main cause-and-effect rule aloud.
  2. Minutes 4 to 10: answer 10 to 15 multiple-choice questions on today's topic and read every explanation, including for the questions you got right.
  3. Minutes 11 to 13: write one grape-region-style triangle on a card from memory.
  4. Minutes 14 to 15: note your two weakest points; they become tomorrow's starting point.

From week three, take a full 50-question test against the clock every two days. Aim for a steady 70% or more in practice: that margin protects you against nerves on the day.

Exam week checklist

  • Know the eight principal grapes with one cool and one warm example each.
  • Be able to explain oak, malolactic conversion and lees in one sentence each.
  • Know which sparkling wines use the traditional method and which use the tank method.
  • Know how noble rot, frozen grapes and fortification each produce a sweet wine.
  • Revise the label terms of France, Italy, Spain and Germany side by side.
  • Remember the food rules: sweetness and umami in food make wine taste harder; salt and acidity in food make it taste softer.

What the pass, merit and distinction marks mean in practice

With 50 questions, each question is worth 2% of the exam. Turning the percentages into a number of correct answers makes your target concrete and helps you read your practice scores.

ResultPercentageCorrect answers out of 50Margin for errors
Pass55%About 28Up to about 22 wrong answers
Merit70%35Up to 15 wrong answers
Distinction85%About 43Up to about 7 wrong answers

The table shows why Level 2 is very passable: you can miss almost half the paper and still pass. It also shows why a distinction needs a different kind of preparation, because seven slips are easy to make through misreading rather than ignorance. If you are aiming for a distinction, put as much effort into reading technique as into new facts.

Two short case studies

Case 1: a candidate reread the course book three times and scored 52% in her first mock. She recognised every term but could not choose between two plausible options. She switched to daily mixed questions and wrote a because sentence for every mistake; two weeks later she was scoring around 75%. The lesson: rereading feels productive, but answering questions builds the recall the exam measures.

Case 2: a sommelier with years of service experience scored 64% on his first timed test, losing marks on sparkling methods, German label terms and why questions. He knew the wines but not the mechanisms. A week of focused work on cause and effect and on the labelling table moved him into merit territory. The lesson: experience helps, but the exam rewards explanations, not just familiarity.

Practice questions with the reasoning explained

Question 1: a wine is described as pale lemon, light-bodied, high in acidity, with green apple and citrus aromas and no oak. Is it more likely from a cool or a warm climate? Answer: cool. Each clue points the same way: high acidity and light body come from slow ripening with modest sugar, and green apple and citrus are cool-climate fruit.

Question 2: which technique makes a wine taste of toast and brioche: tank method or traditional method? Answer: the traditional method, because the second fermentation happens in the bottle and the wine then ages on the dead yeast, which gives these autolytic aromas. The tank method keeps fresh fruit instead.

Question 3: why does a sweet wine taste less sweet with a dessert that is sweeter than the wine? Answer: sweetness in food makes the wine taste drier, more acidic and more bitter. That is why the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert.

Mistakes candidates make while preparing

  • Studying only the topics they enjoy, often red wines, and neglecting sparkling, sweet and fortified wines.
  • Learning lists of grapes without the region and style that make each one recognisable in an option.
  • Never practising under time pressure, then rushing the last ten questions on the day.
  • Checking only whether an answer was right, without reading the explanation.
  • Stopping revision once practice scores reach 55%, which leaves no margin for nerves.

The last 48 hours before the exam

  1. Two days before: one last full 50-question test in 60 minutes, then review every wrong answer.
  2. Make one page of your weakest facts: label terms, sparkling methods, the grapes you mix up.
  3. The day before: only light revision of that page and a few mixed questions; no new topics.
  4. Prepare the documents and directions your school asked for, and sleep well.
  5. On the day: read each stem twice, answer every question and use spare time to check the ones you flagged.

If you continue to WSET Level 3 afterwards, the structure changes: there are two separate units, a theory unit with multiple-choice questions and short written answers, and a tasting unit with two wines tasted blind, each needing 55% and each retaken on its own if failed. Level 2 is the right place to build the cause-and-effect habit that Level 3 written answers rely on.

Frequently asked questions

How hard is WSET Level 2?

It is a moderate step: most candidates who follow the course and practise regularly pass.

How long should I study for WSET Level 2?

Around three weeks of short daily sessions on top of the course is a comfortable plan for most people.

Can I take WSET Level 2 without Level 1?

Yes: Level 1 is not required for Level 2.

What score do I need to pass WSET Level 2?

You need 55% in the 50-question multiple-choice exam, which means at least 28 correct answers. Higher scores lead to a Merit or a Distinction. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so you should answer every question. In practice tests, aiming for around 70% gives you a comfortable safety margin for exam-day nerves.

Is there a tasting exam at WSET Level 2?

No. WSET Level 2 has no tasting exam and no written answers: the assessment is only the multiple-choice paper. You still taste wines during the course, and it is worth doing so carefully, because describing acidity, tannin, body and fruit helps you remember why each grape and region tastes as it does, which is exactly what the questions test.

Which topics carry the most questions in WSET Level 2?

Most questions link the principal grapes to their regions and styles, so grape-region-style knowledge is where most marks are. Climate and winemaking effects come next, followed by sparkling, sweet and fortified wines, label terms and food pairing. Revise the principal grapes first, then the quick-win topics such as sparkling methods and sweet wine production.

How many grapes do I need to know for WSET Level 2?

Focus first on the eight principal international varieties, such as Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Pinot Noir, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Grenache. Then add the regional grapes strongly tied to one place, like Tempranillo, Sangiovese, Nebbiolo, Chenin Blanc, Malbec and Glera. Learn each one with its regions and typical style rather than as a bare name.

Can I pass WSET Level 2 with online study only?

Yes, many candidates pass after an online course, provided they practise regularly. The keys are the same as in a classroom: a daily routine, many practice questions with explanations, and timed mock tests in the final week. Tasting a few classic wines at home, even with friends, makes the grape and region links much easier to remember.

How many questions must I answer correctly to pass WSET Level 2?

The pass mark is 55%, which corresponds to roughly 28 correct answers out of 50. A merit needs 70%, or 35 correct answers, and a distinction needs 85%, about 43 correct answers. Aim for at least 70% in timed practice so that you have a margin on the day.

How do I get a distinction in WSET Level 2?

A distinction leaves room for only about seven mistakes, so accuracy matters as much as knowledge. Learn the full syllabus evenly, including sparkling, sweet and fortified wines and label terms, and take several timed mocks. Most lost marks at this level come from misreading the stem, so underline key words and check flagged questions before the end.

Is it better to reread the course book or do practice questions?

Practice questions. Rereading creates a feeling of familiarity, but the exam asks you to retrieve facts and choose between similar options. Answering questions, reading the explanations and correcting your mistakes builds exactly that skill. Use the book to understand a topic the first time, then switch to active recall.

What happens if I fail WSET Level 2?

You can sit the exam again. Ask your course provider about the arrangements and dates. Before retaking it, analyse your practice results by topic, since most candidates who fail lose marks in a few specific areas, such as label terms or sparkling and fortified methods, rather than across the whole syllabus.

Does WSET Level 2 prepare me for Level 3?

Yes. Level 2 introduces the cause-and-effect logic of climate, grape and winemaking that Level 3 develops in more depth. Level 3 adds short written answers and a separate tasting unit, each unit needing 55%. Building good habits at Level 2, such as explaining why a wine has its style, makes the step to Level 3 much easier.
