A guide for wine schools: better WSET exam results through structured revision

Wine schools improve WSET exam results most when students revise actively between lessons, not only in the last week. Classroom time is precious and is best spent on teaching, tasting and discussion; what usually decides a pass, a Merit or a resit is what students do on their own in the weeks between the first lesson and the exam. This guide explains where students tend to lose marks, how to build revision into your course calendar and how to measure whether it works.

Where WSET students usually lose marks

  • Short written answers: students know the facts but write vague or incomplete answers that miss the expected key points.
  • Revision left too late: material from the first weeks of the course is forgotten by exam day.
  • Uneven foundations: some students start Level 3 with weak Level 2 knowledge and fall behind quickly.
  • Tasting calibration: structural levels such as acidity, tannin and body are placed one level too high or too low.
  • Exam technique: misreading multiple-choice questions or running out of time on the theory paper.

Why revision between lessons matters

Revision between lessons matters because memory is strengthened by retrieval: answering a question about Bordeaux two days after the lesson, again a week later and again before the exam fixes the knowledge far better than rereading the notes once. Spaced practice also reveals misunderstandings early, while there is still time to ask the teacher, instead of in the final mock exam.

The challenge for a school is that this practice happens outside the classroom, where teachers cannot see it. A structured revision tool makes it visible: students get a daily plan based on their exam date and course days, and the school can follow activation and progress without adding marking hours.

How to build revision into your WSET course calendar

  1. Before the first lesson, share the class code or link so that students set their exam date, goal and available time.
  2. After each lesson, point students to the topics just covered so that their daily practice reinforces your teaching.
  3. Every week, set one short written answer to practise in the app, and discuss the most frequently missed key points at the start of the next lesson.
  4. Halfway through the course, ask students to take a timed mock exam and use the results by topic to adjust the second half.
  5. In the last two weeks, encourage full mock exams in exam conditions and focused work on each student's weak topics.
  6. After results day, compare outcomes with previous sessions and decide what to change for the next course.

Teachers who get the best engagement mention practice in every lesson, even for one minute: a quick look at the class's most missed question of the week shows students that their revision is noticed and turns the app into part of the course rather than an optional extra.

Training short written answers at scale

Short written answers are the hardest part of the WSET Level 3 theory exam to train, because every practice answer needs feedback, and marking dozens of scripts each week is not realistic for most teachers. When students write answers that are marked point by point against key points, they can practise every day and see exactly which points they missed. Teachers can then use class time for what software cannot do: discussing reasoning, nuance and the cause-and-effect links that make a strong answer.

  • Students practise more often, so the command words describe, explain and compare become familiar.
  • Teachers see recurring gaps without reading every script.
  • Model answers show students what a precise, well-linked answer looks like.
  • Class discussion moves from what the right answer is to why it is right.

A 15-minute weekly routine for teachers

Running a revision programme should not add hours to a teacher's week. Fifteen minutes is enough to keep a class on track.

  1. Minutes 1 to 5: check activation and practice levels for the class.
  2. Minutes 6 to 10: identify the two topics with the weakest results.
  3. Minutes 11 to 13: pick one question or written answer to discuss at the start of the next lesson.
  4. Minutes 14 to 15: send a short message to the class with the focus of the coming week.

Start with one class rather than the whole school. A pilot with a single group gives you clear before-and-after evidence and real feedback from students before you decide on licences or a white-label app.

Checklist before launching a pilot

  • Choose a class with an exam date known in advance.
  • Share the course calendar so that the study plan can follow it.
  • Prepare a short slide or email introducing the app to students.
  • Agree on the indicators you will look at during and after the session.
  • Check that recommending a third-party revision tool fits your own agreements as a course provider.

Preparing students for both Level 3 units

The WSET Level 3 exam has two separate units, theory and tasting, and students need at least 55% in each. A strong theory paper does not make up for a weak tasting, or the other way round, and a student who fails one unit retakes only that unit. For a school, this means that pass rates depend on the weaker unit of each student, so revision has to cover both, not only the part that is easiest to practise at home.

UnitTypical weaknessWhat helps in classWhat helps between lessons
Theory, multiple choiceReading too fast, choosing the first plausible optionDiscuss one tricky question per lesson and why each distractor is wrongShort daily question sets on the topics just taught
Theory, short written answersLists of keywords without cause and effectShow a weak and a strong answer to the same question side by sideOne written answer a week, marked against key points
TastingStructural levels one step too high or too low; conclusions not justifiedCalibration flights and group discussion of levels before conclusionsFull structured notes on wines tasted at home, compared with class notes

Schools that track the two units separately in mock exams can see early which students are at risk in which unit, and adapt the last weeks of the course instead of discovering the problem on results day.

Using written-answer results in class: a worked example

Imagine a class that practises a written question on why Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is aromatic and high in acidity. Most students mention the cool maritime climate and stainless steel fermentation, but very few explain the role of cool nights in preserving aromatic compounds, and almost nobody mentions early bottling. The teacher does not need to read every answer to see this: the most frequently missed key points stand out at class level.

At the next lesson, the teacher spends five minutes on those two missing links, shows how a single sentence turns each into a scoring point, and asks students to rewrite one paragraph. The discussion is short, specific and directly tied to the exam, and students see that their practice between lessons shapes what happens in class.

Supporting students who need a resit

Even in well-prepared classes, some students will need to retake a unit. Because the units are separate, a resit plan can be focused and short, which makes it easier for students to come back with confidence.

  1. Identify the unit to retake and, within it, the weakest topics or skills from mock results.
  2. Agree a realistic resit date with the student and set it in their study plan.
  3. For theory, combine daily questions on weak topics with one written answer a week.
  4. For tasting, organise short calibration sessions and compare notes against a reference note.
  5. Run a final mock of the unit two weeks before the resit and review it individually.

Level 1 and Level 2 classes

Revision between lessons matters at every level, not only at Level 3. Level 1 and Level 2 exams are made entirely of multiple-choice questions, so regular short question sessions are the most direct preparation, and they also build the foundations that later Level 3 students will need.

  • Level 1 courses are often short: a few days of practice questions after the course help students arrive at the exam with key terms fresh.
  • Level 2 students benefit from linking grapes to regions and styles, which question practice reinforces quickly.
  • Students who finish Level 2 with strong results are better prepared for the written answers and tasting of Level 3.

  • Is it available in the languages our students use?

Frequently asked questions

Is The Wine Goat affiliated with WSET?

No. The Wine Goat is independent and not endorsed by WSET®. Course providers should check that recommending a third-party revision tool fits their own agreement.

Does it replace our course?

No. It is a revision and practice tool between lessons and before the exam. Your teaching, tastings and materials stay at the centre.

Can we try it with one class?

Yes. We offer a free pilot for one class so you can see engagement and results before deciding.

Which levels are covered?

Levels 1, 2 and 3 are all in the app today. The Diploma will follow.

How are students’ data handled?

Accounts are personal and data are stored in the EU-compliant infrastructure we use; we only share aggregated results with the school, with the students’ consent.

How can a wine school improve WSET Level 3 pass rates?

The biggest gains usually come from regular revision between lessons and from training the short written answers, which are where many candidates lose marks. Give students a daily plan linked to the course calendar, set one written answer per week, run a timed mock halfway through the course and use topic results to adjust teaching. Measure outcomes against previous sessions to see what works.

How much teacher time does a revision app require?

Very little once it is set up. Students follow their own study plan and practise written answers marked against key points, so teachers do not mark scripts. About fifteen minutes a week is enough to check activation and weak topics and choose one question to discuss in class. The aim is to save teacher time, not add to it.

Can students revise in a different language from the course?

Yes. The app is available in English, Italian, French and Spanish, so students can consolidate difficult concepts in the language they are most comfortable with and then return to the vocabulary of the course. This is especially useful in international classes. Questions about the language of the exam itself should be handled through the school's usual procedures.

Can a school add its own questions to the app?

Yes, with the white-label offer: the school gets its own branded app, with its logo, colours and domain, and can add its own questions and course calendar next to The Wine Goat content. The same engine can also run courses outside wine, with your own exam format, model answers and marking points.

How do we know whether a pilot class worked?

Agree on a few indicators before starting: how many students activated their account, how regularly they practised, their results by topic in mock exams and their progress in written answers. After the exam, compare pass, Merit and Distinction rates with a similar previous session. Results are shared with the school in aggregated form, with students' consent.

How can a school help students pass both Level 3 units?

Track theory and tasting separately in mock exams, because students need at least 55% in each unit and a strong result in one does not compensate for the other. Give daily question practice and weekly written answers for theory, and regular calibration flights and home tasting notes for the tasting unit. Adapt the last weeks of the course to each student's weaker unit.

Is The Wine Goat useful for Level 1 and Level 2 classes?

Yes. Levels 1, 2 and 3 are all in the app today. Level 1 and Level 2 exams are multiple choice, so short regular question sessions are a very direct preparation. Using the app at these levels also builds the habits and foundations students need if they continue to Level 3.

Does The Wine Goat cover the WSET Diploma?

Not yet. Diploma (Level 4) content is in preparation and is not available today. Schools that teach the Diploma can use the Level 3 material to help students refresh their foundations before starting, and can share their needs with us during a demo so that future Diploma content reflects how they teach.

How can we support students who must retake a unit?

Because Level 3 units are assessed separately, a student retakes only the unit they failed. Build a short, focused plan around that unit: identify weak topics or tasting skills from mock results, set a realistic resit date, combine daily practice with weekly written answers or calibration tastings, and run a final mock of the unit two weeks before the resit.

What should we look for in a revision tool for our students?

Check that it follows your course calendar, trains written answers as well as multiple choice, supports structured tasting notes, shows aggregated progress without extra marking, works in your students' languages and handles data responsibly. Starting with a single pilot class is the simplest way to see whether it fits your teaching.
