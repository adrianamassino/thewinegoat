## A · Cool vs warm climate: same grape, two wines
Files: `posts/a-cool-vs-warm-climate/` (6 slides)

**Caption**
Same grape. Two completely different wines. 🍇
Climate decides how much sugar and acidity a grape keeps.
Cooler: less sugar, more acidity, so lighter and fresher wines.
Warmer: more sugar, less acidity and riper flavours.
Chardonnay: green apple, lemon and wet stones when cool; peach, pineapple and banana when warm.
Sauvignon Blanc: gooseberry and grapefruit when less ripe; peach and passion fruit when riper.
Swipe for the cheat sheet and the two questions to ask about any glass.

Which style do you reach for: cool or warm? 👇

## C · Pot still vs column still in 5 slides
Files: `posts/c-pot-vs-column-still/` (5 slides)

**Caption**
Pot still or column still? The spirits classic, explained in 5 slides. 🥃
Pot still: works in batches, usually two runs (~10% → ~25% → ~75% abv), aromatic spirits full of character.
Column still: runs non-stop, a small distillation on every plate, up to 96% abv.
The golden rule: the higher the strength, the less aroma survives.
Myth buster: Armagnac, Bourbon and rhum agricole are made in column stills and still have lots of character.

Did the "column still ≠ neutral" part surprise you? 👇
Practise free on thewinegoat.com

#wset #wsetspirits #spirits #spiritsstudent #distillation #potstill #columnstill #whisky #bourbon #vodka #bartender #spiritseducation #winestudent

**Alt text**
Five-slide carousel. 1: "Pot still vs column still", illustrated goat with a magnifying glass. 2: The pot still: pot, condenser and linking pipe; batch process; two runs from about 10% to 25% to 75% abv; medium to pronounced aromas; never neutral. 3: The column still: many plates, simultaneous distillations, strongest at the top, continuous, up to 96% abv. 4: Strength ladder: about 75% abv medium to pronounced (Armagnac, Bourbon, rhum agricole), about 90% light (light rums, Scottish grain whisky), 96% neutral (base of vodka). 5: Quiz "Which still can reach 96% abv?", answer: only a column still.

## D · How to read a German wine label
Files: `posts/d-german-wine-label/` (6 slides)

**Caption**
Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese… German labels look scary, but they follow a logic. 🇩🇪
Most German terms tell you how ripe the grapes were at harvest.
Step 1: the category. Landwein (PGI) → Qualitätswein → Prädikatswein (both PDO).
Step 2: the six Prädikat levels, ranked by minimum grape sugar.
Step 3: dry or sweet? Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese can be either. BA, TBA and Eiswein are always sweet.
Bonus words: Trocken = dry, Halbtrocken = some sweetness.

Which German term trips you up the most? 👇
Practise free on thewinegoat.com

#wset #wset2 #wset3 #winestudent #germanwine #riesling #mosel #kabinett #spatlese #auslese #eiswein #sommelier #winelover #wineeducation

**Alt text**
Six-slide carousel. 1: "How to read a German wine label", goat reading among books. 2: Categories: Landwein (PGI), Qualitätswein (PDO, from one of 13 areas such as Mosel or Rheingau), Prädikatswein (PDO, higher minimum sugar, single area). 3: The six Prädikat levels in order: Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Eiswein (same minimum sugar as BA), Trockenbeerenauslese. 4: Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese can be dry or sweet (if dry, riper means more alcohol) vs BA, TBA and Eiswein (always sweet, low alcohol; noble rot for BA and TBA, frozen grapes for Eiswein). Trocken = dry, Halbtrocken = some sweetness. 5: Quiz: a dry Auslese is stronger than a dry Kabinett, plus recap. 6: Question to followers.

## E · Serving temperatures cheat sheet
Files: `posts/e-serving-temperatures/` (6 slides)

**Caption**
Save this before your next dinner party. 🌡️
Sweet wines: 6–8 °C (43–46 °F)
Sparkling: 6–10 °C (43–50 °F)
Light and medium whites, rosés: 7–10 °C (45–50 °F)
Full-bodied whites: 10–13 °C (50–55 °F)
Light reds: about 13 °C (55 °F), lightly chilled
Medium and full reds: 15–18 °C (59–64 °F)
Plus 3 service habits: ice AND water in the bucket, no radiators, and the guest always decides.

Do you chill your light reds? Yes or no 👇

## F · 5 tasting mistakes students make
Files: `posts/f-tasting-mistakes/` (5 slides)

**Caption**
5 tasting mistakes we see all the time, and how to fix them. 🍷
1. Tasting after toothpaste or perfume
2. Pouring a full glass (about 5 cL is enough)
3. Judging acidity by sourness instead of how much your mouth waters
4. Counting bitterness as part of the finish
5. Thinking "more" always means better quality
Quality comes down to four criteria: balance, length, intensity, complexity.

## Reel 2 · Neutral spirit: 96% in 20 seconds
Files: `reels/reel-2-neutral-spirit-96.mp4` (24 s, silent track) + `reels/reel-2-neutral-spirit-96_cover.png`

**Caption**
Why does vodka smell of almost nothing? It's all about 96%. 🧪
Fermentation gives a liquid of about 8–10% abv.
Distillation concentrates it: ethanol boils before water.
The higher the strength, the fewer aromas survive: at 95–96% abv a spirit is neutral.
Only a column still can get there. A pot still never makes a neutral spirit.
Vodka is neutral spirit diluted with pure water, usually to 40% abv.

Did you know vodka starts as neutral spirit? 👇
