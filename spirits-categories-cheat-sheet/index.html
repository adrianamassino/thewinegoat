Types of Spirits: The Complete Cheat Sheet

In short

Spirits are grouped by raw material: fruit (brandy), grain (whisky and most vodka), sugar cane (rum and cachaça), agave (Tequila and Mezcal), and flavoured spirits built on a base spirit (gin, liqueurs and more). For each category, learn four things: the raw material, the key legal rules, the main label terms and the typical flavours.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

Hundreds of bottles on a back bar fit into a handful of families. Once you know what each family is made from and which rules shape it, a new label stops being a mystery: you can predict how the spirit was made and roughly how it will taste.

This cheat sheet summarises the categories covered in the spirits courses, with the exam-relevant numbers and the comparisons that come up most often, such as Cognac vs Armagnac and Tequila vs Mezcal.

Brandy and fruit spirits

Brandy is a spirit distilled from fruit, usually grapes. The best known come from France, Italy and South America.

Cognac comes from a defined area just north of Bordeaux, mostly from Ugni Blanc grapes. The wine must be double distilled in copper pot stills with direct flame heating, and the spirit must spend at least two years in oak. Nearly all Cognacs are blends, often lightly sweetened and colour-corrected. Age terms guarantee the youngest spirit in the blend: VS at least 2 years, VSOP at least 4 years, XO at least 10 years. Grande Champagne, Petite Champagne and Borderies are the most prized sub-regions; Fine Champagne is a blend of the first two with at least half Grande Champagne.

Armagnac comes from south of Bordeaux. Ugni Blanc is widespread, but Baco (prune notes) and Folle Blanche (floral) play a bigger role than in Cognac. Most producers use a short continuous column still, which gives a characterful, low-strength spirit. Look out for vintage-dated Armagnacs and Blanche Armagnac, an unaged style with no Cognac equivalent. Bas Armagnac and Ténarèze are the key sub-regions.

  • Calvados: apple (and some pear) brandy from Normandy, pot or column distilled, aged in oak, often in large casks, so even old examples keep a clear apple aroma.
  • Grappa: Italy's pomace brandy, made from the grape skins left after winemaking. Usually unaged, herbaceous and sometimes sharp; the best show their grape variety, such as floral Moscato, or rest in inert vessels to soften.
  • Pisco: grape brandy from Chile and Peru, made from aromatic varieties, especially the Muscat family. Mostly unaged (always in Peru), with pure, pronounced floral and fruity aromas.

Whisky and whiskey

Whisky is a grain spirit aged in oak. The spelling is usually 'whisky' in Scotland, Canada and Japan and 'whiskey' in Ireland and the USA.

Label terms worth learning: an age statement gives the age of the youngest whisky in the bottle. 'Single' means one distillery, not one cask. 'Cask strength' means undiluted. 'Non-chill-filtered' means the haze-removing filtration was skipped. In the USA, 'Straight' means at least two years in new oak with nothing added for colour or flavour, and 'proof' is double the abv. Scotland's whisky regions are Islay (often peaty), Highland, Speyside, Lowland and Campbeltown.

Rum and cachaça

Rum is made from sugar cane, either fresh cane juice (grassy, vegetal) or molasses, the sticky leftover from sugar making (caramel, brown sugar). There are very few legal rules, so think in terms of light marks, distilled to high strength in columns, and heavy marks, made in pot stills or short columns. Most rums are blends of both.

Colour words (white, golden, dark) are only a rough guide: white rums may be aged and then charcoal-filtered, and many dark rums owe their colour to caramel. Age statements on rum are not standardised. 'Overproof' refers to an old British system and means a rum stronger than about 57% abv. Cachaça is Brazil's cane juice spirit, produced in huge volumes, mostly unaged and often slightly sweetened.

Tequila and Mezcal

Both are Mexican agave spirits. The agave heart (piña) stores inulin, which is cooked into sugar, crushed, fermented and distilled. Tequila must use blue agave from five named states, with Jalisco the most important. A bottle labelled simply 'Tequila' needs only 51% of its sugars from agave; '100% agave' means agave only and a fuller agave character.

Mezcal is always 100% agave. Artisanal Mezcal limits producers to pits or brick ovens, traditional fermentation vessels and directly heated pot stills; Ancestral Mezcal goes further, with pit roasting, hand crushing and clay pots.

Vodka

Vodka can be made from any raw material, but grains and potatoes are traditional. It is a neutral spirit (at least 95% abv in the USA, 96% in the EU) made in column stills, often charcoal-filtered, then diluted to about 40% abv. Styles range from neutral to characterful, where a hint of the raw material and a rounder texture come through. Poland, Russia, Sweden and Finland are the traditional homes.

Gin and other flavoured spirits

Gin is a flavoured spirit whose main flavour must be juniper. Common partners are coriander seed, citrus peel, angelica root and orris root. In the EU, gin is made from neutral spirit; in the USA other base spirits are allowed. The best gins get their flavour by re-distilling the spirit with the botanicals, which can be steeped in the spirit, hung in a basket in the vapour path, or distilled one by one and blended.

  • London Dry Gin: flavoured only by re-distillation, nothing but a trace of sugar added afterwards; can be made anywhere.
  • Distilled Gin: re-distilled, but in the EU extra flavourings may be added afterwards.
  • Old Tom: an older, sweetened style.
  • Flavoured vodka: traditional versions macerate herbs, honey or fruit; modern ones range from fruit to pepper.
  • Spiced rum: usually a sweetened golden rum with vanilla, cinnamon and other spices.
  • Aniseed spirits: pastis (sweetened, must contain liquorice) and absinthe (unsweetened, with wormwood); both turn cloudy with water.
  • Bitters: aperitif, digestif and cocktail styles, built on bitter botanicals such as gentian, quinine and bitter orange.

Liqueurs and vermouth

A liqueur is a flavoured spirit that must be sweetened. The base is usually neutral spirit, sometimes brandy, rum or whisky. Any flavour is allowed, and liqueurs are easiest to remember in groups: herb, fruit, seed and nut, and cream. Many are brightly coloured and some are bottled below normal spirit strength.

Vermouth is not a spirit but an aromatised wine: a mostly white base wine, flavoured with botanicals that must include wormwood, sweetened and fortified with alcohol to about 14.5-22% abv. Red vermouths usually get their colour from caramel, not red wine, and even dry vermouth contains some sugar. South-east France and north-west Italy are the classic regions.

How to learn the categories fast

  • Learn by raw material first, then by country, then by label term.
  • Make one card per category with four lines: raw material, still, ageing rule, label terms.
  • Taste in pairs when you can: Cognac next to Armagnac, Tequila next to Mezcal, Bourbon next to Scotch.
  • Test yourself on numbers daily; they are the easiest marks to lose.

Frequently asked questions

What are the main types of spirits?

The main families are brandy (from fruit), whisky (from grain), rum and cachaça (from sugar cane), Tequila and Mezcal (from agave) and vodka (from any raw material, distilled to neutral). On top of these sit flavoured spirits such as gin, aniseed spirits and bitters, and liqueurs, which are flavoured spirits with added sugar. Vermouth is a related aromatised wine.

What is the difference between Cognac and Armagnac?

Both are French grape brandies, but Cognac comes from north of Bordeaux and must be double distilled in pot stills, while Armagnac comes from further south and is mostly made in a short column still. Armagnac uses more Baco and Folle Blanche, is made mainly by small producers, and often appears as vintage or unaged Blanche, both rare or absent in Cognac.

What is the difference between Tequila and Mezcal?

Tequila must be made from blue agave, mainly in Jalisco, and the agave is usually steamed. Mezcal, mostly from Oaxaca, can use several agave species, especially espadín, and the hearts are often roasted in pits, giving a smoky aroma. Tequila is dominated by large producers, Mezcal by small ones, and Mezcal is always made from 100% agave.

What is the difference between whisky and whiskey?

Only the spelling and the country. Scotland, Canada and Japan generally write 'whisky', while Ireland and the USA usually write 'whiskey'. Both are spirits made from grain and aged in oak. The real differences in style come from the grains used, the type of still, the distillation strength and the barrels, which are set by each country's rules.

Is gin just flavoured vodka?

In a sense, yes: most gin starts from the same neutral spirit as vodka and gets its flavour from botanicals. What makes it gin is that juniper must be the dominant flavour. The best gins are flavoured by re-distillation with the botanicals, and terms like London Dry and Distilled Gin tell you how the flavour was added.

What does XO mean on a bottle?

On Cognac, XO guarantees that the youngest spirit in the blend has aged at least 10 years in oak. Armagnac uses similar terms. On other spirits, such as rum, XO has no legal definition and does not guarantee any minimum age, so always check which category the label belongs to before trusting an age term.

Is vermouth a spirit?

No. Vermouth is an aromatised wine: a base wine, usually white, flavoured with botanicals including wormwood, sweetened and strengthened with a small amount of alcohol to roughly 14.5-22% abv. It is studied alongside spirits because it is essential in many classic cocktails, including the Martini, Manhattan and Negroni, where it adds sweetness, bitterness and acidity.
