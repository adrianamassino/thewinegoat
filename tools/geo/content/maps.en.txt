## What are The Wine Goat's interactive wine maps?
They are a set of 42 clickable maps of the world's main wine countries and regions, with two ways to use them: an atlas to study each area and a game to test what you remember. Both are inside the app and work on phone, tablet and computer.
The maps go from country level down to sub-regions. France, for example, opens on a national map; from there you can go into Bordeaux, the Dordogne and South West France, Burgundy (and from Burgundy into the Côte d'Or, the Côte Chalonnaise and Mâconnais, and Beaujolais), Alsace, the Loire Valley, the Northern and Southern Rhône, Southern France and Champagne. Italy, Spain, Portugal, Germany, North America, South Africa and Australia have their own detailed maps in the same way.

## What can you find on each map?
Every map shows the wine areas of that country or region, plus the rivers, lakes, towns and mountain ranges that help you place them. Wine areas are the core; the other layers are there because exam answers often depend on them.
| Layer | Examples from the maps | Why it matters in the exam |
| Wine areas and appellations | Pauillac, Pomerol, Chablis, Barolo, Rías Baixas, Marlborough | They are what the exam names on labels and in questions |
| Rivers and lakes | Gironde, Dordogne, Garonne, Rhône, Douro, Mosel, Danube | Banks, valleys and water moderate climate and divide regions |
| Cities and towns | Bordeaux, Libourne, Reims, Alba, Haro, Mendoza | Reference points for "north of", "east of", "near" |
| Mountains and relief | Alps, Andes, Pyrenees, Massif Central, Coast Ranges | Altitude, rain shadows and shelter explain styles |
In the atlas, tapping a wine area opens an information card. For each area the card can show its classification level (for example a regional or a communal appellation), its main grapes, its soils and a short note on what makes it distinctive. For Côtes de Bourg, for instance, the card shows a communal or local AOC, Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon and Malbec as grapes, clay and limestone soils, and a note that its hills sit on the right bank of the Gironde facing the Médoc.

## How does the atlas work?
The atlas is the study mode: you explore freely, with no score and no timer, until the layout of a region makes sense.
1. Choose a country from the row at the top (France, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Germany, Austria, Hungary, Greece, North America, Chile, Argentina, South Africa, Australia, New Zealand).
2. Open a region with the buttons under the country name, or tap it on the map. A breadcrumb (for example France › Burgundy › Côte d'Or) shows where you are.
3. Tap any area on the map or in the legend below it. The map zooms on it and the information card appears.
4. Use "Whole map" to zoom back out, or open the next level down when a region has its own detailed map.
The relief of the land is shaded on the map, so you can see at a glance why vineyards cling to river valleys or to the lower slopes of a range.

## How does the map game work?
The map game turns the maps into short quizzes. You choose a map, a game mode, a difficulty level and the length of the session, then answer one place at a time with an instant correction.

### Five ways to play
| Mode | What you do | Best for |
| Find it | The app names a place, you tap it on the map | Building the mental picture of a region |
| What is it called? | The app highlights an area, you pick its name from 4 | Recognising areas you have already seen |
| Type the name | The app highlights an area, you type the name (accents are ignored) | The hardest recall, close to exam conditions |
| Grapes and appellations | Names are shown; you pick the grapes and the category of each area | Linking places to grapes and quality levels |
| Atlas | Study and search by name, no score | First contact with a new region |
### Three difficulty levels
- Basic: the main places, with borders shown.
- Advanced: adds extra appellations and crus, with answers that are close to each other.
- Expert: no borders at all; you place everything from memory.
### Sessions, categories and progress
You can play 10 or 20 places, or the whole map, and choose which categories to include: wines and appellations, rivers and lakes, cities, mountains and relief. Each map shows how many places it contains and your mastery as a percentage. Detailed maps unlock when you have mastered the map above them (by finding enough of its places or reaching a good best score), or you can unlock every level at once if you prefer to jump straight to a region. Places you miss come back more often, a day streak and a 28-day activity grid show your consistency, and the total of places mastered counts up to 1,053.
A speaker button can read a place name aloud, which helps with names you have only ever seen written, from Châteauneuf-du-Pape to Württemberg.

## How accurate are the maps?
Borders and rivers come from real geographic data, and the app says so on the map screen. River courses come from Natural Earth. In France and Italy, appellations are drawn by joining the real borders of the communes they include (French commune data from IGN, Italian data from ISTAT via openpolis), so their shapes follow the real territory. For other countries, appellations are drawn as schematic areas around their real position, sized to be easy to tap.
That difference matters for how you use them. For France and Italy you can trust shapes and neighbours closely. For the other countries, trust the position and the relationships (north of, along, between), which is what exam questions test, rather than the exact outline.

## Who benefits from interactive wine maps?
Anyone who has to place wine regions from memory benefits, but the maps are most useful from Level 2 upwards, where geography carries real weight.
- Level 2 candidates meet dozens of geographical indications across the main wine countries. The Basic level of the game, with borders shown, is enough to fix where each one is.
- Level 3 candidates need much finer geography: communes of the Médoc, the order of the Côte d'Or villages, the banks of the Rhône, the sub-regions of the Douro, the valleys of Chile. The Advanced and Expert levels are built for that.
- Visual learners, who remember a picture more easily than a list, get a way into a topic that suits them.
- Candidates studying in a second language can learn place names by position rather than by reading long sentences.
- Wine professionals and enthusiasts who simply want to read a wine list or a label with more confidence can use the atlas without any exam in mind.

## How to study wine geography with the maps
A short, regular routine works better than a long session the night before. Here is a method that fits into ten minutes a day.
1. Before a new chapter, open the region in the atlas and tap every area once. Read the card: grapes, soils, level.
2. Read the lesson of the chapter in the app. Each time a place is mentioned, you already know roughly where it is.
3. Play "Find it" on Basic for that map, 10 places. Repeat the next day.
4. When you score well, move to "What is it called?", then to "Type the name".
5. Once a week, play a whole country on Advanced to mix regions you studied on different days.
6. In the last two weeks before the exam, play Expert on the regions that carry most weight in your level.
! Say the relationship out loud when you find a place: "Pauillac, between Saint-Estèphe and Saint-Julien, on the Gironde". Exam questions test relationships much more often than exact outlines.

## Common wine-geography mistakes the maps help fix
Some confusions come up again and again in practice questions. Each of them is easy to fix with a map in front of you.
| Common mistake | What the map shows |
| Mixing up the Left and Right Bank of Bordeaux | The Médoc and Graves lie west and south of the Gironde and Garonne; Saint-Émilion and Pomerol are on the right bank of the Dordogne; Entre-Deux-Mers sits between the two rivers |
| Getting the order of the Médoc communes wrong | From north to south: Saint-Estèphe, Pauillac, Saint-Julien, Margaux |
| Placing Chablis next to the Côte d'Or | Chablis is far to the north-west, on the Serein, closer to Champagne than to Beaune |
| Confusing the Côte de Nuits and the Côte de Beaune | The Côte de Nuits is the northern half of the Côte d'Or, the Côte de Beaune the southern half |
| Putting Hermitage and Saint-Joseph on the same bank | Hermitage is on the east bank at Tain-l'Hermitage; Saint-Joseph runs along the west bank opposite |
| Forgetting that Tavel and Lirac are west of the river | They are the Southern Rhône crus on the west bank; Gigondas and Vacqueyras lie east of Châteauneuf-du-Pape |
| Placing Toro east of Ribera del Duero | Toro is to the west, further down the Duero towards Portugal |
| Swapping Barolo and Barbaresco around Alba | Barbaresco lies north-east of Alba, Barolo to the south-west |
| Thinking the Douro starts at Porto | The vineyards begin about 80 km inland; Porto and Vila Nova de Gaia are where the wine was traditionally aged and shipped |

## Five regions to learn first, and what to look for
If you have little time, start with the regions where position explains style most clearly. These five give the best return on a few minutes of map practice.
### Bordeaux
Look at the three rivers first: the Garonne and the Dordogne join to form the Gironde estuary. Everything else follows: gravel-rich Left Bank communes along the Gironde, clay-rich Right Bank appellations around Libourne, and Entre-Deux-Mers between the rivers. Sauternes and Barsac sit along the Garonne, where autumn mists from the cool Ciron help noble rot.
### Burgundy
Read it as a long north–south line. Chablis is isolated far to the north; then the Côte d'Or (Côte de Nuits, then Côte de Beaune), the Côte Chalonnaise, the Mâconnais and finally Beaujolais. On the Côte d'Or map, notice how the famous villages form a thin strip along the slope.
### The Rhône
Two separate blocks. The Northern Rhône is a narrow valley with steep slopes on both banks; the Southern Rhône opens into a wide plain around Châteauneuf-du-Pape, with Gigondas and Vacqueyras to the east and Tavel and Lirac to the west.
### Piemonte
Find Alba first, then Barolo to the south-west and Barbaresco to the north-east, both in the Langhe hills along the Tanaro. Asti and the Barbera d'Asti zone lie further north-east; Gavi is in the south-east, towards Liguria.
### New Zealand
Follow the islands from north to south: Hawke's Bay and Gimblett Gravels on the North Island's east coast, Martinborough near Wellington, Marlborough at the north-east tip of the South Island, and Central Otago, the southernmost and most continental region.

Questions you miss on a geography topic come back in your mistakes review; flashcards cover the same places with grapes and rules; the films show some regions in motion; and the app suggests map sessions among its daily recommendations. If you are not sure where to start, the wine knowledge test will show which regions are your weakest, and those are the maps to open first.
