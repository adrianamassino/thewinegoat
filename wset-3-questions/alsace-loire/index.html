Study notes: Alsace and the Loire for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Alsace and Loire questions focus on cool-climate white wines and the reasons behind their styles. Alsace is a dry, sunny region protected by mountains, making aromatic, mostly varietal whites from dry to lusciously sweet. The Loire is a long river valley, maritime at its mouth and more continental inland, famous for Melon, Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Franc. These notes go beyond the sample questions above and follow the cause, effect and style logic that exam answers are marked on.

Alsace for WSET 3: climate, grapes and labels

Why Alsace is dry and sunny

Alsace lies in north-east France, between the Vosges mountains to the west and the Rhine to the east. Its climate is continental, but the Vosges create a rain shadow, so Alsace is one of the driest and sunniest parts of France. Long, dry, sunny autumns allow grapes to hang late on the vine, which explains why late-harvest and botrytised sweet wines are possible and why even dry wines can be full-bodied with ripe fruit. The best vineyards are on the lower slopes of the Vosges facing east and south-east, where they catch the morning sun; the flatter plain gives higher yields and is often used for Crémant d'Alsace.

Alsace winemaking

Alsace winemaking aims to preserve the pure fruit of aromatic grapes. Malolactic conversion is usually avoided to keep freshness, and wines are fermented and stored in stainless steel or large old oak casks (foudres) that add texture but little oak flavour. Producers may ferment to dryness or stop with some residual sugar, so the same grape can be dry, off-dry or medium. Because sweetness is not always obvious from the label, many producers now show a sweetness indication on the bottle.

Appellations and ripeness terms

  • Alsace AOC: the regional appellation for still wines, usually varietally labelled (100% of the named grape).
  • Alsace Grand Cru AOC: just over fifty named vineyards, mainly for Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat, with lower maximum yields.
  • Crémant d'Alsace: traditional-method sparkling wine, mostly Pinot Blanc, also Riesling, Pinot Gris, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir.
  • Vendanges Tardives (VT): late-harvested grapes with higher ripeness; the wine can be off-dry to sweet, with or without botrytis.
  • Sélection de Grains Nobles (SGN): grapes affected by noble rot, higher sugar thresholds than VT, always sweet and not made every year.
  • Edelzwicker or Gentil: blends of several grapes, not varietally labelled.

Common mistake: assuming every Alsace Gewurztraminer or Pinot Gris is sweet. Write that style depends on the producer's choice to ferment to dryness or leave residual sugar, and on ripeness at harvest; only SGN is always sweet.

The Loire Valley for WSET 3: four areas, four styles

The Loire Valley for WSET 3 is easiest to learn from west to east. Near the Atlantic the climate is cool and maritime; travelling inland it becomes more continental, with colder winters, warmer summers and greater frost risk. Throughout the valley grapes struggle to ripen in cool years, so high acidity is the common thread of almost every Loire wine.

Pays Nantais: Muscadet

Muscadet is made from Melon (Melon Blanc). The maritime climate and the grape's neutral flavour give light-bodied, dry, high-acid wines with low to medium alcohol. Many are aged sur lie, on the dead yeast through the winter, which adds texture and a slightly bready character. Muscadet Sèvre et Maine is the largest and best-known sub-area, and a few named village areas (crus communaux) make wines with longer lees ageing.

Anjou-Saumur: Chenin Blanc and Cabernet Franc

Chenin Blanc is the key white grape here: it has high acidity, ripens unevenly and is susceptible to noble rot, so it can make every style. Savennières makes dry, intense Chenin with high acidity that ages well. Coteaux du Layon, with Bonnezeaux and Quarts de Chaume, makes sweet wines from late-harvested or botrytised Chenin, helped by autumn mists from the Layon. Saumur makes traditional-method sparkling and Saumur-Champigny makes fresh, medium-bodied Cabernet Franc. Rosé d'Anjou and Cabernet d'Anjou are medium-dry to medium rosés.

Touraine: Vouvray, Chinon and Bourgueil

Vouvray and Montlouis-sur-Loire make Chenin Blanc from dry to sweet, plus sparkling wine; the style depends on the vintage. Chinon, Bourgueil and Saint-Nicolas-de-Bourgueil are the main Cabernet Franc appellations: light to medium body, high acidity, raspberry and red cherry fruit, and leafy or green pepper notes in cooler years. Generic Touraine is a source of good-value Sauvignon Blanc.

Central Vineyards: Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé

Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé, almost in the centre of France, have a continental climate. Sauvignon Blanc here is dry, high in acidity, light to medium bodied, with gooseberry, green apple and grassy notes, usually unoaked; some producers use oak for richer cuvées. Soils include limestone, marl and flint (silex). Pouilly-Fumé is white only; Sancerre also makes light Pinot Noir reds and rosés. Menetou-Salon, Quincy and Reuilly are nearby, cheaper alternatives.

Exam technique: if asked why Loire Cabernet Franc can taste green, link cause and effect: cool continental climate, short season, late-ripening grape, so in cool years it reaches lower sugar levels and keeps herbaceous green pepper flavours and high acidity. Warmer vintages and the best slopes give riper fruit.

Alsace and the Loire in the two WSET Level 3 units

WSET Level 3 is assessed in two separate units: a theory unit (50 multiple-choice questions and 4 short written answers) and a tasting unit (two wines assessed blind with the Systematic Approach to Tasting). Each unit needs at least 55% and is marked on its own; if you fail one, you retake only that unit. Alsace and the Loire are classic theory topics, especially for written answers on how one grape, such as Chenin Blanc, can give several styles.

They are also ideal practice wines for the tasting unit, even though you are never asked to name the wine. Aromatic whites train you to describe pronounced primary aromas precisely; Gewurztraminer teaches you to recognise low acidity with high alcohol; Muscadet teaches you what light body and lees character feel like; sweet Chenin teaches you to judge sweetness and acidity together.

Chenin Blanc: one grape, five styles

A favourite written-answer theme is how the same grape gives very different wines. Chenin Blanc in the Loire is the textbook case because high acidity, uneven ripening and susceptibility to noble rot give the producer many options.

In a written answer, always link the style to a cause: the vintage (warm years give riper, sweeter wines), the site (mists near rivers favour noble rot) and the producer's choice (stop fermentation or ferment to dryness).

Practice questions with answers explained

Question 1: why can Alsace make full-bodied dry whites although it lies in northern France? Answer: the Vosges mountains create a rain shadow, so Alsace is dry and sunny with a long autumn. Grapes on the east-facing lower slopes ripen fully, reaching high sugar levels; fermented to dryness, this gives high alcohol and full body.

Question 2: explain why Muscadet is usually light-bodied and neutral. Answer: the Pays Nantais has a cool maritime climate, so grapes ripen moderately and keep high acidity with low to medium alcohol. Melon is a neutral grape, and producers avoid oak and malolactic conversion. Ageing sur lie adds texture and a bready note without making the wine heavy.

Question 3: what is the difference in style between Sancerre and Muscadet? Answer: both are dry, high-acid, mostly unoaked whites, but Sancerre is made from aromatic Sauvignon Blanc in a continental climate, with pronounced gooseberry and grassy notes, while Muscadet is made from neutral Melon near the Atlantic, with delicate citrus and lees character.

Question 4: why is Gewurztraminer from Alsace often off-dry? Answer: the grape accumulates high sugar levels while its acidity falls; some producers keep a little residual sugar to balance the high alcohol and the bitter phenolic grip, and to emphasise the lychee and rose aromas.

Glossary for Alsace and the Loire

  • Foudre: large old oak cask used in Alsace; adds texture with little oak flavour.
  • Sur lie: ageing on the dead yeast through the winter before bottling, typical of Muscadet.
  • Tries: successive pickings through the vineyard to select only botrytised or shrivelled grapes.
  • Silex: flint soil found in parts of Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé.
  • Tuffeau: soft limestone of Saumur and Touraine, also used to dig cool cellars.
  • Crémant: French traditional-method sparkling wine made outside Champagne, such as Crémant d'Alsace and Crémant de Loire.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions on Alsace and the Loire are in The Wine Goat?

The app has 79 multiple-choice questions on Alsace and the Loire, each with an explanation, plus short written answer questions and flashcards on the same topic.

Is Alsace and the Loire important for the WSET Level 3 exam?

Yes. The 50 multiple-choice questions cover the whole syllabus and the 4 short written answers often focus on one region or process, so every topic, including Alsace and the Loire, can come up.

Which grapes are allowed in Alsace Grand Cru for WSET 3?

For WSET Level 3, remember that Alsace Grand Cru is mainly reserved for the four noble grapes: Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat. A few individual grands crus allow exceptions, such as Sylvaner or blends, but the exam expects the four main varieties. Labels usually show the vineyard name, grape variety and vintage.

What is the difference between VT and SGN in Alsace?

Vendanges Tardives (VT) is made from late-harvested, very ripe grapes and can range from off-dry to sweet, with or without noble rot. Sélection de Grains Nobles (SGN) requires higher ripeness, usually achieved through noble rot, so it is always sweet, more concentrated and only made in suitable years. Both terms can be used with Alsace and Alsace Grand Cru.

What does sur lie mean for Muscadet?

Sur lie means the wine stays on its dead yeast cells (lees) over the winter after fermentation and is bottled from the lees, without racking. This adds texture, body and a subtle bready or yeasty note to the naturally neutral Melon grape, and can keep a slight prickle of carbon dioxide. It is the classic technique for quality Muscadet Sèvre et Maine.

Why can Chenin Blanc make so many styles in the Loire?

Chenin Blanc has high acidity, so it balances sweetness and suits sparkling wine. It ripens unevenly, so growers can pick in several passes for different styles, and its thin skin makes it susceptible to noble rot in sites with autumn mists. That is why the Loire makes dry Savennières, sweet Coteaux du Layon and sparkling Vouvray from the same grape.

Is Sancerre always Sauvignon Blanc?

White Sancerre is always Sauvignon Blanc, but the appellation also allows red and rosé wines made from Pinot Noir, which are light-bodied with red fruit and high acidity. Pouilly-Fumé, across the river, is white only, from Sauvignon Blanc. For WSET 3 answers, describe white Sancerre as dry, high acid, gooseberry and grassy, usually unoaked.

Can an Alsace or Loire wine come up in the WSET 3 tasting unit?

Any wine within the syllabus can be poured, but you are never asked to identify it. The tasting unit, which is marked separately from theory with its own 55% pass mark, rewards an accurate, complete SAT note and justified conclusions. Aromatic Alsace whites and Loire Chenin are excellent practice because they test aroma intensity, sweetness and acidity judgements.

How is sweet Coteaux du Layon made?

Chenin Blanc is left on the vine late into autumn. Mists from the Layon river favour noble rot, which concentrates sugar and acidity, and pickers go through the vineyard several times to select only the best shrivelled or botrytised grapes. The result is a sweet wine with high acidity, honey and dried fruit notes, and good ageing potential.

What is the difference between Muscadet and Muscat?

They are unrelated. Muscadet is a dry white wine from the western Loire made from the neutral Melon grape, often aged sur lie. Muscat is a family of very aromatic grapes with a grapey, floral character; in Alsace it usually makes dry, light to medium-bodied wines. Confusing the two is a classic multiple-choice trap.

Why is Loire Cabernet Franc lighter than Bordeaux reds?

The Loire is cooler, especially in Touraine, so Cabernet Franc ripens less fully than Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot do in Bordeaux. The result is lighter body, higher acidity, red fruit and sometimes leafy notes. Many producers also use limited extraction and older oak or stainless steel to keep the fresh fruit character.

Is Crémant d'Alsace made like Champagne?

Yes, it uses the traditional method: a second fermentation in the bottle followed by ageing on the lees. The main grape is Pinot Blanc, with Riesling, Pinot Gris, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir also allowed. It is often made from grapes grown on the flatter plain, where yields are higher, and is usually fresher and fruitier than Champagne.
