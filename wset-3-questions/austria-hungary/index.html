Study notes: Austria and Hungary for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Austria and Hungary questions focus on two ideas: Austria's high-quality dry whites from Grüner Veltliner and Riesling, with the sweet wines of Burgenland, and Hungary's Tokaj, one of the world's great botrytised sweet wines. Both countries have cool continental climates where water bodies, warm winds and autumn mists make a big difference. These notes go beyond the sample questions above and follow the cause, effect and style logic used to mark exam answers.

Austria for WSET 3: regions and climate

Austria's vineyards are in the east of the country, because the Alps cover the west. The climate is cool continental, with cold winters and warm summers. In Niederösterreich (Lower Austria) cool air from the north and the forests helps keep acidity; towards the Hungarian border in Burgenland, warmer air from the Pannonian plain allows fuller reds and sweet wines. Rivers and lakes moderate temperatures and, around Lake Neusiedl, create the humidity needed for noble rot.

Niederösterreich: Wachau, Kamptal, Kremstal, Weinviertel

The Wachau is a narrow stretch of the Danube with steep terraced vineyards on primary rock. Warm days and cool nights, helped by cold air from the forested hills to the north, give whites with ripe fruit and high acidity. Kamptal and Kremstal, just to the east, make similar Grüner Veltliner and Riesling, often from loess and rock soils. The Weinviertel is Austria's largest region and its classic wine is fresh, peppery Grüner Veltliner; it was the first region to adopt a DAC.

Wachau quality categories

CategoryAlcoholStyle
SteinfederUp to 11.5% abvLight, fresh, dry, for early drinking

These categories are used by a Wachau producers' association and describe dry wines by ripeness and body. They are a common multiple-choice topic because they are unique to the Wachau.

Burgenland and Steiermark

Burgenland is the warmest region. The shallow Lake Neusiedl (Neusiedlersee) creates morning mists in autumn, and warm, dry afternoons then concentrate the grapes, so noble rot develops reliably. Botrytised sweet wines are made from Welschriesling and other grapes, and the town of Rust is famous for Ruster Ausbruch. Burgenland is also Austria's main red wine area: Blaufränkisch gives deep colour, high acidity, firm tannin and black cherry and spice, especially in Mittelburgenland; Zweigelt, Austria's most planted black grape (a crossing of Blaufränkisch and St. Laurent), is softer, fruity and easy-drinking. Steiermark (Styria), in the south, has hilly vineyards and is known for crisp, aromatic Sauvignon Blanc.

Austrian wine law: DAC and Prädikat

Austrian wine law combines a German-style ripeness system with a newer regional system. The DAC (Districtus Austriae Controllatus) system links each region to its typical grapes and style, so the label shows the origin rather than the variety first; for example, Weinviertel DAC means Grüner Veltliner. Many DACs now use a pyramid of regional, village and single-vineyard wines.

  • Qualitätswein: quality wine from a single named region, the main category for dry wines.
  • Prädikatswein: categories based on the sugar level of the grapes at harvest, as in Germany: Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein.
  • Ausbruch: a traditional category between Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese, associated with Rust.
  • Strohwein or Schilfwein: sweet wine from grapes dried on straw or reeds after harvest.
  • Unlike Germany, Austrian Prädikat wines are almost always sweet; Spätlese and above cannot be chaptalised.

Exam technique: if asked to describe Grüner Veltliner, include structure as well as flavour. A strong answer: medium to full body, high acidity, citrus, green apple and stone fruit with a typical white pepper note; the best examples from the Wachau and Kamptal age to honey and toast. Mention that at high yields it gives simple, light wines.

Hungary for WSET 3: Tokaj

Tokaj is in north-east Hungary, where vineyards on volcanic hills meet the Bodrog and Tisza rivers. In autumn the rivers create morning mists that encourage noble rot, while warm, dry afternoons concentrate the shrivelled berries. The climate is continental, with long, dry autumns that allow late picking.

The Tokaj grapes

  • Furmint: the main grape, with high acidity, susceptibility to botrytis and the ability to make fine dry and sweet wines.
  • Hárslevelű: aromatic and floral, often blended with Furmint.
  • Sárgamuskotály (Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains): adds grapey aromatics in small proportions.

How Tokaji Aszú is made

  1. Botrytised (aszú) berries are picked individually, often in several passes through the vineyard.
  2. A base wine or fermenting must is made from healthy grapes.
  3. The aszú berries are crushed to a paste and macerated in the base wine or must, which extracts sugar, acidity and flavour.
  4. The wine is pressed, fermentation finishes slowly, and the wine is aged in small oak casks in cool, humid underground cellars.
  5. The result is sweet, high in acidity, with apricot, marmalade, honey and, with age, nutty and caramel notes.

Sweetness was traditionally expressed in puttonyos, the number of baskets of aszú berries added to a cask of base wine. Today Tokaji Aszú must reach a high minimum level of residual sugar, so the old lower categories of 3 and 4 puttonyos are no longer used; 5 and 6 puttonyos may still appear on labels.

Outside Tokaj, Hungary makes red wines from Kékfrankos (Blaufränkisch), Cabernet Franc and blends, notably in Eger and Villány, but at WSET Level 3 most questions focus on Tokaj.

Common mistake: describing Eszencia as the same thing as Aszú. Eszencia is only the syrup-like juice that runs naturally from the aszú berries under their own weight; it is so sweet that it ferments for years and stays very low in alcohol.

Worked example: a short written answer on Austria and Tokaj

Short written answers reward a clear chain of cause and effect. Here is a typical question and a model answer, followed by a note on why it would score well.

Question: Explain how the natural conditions around Lake Neusiedl and in Tokaj favour the production of high-quality sweet wines, and describe how Tokaji Aszú is made.

Model answer: Both areas have a body of water close to the vineyards. In autumn the water creates morning mists, and the humidity allows Botrytis cinerea to develop in its noble form. Warm, dry afternoons then stop the fungus from turning into grey rot and let the berries lose water, so sugar, acidity and flavour become concentrated. Long, dry autumns in a continental climate allow late picking in several passes. In Tokaj, Furmint is ideal because it is very susceptible to noble rot and keeps high acidity, which balances the sweetness. To make Aszú, the shrivelled berries are picked one by one, crushed to a paste and macerated in a base wine or fermenting must; the wine is then pressed, finishes fermenting slowly and is aged in small casks in cool, humid cellars. The result is a sweet wine with high acidity and apricot, marmalade and honey aromas that can age for decades.

Why it works: each point is linked to its effect (mist leads to noble rot, dry afternoons lead to concentration, acidity balances sugar). Listing facts without the links would earn fewer marks. The answer also names the grape and the key steps in the right order, which examiners look for.

Sample questions answered in text

Question: A label reads Wachau Federspiel. What does it tell you? Answer: The wine comes from the Wachau and belongs to the middle of the three local categories, a dry wine of moderate alcohol, fuller than Steinfeder but lighter than Smaragd. It is most likely Grüner Veltliner or Riesling.

Question: Why can Austria make dry whites of high quality in a cool continental climate? Answer: Steep, terraced slopes facing south along the Danube catch more sunlight, while cool night air coming down from the hills keeps acidity high. The river also moderates temperatures. Ripe flavours plus high acidity give the typical structure.

Question: What is Szamorodni? Answer: A Tokaj wine made from whole bunches that include some botrytised berries, rather than from selected aszú berries. It can be dry (száraz) or sweet (édes); the dry version is often aged in cask and can show nutty, slightly oxidative notes.

Question: Which Austrian red would you expect to have the firmest tannins and highest acidity? Answer: Blaufränkisch, especially from Mittelburgenland. Zweigelt is softer and fruitier.

Common mistakes on Austria and Hungary

  • Confusing Welschriesling with Riesling: they are different varieties, and Welschriesling is mainly important for sweet wines.
  • Thinking Austrian Spätlese can be dry, as in Germany: in Austria the Prädikat categories are essentially sweet wines.
  • Placing the Wachau in Burgenland: the Wachau is on the Danube in Niederösterreich, west of Vienna.
  • Forgetting that Smaragd is the ripest and fullest category, not the lightest.
  • Writing that Tokaji Aszú is low in acidity: Furmint gives high acidity, and this is why the wines age so well.
  • Mixing up Eszencia and Aszú: Eszencia is the free-run juice of aszú berries and has very low alcohol.

Austria and Hungary in the tasting unit

WSET Level 3 has two separate units: theory (multiple-choice questions plus short written answers) and tasting (two wines assessed blind with the SAT). You need at least 55% in each unit, and if you fail one unit you only retake that unit. Grüner Veltliner and Riesling are typical examples of high-acid, unoaked whites, so practise describing them precisely: pale lemon, medium(+) intensity, citrus and stone fruit, high acidity, dry. A sweet botrytised wine would show higher sugar, apricot, honey and marmalade, balanced by acidity.

Exam technique: in the tasting unit, link structure to quality. For a good Grüner Veltliner, mention that the high acidity is balanced by ripe fruit and medium to full body, and that the long finish and intensity justify a conclusion of very good quality.

Mini glossary of label terms

  • Ried: a single named vineyard site on Austrian labels.
  • Gebietswein, Ortswein, Riedenwein: regional, village and single-vineyard levels of the Austrian origin pyramid.
  • Steinfeder, Federspiel, Smaragd: the three Wachau categories, from lightest to fullest.
  • Ausbruch: a traditional sweet category associated with Rust.
  • Aszú: botrytised, shrivelled berries and the sweet wine made from them in Tokaj.
  • Száraz and édes: dry and sweet, used for Szamorodni.
  • Eszencia: the free-run juice of aszú berries, extremely sweet.

Frequently asked questions

Is Austria and Hungary important for the WSET Level 3 exam?

What are Steinfeder, Federspiel and Smaragd for WSET 3?

They are the three Wachau categories for dry white wines, defined by ripeness and alcohol. Steinfeder is light, up to 11.5% abv; Federspiel is medium-bodied, between 11.5% and 12.5%; Smaragd comes from the ripest grapes and is full-bodied, with at least 12.5%. They apply mainly to Grüner Veltliner and Riesling.

What does DAC mean on an Austrian wine label?

DAC stands for Districtus Austriae Controllatus. It is an Austrian appellation that links a region to its typical grape varieties and style, so the region name signals the wine. For example, Weinviertel DAC is peppery Grüner Veltliner, while other DACs cover Riesling, Blaufränkisch or Sauvignon Blanc. Many DACs now distinguish regional, village and single-vineyard wines.

Why is Furmint so important in Tokaj?

Furmint is the main Tokaj grape because it combines very high acidity with thin skins that are susceptible to noble rot. High acidity balances the extreme sweetness of Aszú and Eszencia and helps them age for decades. Furmint also makes high-quality dry wines, so it shows the full range of Tokaj styles.

What does puttonyos mean in Tokaji?

Puttonyos refers to the traditional baskets used to collect aszú (botrytised) berries; the number added to a cask of base wine indicated sweetness. Today Tokaji Aszú must reach a high minimum residual sugar, so the old 3 and 4 puttonyos levels have disappeared, while 5 and 6 puttonyos can still be shown on labels.

Which red grapes should I know for Austria at WSET Level 3?

Learn Zweigelt and Blaufränkisch. Zweigelt is Austria's most planted black grape, a crossing of Blaufränkisch and St. Laurent, giving soft, fruity, red cherry wines for early drinking. Blaufränkisch, especially in Burgenland, gives deeper colour, high acidity, firm tannins and black fruit with spice, and can age. St. Laurent and Pinot Noir are also grown.

How do I answer a WSET 3 short written question on Tokaji Aszú?

Start with the conditions: rivers that create autumn mists, dry afternoons and a long autumn. Then explain why Furmint suits noble rot and keeps acidity. Describe the steps in order: picking aszú berries by hand, making a paste, maceration in base wine or must, slow fermentation and cask ageing in humid cellars. Finish with the style: sweet, high acidity, apricot, marmalade, honey, long ageing potential.

Can Grüner Veltliner come up in the WSET Level 3 tasting?

It can, because it is a classic example of a high-acid, unoaked white wine. Practise describing it with precise SAT terms: pale lemon, medium(+) intensity, citrus, green apple and stone fruit, sometimes white pepper, dry, high acidity and medium to full body. The tasting unit is assessed separately from theory, so regular practice with real wines is the best preparation.

What happens if I pass the WSET 3 theory but fail the tasting?

The theory and the tasting are two separate units, and each needs at least 55%. If you fail one of them, you only retake that unit; the unit you passed is kept. This is why it is worth preparing the blind tasting with the same care as the theory, rather than relying on a strong theory result.

What is the difference between Ausbruch and Trockenbeerenauslese?

Both are sweet wines from botrytised grapes. Ausbruch is a traditional Austrian category linked to the town of Rust on Lake Neusiedl, with a must weight between Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese. Trockenbeerenauslese is the highest Prädikat category, made from individually selected shrivelled berries with very high sugar levels. For the exam, remember that Ausbruch sits just below TBA.

Which Austrian region should I link with red wine in WSET 3 questions?

Burgenland is the key red wine region, as it is the warmest part of Austria. Mittelburgenland is known for Blaufränkisch with deep colour, high acidity, firm tannin and black cherry and spice. Zweigelt, Austria's most planted black grape, is found widely and gives softer, fruitier wines. Steiermark is better known for Sauvignon Blanc.
