Study notes: Bordeaux for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Bordeaux questions test one idea above all: how a moderate maritime climate, two contrasting soil types and a blending culture produce wines that change from bank to bank and from vintage to vintage. If you can explain why Cabernet Sauvignon leads on the left bank and Merlot on the right bank, why Sauternes exists, and why prices vary so much, you can answer most multiple-choice items and write a strong short written answer. These notes go beyond the sample questions above and are organised the way exam answers are marked: cause, then effect, then style.

Climate in one paragraph

Bordeaux has a moderate maritime climate warmed by the Gulf Stream, which lengthens the growing season so that grapes can keep ripening into October. The Landes pine forest and coastal dunes shelter the vineyards from the strongest Atlantic winds, but the ocean still brings rain and humidity. Rain at flowering causes coulure and millerandage (poor fruit set), rain at harvest dilutes the grapes and encourages grey rot, and a cool year can leave Cabernet Sauvignon under-ripe. This is why vintage variation is so marked in Bordeaux and why blending several varieties is a form of insurance.

Bordeaux left bank vs right bank for WSET 3

The Bordeaux left bank vs right bank distinction is the single most examined idea for this region. The Gironde estuary is formed where the Garonne and the Dordogne meet. The left bank (Médoc and Graves) lies west of the Garonne and the Gironde; the right bank (Libournais, with Saint-Émilion and Pomerol) lies east of the Dordogne. Between the two rivers is Entre-Deux-Mers.

Left bank: gravel and Cabernet Sauvignon

Deep, free-draining gravel soils warm up quickly and reflect heat back to the vines. Late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon needs exactly that extra warmth, so it dominates the best left bank blends of the Haut-Médoc and Pessac-Léognan. The resulting wines have deep colour, high acidity, high tannin, blackcurrant and cedar notes, and great ageing potential. Merlot and Cabernet Franc fill out the mid-palate and add perfume; Petit Verdot, a very late ripener, is used in small amounts for colour, tannin and spice.

Right bank: clay, limestone and Merlot

On the right bank, cooler clay and limestone soils hold water and delay ripening, which suits early-ripening Merlot. In Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, Merlot leads the blend, usually with Cabernet Franc (and some Cabernet Sauvignon on warmer sites). The wines are typically plummier and rounder, with softer tannins than the left bank, but the best still age for decades. Pomerol has a small area of iron-rich clay that gives powerful, plush Merlot.

Exam technique: when a short written answer asks why Cabernet Sauvignon is planted on gravel, do not just write that gravel is warm. Write the full chain: gravel drains well and retains heat, Cabernet Sauvignon ripens late, so the extra warmth lets it reach full ripeness, giving ripe blackcurrant fruit and riper tannins instead of green, herbaceous flavours.

Key Bordeaux appellations to know for WSET Level 3

Bordeaux appellations follow a pyramid: generic Bordeaux and Bordeaux Supérieur at the base, then district appellations such as Médoc, Haut-Médoc, Graves and Entre-Deux-Mers, then commune appellations. A vineyard can often qualify for more than one, and producers choose the most prestigious one allowed.

  • Haut-Médoc communes: Saint-Estèphe (more clay, firm and robust), Pauillac (deep gravel, powerful Cabernet Sauvignon), Saint-Julien (balanced, elegant), Margaux (thinner gravel, perfumed and fragrant).
  • Pessac-Léognan: the northern, best part of Graves, famous for both reds and dry whites (Sauvignon Blanc and Semillon, often fermented and aged in oak).
  • Graves: reds plus dry whites; its southern enclave includes Sauternes and Barsac.
  • Saint-Émilion and Saint-Émilion Grand Cru; Pomerol; and satellite appellations around them for good-value Merlot.
  • Entre-Deux-Mers: dry white wines, mainly Sauvignon Blanc and Semillon, usually fresh and unoaked.
  • Côtes de Bordeaux: hillside areas on the right bank of the Garonne and Dordogne, for good-value, mostly Merlot-based reds.

Sauternes and Barsac: why noble rot happens here

Sauternes and Barsac sit where the cool Ciron river meets the warmer Garonne. In autumn this creates morning mists that encourage Botrytis cinerea, followed by warm, dry afternoons that stop it turning into grey rot. Noble rot concentrates sugar, acidity and flavour, and adds notes of honey, apricot and marmalade. Semillon is the main grape because its thin skin makes it susceptible to botrytis; Sauvignon Blanc adds acidity and aroma. Pickers go through the vineyard several times (tries) to harvest only affected berries, so yields are tiny and production is costly and risky. Barsac wines may be labelled Sauternes or Barsac. Nearby Loupiac, Cadillac and Sainte-Croix-du-Mont make lighter, cheaper sweet wines.

Winemaking, classifications and the Bordeaux trade

Red winemaking

Each variety and plot is usually fermented separately, then the final blend is assembled after fermentation. This lets the winemaker adjust the proportions every year to the vintage. Premium reds are aged in French oak barriques (225 litres), with a high share of new oak for the top estates, typically for well over a year. Inexpensive Bordeaux is Merlot-led, aged briefly or not at all in oak, and made for early drinking. Many estates also produce a second wine from younger vines or lots not selected for the grand vin, which keeps the quality of the main wine high.

The classifications

  1. 1855 classification: ranks red wines of the Médoc (plus one Graves estate) into five growths, and the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac. It has changed very little since.
  2. Graves classification: covers red and white wines; all classified estates are in Pessac-Léognan.
  3. Saint-Émilion classification: Premier Grand Cru Classé and Grand Cru Classé, revised roughly every ten years, so estates can be promoted or demoted.
  4. Cru Bourgeois: a separate quality ranking for Médoc estates outside the 1855 list.
  5. Pomerol: no official classification, despite some of the most expensive wines in the world.

En primeur and the négociants

Most top Bordeaux is sold en primeur: the wine is offered as futures in the spring after the harvest, while it is still in barrel, and delivered after bottling. The estates sell through courtiers (brokers) to négociants, who distribute worldwide. This system gives producers early cash flow and lets buyers secure scarce wines, but prices depend heavily on vintage reputation and critics' scores.

Common mistake: candidates confuse Graves (gravel, left bank, red and dry white) with the right bank. Remember that the word Graves itself means gravel, and Sauternes is inside the Graves district.

How to answer WSET 3 Bordeaux questions

Bordeaux short written answers often ask you to compare two wines or explain a price difference. A good structure is: name the location and climate influence, explain the soil and its effect on ripening, state the grape and the resulting structure and flavours, then link to winemaking and price. Examiners reward specific vocabulary, so use terms such as gravel, clay, Gulf Stream, botrytis, tries, barrique, en primeur and second wine where they fit.

  • Inexpensive Bordeaux: high yields on the plains, Merlot for soft fruit, little or no oak, sold under the generic Bordeaux AOC.
  • Premium Bordeaux: lower yields, best gravel or limestone sites, careful selection, new French oak, sold en primeur at high prices.
  • Dry white Bordeaux: Sauvignon Blanc for aroma and acidity, Semillon for body and ageing; oak ageing and lees contact in Pessac-Léognan.
  • Sweet Bordeaux: noble rot from autumn mists, multiple tries, Semillon-led, long-lived.

How to revise Bordeaux in 15 minutes a day

  1. Day 1: draw the map from memory: Gironde, Garonne, Dordogne, Ciron, then place Médoc, Graves, Entre-Deux-Mers, Saint-Émilion, Pomerol and Sauternes.
  2. Day 2: write the left bank vs right bank table without looking, then check it.
  3. Day 3: learn the four Haut-Médoc communes in order from north to south: Saint-Estèphe, Pauillac, Saint-Julien, Margaux.
  4. Day 4: explain Sauternes noble rot aloud in five steps: mist, botrytis, dry afternoons, tries, concentration.
  5. Day 5: list the five classification systems and one fact about each.
  6. Day 6: write one timed short answer comparing a Pauillac and a Saint-Émilion.
  7. Day 7: run a set of Bordeaux questions in the free app and review only the ones you got wrong.

Fifteen focused minutes a day for a week is enough to make Bordeaux one of your strongest regions, because almost every fact connects back to the same three causes: the Atlantic climate, the soil and the grape.

Which grapes are allowed in Bordeaux for WSET 3?

For WSET Level 3 you need the main ones: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Cabernet Franc for reds, with minor Petit Verdot (and Malbec and Carmenère in small amounts), and Semillon, Sauvignon Blanc and Muscadelle for whites. A few newer varieties are now permitted in small proportions in generic appellations to adapt to climate change, but the classic grapes are what the exam focuses on.

What is the difference between the left bank and right bank of Bordeaux?

The left bank (Médoc and Graves) has warm, free-draining gravel soils where late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon leads, giving firm, high-tannin, blackcurrant wines. The right bank (Saint-Émilion and Pomerol) has cooler clay and limestone soils that suit early-ripening Merlot, giving rounder, plummy wines with softer tannins. Both blend several varieties.

Why is Sauternes sweet?

Sauternes is sweet because morning mists from the cool Ciron river meeting the warmer Garonne encourage noble rot (Botrytis cinerea), while dry, sunny afternoons keep it from becoming grey rot. Botrytis dehydrates the berries and concentrates sugar, acid and flavour. Pickers harvest affected grapes in several passes, so the wine is rich, sweet and still fresh.

What does en primeur mean in Bordeaux?

En primeur is the system of selling wine as futures, usually in the spring after the harvest, while the wine is still ageing in barrel. Buyers pay in advance and receive the bottles one to two years later. Estates sell through brokers to négociants, gaining early cash flow, while buyers secure limited wines, often at a price that depends on the vintage reputation.

Is the 1855 classification on the WSET Level 3 exam?

Yes, at a basic level. You should know that it ranks red wines of the Médoc (plus Haut-Brion in Graves) in five growths and the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac, and that it has barely changed since 1855. Contrast it with the Saint-Émilion classification, which is revised periodically, and with Pomerol, which has no official classification.
