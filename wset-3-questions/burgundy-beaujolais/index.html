Study notes: Burgundy and Beaujolais for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Burgundy questions test three things again and again: how a cool continental climate shapes Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, how the appellation hierarchy works, and why one district tastes different from the next. These study notes give you a compact revision guide for Burgundy and Beaujolais, written the way exam answers are marked: cause, then effect, then style. Read them once, then use the sample questions above to check what has stuck.

Climate: why Burgundy is marginal

Burgundy has a cool continental climate: cold winters, warm summers and a fairly short growing season. Because the region is close to the limit for ripening Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, vintage variation is high. Both grapes bud early, so spring frost is a real danger, especially in Chablis, the coolest and most northerly district. Summer hail can wipe out a large part of a village's crop in minutes, and rain around harvest raises the risk of rot on thin-skinned Pinot Noir. A good answer links each hazard to its consequence: frost and hail reduce yield, rain at harvest dilutes flavour and spreads botrytis, and a cool year leaves wines with high acidity and lighter body.

Slope, aspect and soil in the Côte d'Or

The Côte d'Or is a long east and south-east facing escarpment. The best vineyards sit mid-slope because they get more sunlight in the morning, are sheltered from cool westerly winds by the hills behind, drain well on thin limestone and marl soils and are less exposed to frost, since cold air drains down to the flat land below. Vineyards at the foot of the slope have deeper, more fertile soils and usually make village or regional wines. Higher up, in the Hautes-Côtes, it is cooler and more exposed, so wines are lighter and leaner.

Chablis: cool, frost-prone and mineral

Chablis is cooler than the Côte d'Or, so its Chardonnay has higher acidity, lighter body and green fruit flavours. The grand cru vineyards lie together on one south-west facing slope above the town and the river Serein, which gives them more sun. Frost protection is central to Chablis questions: growers use heaters or candles among the vines and water sprinklers, which coat the buds in ice that releases latent heat and keeps them at around freezing point instead of below it. Many producers ferment in stainless steel to keep fruit purity; some premier and grand cru wines see oak, usually not much new oak.

Côte Chalonnaise and Mâconnais

South of the Côte d'Or the hills are less continuous, so vineyards are scattered and wines are generally lighter and less expensive. Montagny is white only, and Bouzeron is the one village appellation for Aligoté. The Mâconnais is warmer still, so Chardonnay is riper, with lower acidity and more melon and stone fruit; most is made in large volume by cooperatives, while Pouilly-Fuissé and Saint-Véran can be oaked, concentrated and serious.

The Burgundy appellation hierarchy explained

Burgundy classifies vineyards, not producers. Each level is more precise about where the grapes grew, and in general the price goes up with each step because supply goes down.

  1. Regional appellations: Bourgogne (and variants such as Bourgogne Aligoté and Crémant de Bourgogne). Grapes may come from anywhere in the region.
  2. Village appellations: wine from a single named village, for example Meursault or Gevrey-Chambertin.
  3. Premier Cru: a superior named vineyard within a village; the label shows the village, the vineyard name and Premier Cru or 1er Cru.
  4. Grand Cru: the very best named vineyards, a small fraction of production. The label shows only the vineyard name, such as Chambertin, Richebourg or Montrachet.

Two more ideas often appear in WSET Level 3 Burgundy questions. First, many famous villages added the name of their best grand cru to their own (Gevrey became Gevrey-Chambertin), so a village wine can carry a grand cru name. Second, ownership is extremely fragmented, because inheritance laws divided vineyards between heirs. A single grand cru such as Clos de Vougeot can have dozens of owners. This explains the importance of négociants, who buy grapes, must or wine from many growers and blend them, and of domaines, which bottle wine from their own vineyards. A vineyard owned entirely by one domaine is called a monopole.

Exam tip: if a short written answer asks why two Burgundies from the same vintage differ, name at least three factors: the site (slope position, aspect, soil), the level of the appellation, and the producer's decisions (yield, whole bunches, new oak). Generic phrases like 'terroir is different' earn very little.

Winemaking in Burgundy: Pinot Noir and Chardonnay

Red Burgundy

Pinot Noir has thin skins and relatively little colour and tannin, so winemakers aim to extract gently. Common options include a cold soak before fermentation to extract colour and fruit flavour without tannin, fermentation with some or all whole bunches to add fresh, herbal and spicy notes and a lighter, more aromatic style, and punching down rather than aggressive pump-overs. Premium wines are matured in oak barrels, typically with only a portion of new oak so that the fruit is not masked. With age, red fruit evolves towards earth, mushroom, forest floor and game.

White Burgundy

Premium white Burgundy is the model for barrel-fermented Chardonnay. Fermentation and maturation in barrel add toast and vanilla and integrate the oak better than ageing alone; malolactic conversion softens acidity and adds buttery notes; lees contact and stirring add body and biscuity, creamy texture. The balance between these techniques and the natural acidity of the site gives the style. Puligny-Montrachet is often described as finer and more mineral, Meursault as richer and nuttier: useful shorthand for multiple-choice questions.

Beaujolais: Gamay, granite and carbonic maceration

Beaujolais lies south of the Mâconnais and is warmer than the rest of Burgundy. Its grape is Gamay, which gives wines with high acidity, low to medium tannin and red fruit such as cherry, raspberry and strawberry. Traditional vines are bush-trained and hand-harvested, which matters because carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration need whole, undamaged bunches. Semi-carbonic maceration extracts colour with little tannin and adds aromas of banana, kirsch and bubblegum, which is why simple Beaujolais is soft, fruity and best drunk young.

Beaujolais, Beaujolais-Villages and the ten crus

The south of the region, on richer clay and limestone soils, mostly produces basic Beaujolais, including Beaujolais Nouveau, released on the third Thursday of November after the harvest. The north has granite hills and makes Beaujolais-Villages and the ten crus: Saint-Amour, Juliénas, Chénas, Moulin-à-Vent, Fleurie, Chiroubles, Morgon, Régnié, Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly. Fleurie and Chiroubles are usually lighter and perfumed; Moulin-à-Vent and Morgon are the most structured and can age, sometimes made with traditional fermentation and oak, so with time they may taste more like Pinot Noir.

Sample short written answer: why vineyard position matters on the Côte d'Or

Question: Explain why the best vineyards of the Côte d'Or are usually found on the middle of the slope. (Model answer.)

The Côte d'Or has a cool to moderate continental climate, so ripening is not guaranteed and small differences in site have a large effect on quality. The middle of the slope faces east and south-east, so the vines receive the morning sun and more sunlight overall, which helps the grapes ripen fully. The slope gives good drainage, and the soils there are a well-balanced mix of limestone and marl, neither too fertile nor too thin, which limits vigour and yields and concentrates flavour.

At the bottom of the slope the soils are deeper and more fertile and drainage is poorer, so yields are higher and the grapes are less concentrated; cold air also collects there, increasing frost risk. At the top the soils are thin and the vines are more exposed to cold winds, so ripening is harder. This is why grand crus and premier crus are concentrated on the mid-slope, while village and regional wines come from the flatter land below.

Common mistakes on Burgundy and Beaujolais

  • Writing that all white Burgundy is oaked: Chablis and basic Mâcon are often unoaked.
  • Placing Pouilly-Fuissé in the Loire: it is in the Mâconnais (Pouilly-Fumé is the Loire Sauvignon Blanc).
  • Forgetting that a grand cru label shows the vineyard name only, without the village.
  • Saying that Beaujolais crus are made only by carbonic maceration: many are made with semi-carbonic or traditional methods and some are oaked for longer ageing.
  • Confusing Côte de Nuits (mostly red) with Côte de Beaune (famous for both whites and reds).
  • Ignoring vintage variation: in a marginal climate, weather explains differences between years.

Burgundy and Beaujolais in the tasting unit

Remember that at Level 3 theory and tasting are separate units: you need at least 55% in each, and a failed unit is retaken on its own. Pinot Noir and Gamay are frequent practice wines for the tasting because they test your judgement of colour, acidity and tannin. A red Burgundy is usually pale to medium ruby, with red fruit (cherry, raspberry), high acidity and low to medium tannin; older examples show garnet, mushroom and forest-floor notes. A Beaujolais is often purple-ruby, with very fruity red-berry aromas, sometimes banana or bubblegum from carbonic maceration, high acidity and low tannin.

For a good quality conclusion, explain the balance between fruit, acidity and tannin and the length of the finish. A light colour is not a sign of low quality for Pinot Noir; describe what you see and taste accurately, and link the readiness for drinking to the intensity of fruit and the presence of tertiary notes.

Frequently asked questions

Which Burgundy villages should I know for WSET Level 3?

Know the main ones by district: Gevrey-Chambertin, Chambolle-Musigny, Vosne-Romanée and Nuits-Saint-Georges in the Côte de Nuits; Pommard, Volnay, Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet and Chassagne-Montrachet in the Côte de Beaune; Mercurey, Givry, Rully and Montagny in the Côte Chalonnaise; Pouilly-Fuissé and Saint-Véran in the Mâconnais. Being able to say the main colour of each is enough for most questions.

How do you tell a grand cru from a premier cru on a Burgundy label?

A grand cru label normally shows only the vineyard name, such as Chambertin or Montrachet, plus Grand Cru. A premier cru label shows the village name followed by the vineyard name and the words Premier Cru or 1er Cru, for example Meursault 1er Cru Perrières. If a label shows only a village name, it is a village wine.

What are the ten Beaujolais crus for WSET 3?

The ten crus, roughly north to south, are Saint-Amour, Juliénas, Chénas, Moulin-à-Vent, Fleurie, Chiroubles, Morgon, Régnié, Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly. They are in the granite north of the region. Moulin-à-Vent and Morgon are the fullest and most age-worthy; Fleurie and Chiroubles are lighter and more floral.

Why is Chablis different from Côte d'Or Chardonnay?

Chablis is further north and cooler, so its Chardonnay ripens less: it has higher acidity, lighter body and green apple and citrus flavours. Many producers use stainless steel or old oak to keep that fresh, pure style, while premium Côte d'Or whites are usually fermented and aged in barrel with some new oak, giving riper fruit and more toast and creaminess.

Is Beaujolais part of Burgundy in the WSET Level 3 syllabus?

For study purposes Beaujolais is grouped with Burgundy because it lies just south of the Mâconnais and is often discussed alongside it. Its climate, granite soils, Gamay grape and use of carbonic maceration make its wines quite different, so revise it as its own region with its own hierarchy: Beaujolais, Beaujolais-Villages and the ten crus.

How do I compare Chablis and Meursault in a WSET 3 answer?

Start with climate: Chablis is further north and cooler, so its Chardonnay keeps high acidity and shows green apple and citrus. Meursault, in the Côte de Beaune, ripens more fully, giving riper fruit and fuller body. Then add winemaking: Chablis is often unoaked, while Meursault is usually fermented and aged in barrel, with lees stirring and malolactic conversion adding nutty, buttery notes.

Which areas of the Côte Chalonnaise should I know for WSET 3?

Learn Rully, Mercurey, Givry and Montagny. Rully is known for whites and sparkling base wines, Mercurey and Givry mainly for reds, and Montagny for whites only. Bouzeron is the appellation for Aligoté. The wines are similar in style to the Côte d'Or but usually lighter and less concentrated, and they offer good value.

Why is frost a major risk in Chablis?

Chablis has a cool continental climate with cold springs, and frost can damage the young shoots after budburst, reducing the crop. Growers protect the vines with heaters or candles placed between the rows and with sprinkler systems that coat the buds in ice, which releases heat as it freezes and keeps the bud temperature close to zero.

Do Beaujolais crus age?

Some do. Crus such as Moulin-à-Vent and Morgon can be made with longer maceration and sometimes oak ageing, which gives more structure and allows the wines to develop for several years, taking on savoury notes that can resemble Pinot Noir. Lighter crus such as Fleurie or Chiroubles are usually best young, when their floral and red-fruit aromas are most vivid.

How does Burgundy appear in the WSET 3 tasting unit?

Theory and tasting are separate units, each needing at least 55%. In the tasting you might assess a Pinot Noir or a Chardonnay, but you are not asked to name it. You describe appearance, nose and palate with the systematic approach and reach logical conclusions on quality and drinking window. Accurate acidity and tannin levels matter more than guessing the origin.
