Study notes: climate and environment for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 climate questions ask you to explain how the natural environment of a vineyard affects the vine, the grapes and finally the wine in the glass. The topic underpins every region in the syllabus: once you understand why a site is cool, warm, wet or dry, you can work out most regional answers from first principles. These study notes give you a structured revision guide to climate and environment, written in the cause, effect, style format that markers reward.

Climate, weather and the vine's needs

Climate vs weather

Climate is the typical pattern of temperature, sunlight and rainfall in a place, averaged over many years. Weather is what actually happens in a given year. Climate decides which grape varieties and styles a region can produce; weather explains vintage variation. Vintage variation is greatest in cool, marginal regions such as Burgundy, Champagne or Germany, where a cold or wet year can stop grapes ripening fully, and smaller in warm, stable regions where ripening is rarely a problem.

Heat, sunlight, water and nutrients

The vine needs heat, sunlight, water, nutrients and carbon dioxide. Heat drives the speed of the growth cycle and ripening: below about 10 °C the vine does not grow. Sunlight powers photosynthesis, which produces the sugars that accumulate in the grapes. Water is needed for photosynthesis and to keep the vine cool; too little causes stress, too much encourages vigorous leaf growth at the expense of fruit. Nutrients, especially nitrogen, support growth, but excess nitrogen also leads to vigour, shading and lower-quality fruit.

WSET 3 climate classification: cool, moderate, warm and hot

WSET Level 3 classifies climates by the average temperature of the growing season (roughly April to October in the northern hemisphere and October to April in the southern hemisphere). Learn the bands and the style each one produces.

Continental, maritime and Mediterranean climates

Continentality is the difference between the average temperature of the hottest and coldest months. Continental climates, far from the sea, have hot summers, cold winters and a short growing season; temperatures fall quickly in autumn, so early-ripening varieties suit them, and spring frost is a major risk. Maritime climates, near the sea, have a small annual range, mild winters, cooler summers and rain spread through the year, which raises disease pressure. Mediterranean climates have warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters, giving reliable ripening but often a need for irrigation.

Factors that change temperature and sunlight

Within any climate, local factors can make a vineyard warmer or cooler. These are the ones WSET Level 3 climate questions most often test.

  • Latitude: most vineyards lie between 30° and 50° north and south; closer to the Equator, the sun is stronger and the climate hotter.
  • Altitude: temperature falls by roughly 0.6 °C for every 100 m of height, so high vineyards can offset low latitude, as in Salta.
  • Slope and aspect: slopes facing the Equator (south in the northern hemisphere) receive sunlight at a steeper angle, so they warm more; steep slopes also drain cold air and water.
  • Bodies of water: oceans, lakes and rivers moderate temperatures, warming nights and autumns and reducing frost risk; they can also reflect sunlight onto slopes.
  • Ocean currents and fog: cold currents such as the Humboldt and Benguela cool coastal areas; fog shades vines and slows ripening.
  • Soil colour and stones: dark or stony soils absorb heat by day and release it at night, helping ripening in cool regions.
  • Diurnal range: a big difference between day and night temperatures preserves acidity and fresh aromas while sugars still accumulate.

Exam tip: when asked how a factor affects quality, always finish the chain in the glass. 'Cool sea breezes lower temperatures, so ripening is slower, so grapes keep more acidity and fresh fruit, giving a lighter, fresher wine' scores far better than 'sea breezes cool the vineyard'.

Water, soils and natural hazards

Water supply and soil

How much water reaches the roots depends on rainfall, evaporation and soil. Free-draining soils such as gravel and sand stay warm and limit vigour, but may need irrigation in dry climates. Clay holds water and stays cool, which helps in hot, dry summers but can delay ripening in cool years. Mild water stress after véraison is often considered beneficial: shoot growth stops and the vine directs energy to ripening, giving smaller berries with more concentrated flavour. Severe stress, however, closes the leaves' pores, stops photosynthesis and can halt ripening altogether.

Frost, hail and other hazards

Winter freeze can kill vines when temperatures drop far below zero. Spring frost is more common: it damages young buds and shoots and reduces yield. Hail tears leaves and splits berries, opening the way to rot. Rain at flowering causes poor fruit set (coulure and millerandage), while rain at harvest dilutes flavours and spreads rot. Heatwaves and drought can cause sunburn, shut down the vine and raise alcohol. Growers respond with site selection and specific techniques.

  1. Choose sites with good air drainage, such as slopes, and avoid frost pockets in valley bottoms.
  2. Prune late or train vines higher, so buds burst later or sit above the coldest air.
  3. Use wind machines to mix warmer air from above into the vineyard during still, clear nights.
  4. Use heaters or candles to warm the air around the vines.
  5. Use sprinklers: as water freezes on the buds, it releases latent heat that protects them.
  6. Use hail nets where hail is frequent, as in parts of Argentina.

Climate change

Warmer growing seasons are bringing earlier harvests, riper grapes, higher alcohol and lower acidity in many regions, plus more extreme events such as drought, heatwaves and wildfire. Responses include planting at higher altitude or on cooler aspects, choosing later-ripening varieties or clones, leaving more leaf cover to shade bunches, and picking earlier.

What is the difference between a continental and a maritime climate for WSET 3?

A continental climate has a big difference between summer and winter temperatures: hot summers, cold winters and a short season that cools quickly in autumn, with high spring frost risk. A maritime climate, near the sea, has a small annual range, mild winters, cooler summers and rain throughout the year, which brings more disease pressure but less frost.

Which temperatures define cool, moderate, warm and hot climates in WSET Level 3?

The bands are based on average growing season temperature: cool is 16.5 °C or less, moderate is 16.5 to 18.5 °C, warm is 18.5 to 21 °C and hot is above 21 °C. Learn them with an example region each, because questions often ask you to link a band to typical acidity, body, alcohol and fruit character.

How do sprinklers protect vines against frost?

Sprinklers spray water onto buds and shoots during a frost. As the water freezes it releases latent heat, which keeps the tissue inside the ice at around 0 °C instead of colder temperatures that would kill it. Spraying must continue until the ice melts. The method needs a large water supply and can cause waterlogging.

Why does a wide diurnal range improve wine quality?

Warm days let grapes build sugar and ripe flavours, while cool nights slow the breakdown of acids and help preserve aroma compounds. The result is wine with ripe fruit but fresh acidity and good aromatic intensity. High-altitude and some coastal regions are known for this effect.

Does soil affect wine style in WSET Level 3?

Yes, mainly through water and heat. Free-draining gravel and sand reduce vigour and warm up quickly; clay holds water and stays cool; stony or dark soils store heat and release it at night. Soil fertility matters too: very fertile soils encourage vigour and shading, which can lower fruit quality.
