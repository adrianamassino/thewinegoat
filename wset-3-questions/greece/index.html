Study notes: Greece for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Greece questions are short and predictable: they test the Mediterranean climate and how altitude and sea breezes moderate it, a handful of native grapes, and the link between each grape and its appellation. Greece is a small topic in the syllabus, which makes it an easy place to collect marks if you learn the key pairs and can explain them with cause and effect. These notes are the revision guide behind the sample questions above.

Climate: hot, dry summers moderated by altitude and the sea

Greece has a Mediterranean climate: hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. Almost no rain falls during the growing season, so drought and water stress are real risks, even in the wetter west of the country. The Pindus mountains run down the centre and block rain coming from the Ionian Sea, so the west is wetter and the east drier. Northern Greece, especially Macedonia, is cooler and more continental than the south and the islands.

  • Altitude cools vineyards: many quality sites are planted on slopes and plateaus to slow ripening and keep acidity.
  • Sea breezes from the Aegean and Ionian seas moderate temperatures on the coasts and islands.
  • Strong winds, such as the summer winds of the Aegean islands, cool the vines but can damage shoots and reduce yields.
  • Low summer rainfall means little fungal disease, but irrigation or drought-tolerant training is often needed.
  • Most native grapes are well adapted to heat and drought, which is one reason Greece relies on them.

In a short written answer on Greek wine, always add the moderating factor. Hot Mediterranean climate on its own explains ripeness and alcohol, but not freshness. Write: hot climate, but vineyards at altitude or near the sea, so cooler nights and slower ripening, so the wines keep acidity.

Greek grape varieties for WSET Level 3

Greece has more than 200 native grape varieties. For WSET Level 3 you need a small group, and above all the three most recognised abroad: Assyrtiko, Agiorgitiko and Xinomavro. International grapes such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Chardonnay are also grown and are often used in PGI wines, sometimes blended with native grapes.

Assyrtiko

Assyrtiko is the flagship white grape of Greece. Its key quality is that it retains very high acidity even in a hot climate, which is why it can make fresh, structured wines on a sun-baked island. It shows citrus fruit such as lemon and grapefruit, and often a stony, mineral character. It can be made in stainless steel for freshness or aged in oak for extra texture, and the best examples develop with bottle age. It is also used for sweet wines.

Agiorgitiko

Agiorgitiko is the most important black grape of the Peloponnese and the only grape allowed in Nemea red. It is versatile: it can make rosé, light fruity unoaked reds with soft tannins and red cherry and plum fruit, or richer oak-aged reds with sweet spice. Style depends strongly on altitude, as described below.

Xinomavro

Xinomavro is the leading black grape of northern Greece. Its name means acid black, which is a useful clue: it has high acidity and high tannin with only medium colour that develops a tawny tint early. It is often compared with Nebbiolo. Young wines show red cherry and raspberry with tomato and olive notes, and with age the best wines develop dried fruit, spice and earth. Because of its structure it needs ageing, often in oak, and can age for many years.

Key Greek appellations: Naoussa, Nemea, Santorini, Mantinia

Most Greek PDO wines must be made from native grapes, which is why the exam links each appellation to a single grape. Learn these pairs as a block: Naoussa and Amyndeon with Xinomavro, Nemea with Agiorgitiko, Santorini with Assyrtiko, Mantinia with Moschofilero.

Naoussa and Amyndeon (Macedonia)

Naoussa PDO, in Macedonia in northern Greece, makes red wine only from Xinomavro. The climate is cooler than in the south because of the northern latitude and the hillside vineyards, so the grape keeps its high acidity and firm tannins. Amyndeon is higher and cooler still; there Xinomavro is also made into rosé and sparkling wine, where its acidity is an asset.

Nemea (Peloponnese)

Nemea PDO, in the northern Peloponnese, makes red wine only from Agiorgitiko. Vineyards range widely in altitude and this changes the style: low, warm sites give ripe, jammy, soft wines; mid-altitude sites are usually considered the best balance of ripeness and freshness; the highest, coolest sites give more acidic, lighter wines that suit rosé.

Santorini (Aegean island)

Santorini PDO is a volcanic island in the Aegean famous for Assyrtiko. It has very low rainfall and strong winds. The volcanic, sandy soils are poor and phylloxera cannot survive in them, so many vines are very old and ungrafted. To protect the grapes from the wind and windblown sand, vines are traditionally trained low into basket shapes on the ground (koulara). Yields are very low, which gives concentrated wines with high acidity, full body and citrus and mineral flavours. Santorini also makes Vinsanto, a sweet wine from sun-dried grapes, mainly Assyrtiko, aged for a long time in oak.

Mantinia (Peloponnese)

Mantinia PDO is a high plateau in the central Peloponnese and one of the cooler places to grow grapes in southern Greece. It makes white wine from Moschofilero: light-bodied, high in acidity, with pronounced floral and grapey aromas. The altitude explains the freshness despite the southern latitude.

Sample short written answer: Santorini Assyrtiko

Exam-style question: explain how climate, soil and viticulture influence the style of Santorini Assyrtiko. Points to include:

  1. Hot, dry Mediterranean climate with very little rain, so the grapes ripen fully and water is scarce.
  2. Strong winds cool the vines but can damage them, so vines are trained low in basket shapes to protect shoots and bunches.
  3. Poor volcanic soils with low fertility, so yields are very low and the wines are concentrated.
  4. Phylloxera cannot survive in the sandy soils, so many vines are old and ungrafted, adding concentration.
  5. Assyrtiko naturally retains high acidity in heat, so the wine is full-bodied yet fresh, with citrus and stony notes, and can age.

Common mistake: mixing up the pairs, for example Nemea with Xinomavro. A quick memory trick: N for North for Naoussa (Xinomavro, north), Nemea is the Peloponnese with Agiorgitiko, and S for Santorini and Sea for Assyrtiko on the island.

Frequently asked questions

Which Greek grapes do you need for WSET 3?

The essential grapes are Assyrtiko (white, Santorini), Agiorgitiko (black, Nemea) and Xinomavro (black, Naoussa and Amyndeon). It also helps to know Moschofilero, a pink-skinned aromatic grape from Mantinia, and Malagousia, an aromatic white. Learn each grape with its colour, appellation and two or three style words.

Why are Santorini vines trained in baskets?

On Santorini the vines are trained low into basket shapes, called koulara, to protect the shoots and bunches from strong winds and windblown volcanic sand. The basket also shades the grapes and helps the vine use the limited moisture available. This is a classic WSET 3 example of training adapted to local climate.

Why is Santorini phylloxera-free?

Phylloxera cannot survive in Santorini's sandy volcanic soils, so the island never suffered the pest. As a result vines are planted on their own roots and many are very old. Old, ungrafted, low-yielding vines contribute to the concentration of Santorini Assyrtiko.

How does altitude affect Nemea wines?

In Nemea, Agiorgitiko is planted across a wide altitude range. Low, warm sites give ripe, soft, jammy reds; mid-altitude vineyards are generally seen as the best balance of ripeness and acidity; the highest sites give lighter, more acidic wines that are often used for rosé. This is a frequent WSET 3 cause-and-effect question.

What is Vinsanto from Santorini?

Vinsanto is a sweet wine from Santorini made mainly from Assyrtiko grapes that are dried in the sun after harvest to concentrate their sugar. The wine is then aged for a long time in oak, giving a rich, sweet style with high acidity and dried fruit and caramel notes.
