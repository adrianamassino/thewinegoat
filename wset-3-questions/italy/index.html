Study notes: Italy for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Italy questions cover a huge country, so the trick is structure: learn Italy from north to south, link each region to its key grape and its climate, and memorise a short list of ageing rules and labelling terms. With so many native grapes and appellations, Italy is one of the biggest topics in the syllabus. These study notes give you the revision framework behind the sample questions above.

Italian wine labels: DOCG, DOC, IGT, Classico, Superiore, Riserva

Italy uses traditional terms alongside the EU categories. DOCG and DOC are PDO wines, with DOCG the higher tier with stricter rules; IGT is the PGI category, often used for wines made outside appellation rules, such as many Super Tuscans. Several other label terms appear in exam questions:

  • Classico: wine from the historic, original heart of an appellation, often on the best hillside sites (Chianti Classico, Soave Classico, Valpolicella Classico).
  • Superiore: a higher minimum alcohol and sometimes a longer minimum ageing than the basic wine.
  • Riserva: a longer minimum ageing period than the basic wine, set by each appellation.
  • Gran Selezione: the top tier of Chianti Classico, above Riserva.

Northern Italy for WSET Level 3: Piedmont and Veneto

Piedmont: Nebbiolo, Barbera, Dolcetto

Piedmont sits at the foot of the Alps and has a moderate continental climate with fog in autumn. Nebbiolo buds early and ripens very late, so it needs the warmest south-facing hillside sites. It gives pale wines that develop a garnet and orange rim early, with high acidity, high tannin and aromas of red cherry, roses and violets, developing tar, leather and dried fruit with age. Barolo and Barbaresco are the top Nebbiolo DOCGs; Barbaresco is generally slightly lighter and more approachable younger. Barbera has deep colour, high acidity and low tannin, with red cherry and plum, and can be made unoaked or with new oak (Barbera d'Asti, Barbera d'Alba). Dolcetto ripens early and gives soft, deeply coloured, fruity reds for early drinking. Cortese makes Gavi, a light, high-acid dry white.

Veneto: Soave, Valpolicella, Amarone

Soave is made mainly from Garganega, giving dry whites with medium to high acidity, lemon, pear and almond notes; the best come from the volcanic hills of the Classico zone. Valpolicella is based on Corvina, a black grape with high acidity, light colour and sour red cherry flavours. Basic Valpolicella is light and fruity. Amarone della Valpolicella is made from grapes dried after harvest (appassimento), which concentrates sugar, flavour and tannin; fermenting to dryness gives a full-bodied, high-alcohol red with dried fruit notes. Recioto della Valpolicella is the sweet version from the same dried grapes. Valpolicella Ripasso is Valpolicella refermented on the pomace left after Amarone, gaining body, alcohol, tannin and dried fruit character.

Further north, Alto Adige and Trentino make fresh whites and light reds thanks to altitude and a wide diurnal range, while Friuli is known for high-quality, often unoaked whites such as Friulano and Pinot Grigio.

Central Italy: Tuscany and Sangiovese

Sangiovese is the most planted black grape in Italy and the backbone of Tuscany. It is late-ripening, with high acidity and high tannin, medium colour, and flavours of red cherry, plum and dried herbs, developing leather and earthy notes with age. The Tuscan climate is warm Mediterranean, moderated by altitude in the hills where the best vineyards lie.

Ageing rules and key numbers

A small set of numbers comes up again and again. Barolo DOCG requires at least 38 months of ageing, of which 18 months in wood (Riserva 62 months). Barbaresco DOCG requires at least 26 months, of which 9 months in wood (Riserva 50 months). Brunello di Montalcino must spend at least two years in oak and cannot be released until five years after the harvest (six for Riserva). If an exact rule is not asked, explain the effect: long ageing in large old casks or barrels softens tannins and adds tertiary notes such as leather, dried fruit and earth.

Common mistake: confusing the Montepulciano grape (Abruzzo) with Vino Nobile di Montepulciano (a Tuscan town, Sangiovese). Another classic trap: thinking Barbera is tannic. Barbera is high in acidity but low in tannin; Nebbiolo is high in both.

Sample short written answer: Amarone della Valpolicella

Exam-style question: explain how Amarone is made and how the process affects its style. A strong answer:

  1. Healthy bunches of Corvina (with other local grapes) are picked and laid out to dry for several months in well-ventilated lofts.
  2. The grapes lose water, so sugar, acids, flavour compounds and tannins become concentrated.
  3. The must is fermented to dryness, slowly because of the high sugar, giving high alcohol and full body.
  4. The wine is aged, typically in oak casks, which softens tannins and adds complexity.
  5. Result: a dry, full-bodied red with high alcohol, high tannin and acidity balanced by concentrated dried cherry, raisin and spice flavours, able to age for many years.

  2. Day 2: Nebbiolo vs Barbera vs Dolcetto: colour, acidity, tannin, body in a three-column table.
  3. Day 3: Valpolicella family: basic, Classico, Superiore, Ripasso, Amarone, Recioto, in order of richness.
  4. Day 4: Tuscany table: Chianti, Chianti Classico, Brunello, Vino Nobile, Bolgheri, with grape rules.
  5. Day 5: the south: match six wines to their grapes until perfect.
  6. Day 6: write the Barolo, Barbaresco and Brunello ageing rules from memory.

Frequently asked questions

Which Italian regions are most important for WSET 3?

Prioritise Piedmont (Nebbiolo, Barbera, Dolcetto, Gavi), Veneto (Soave, Valpolicella, Amarone), Tuscany (Chianti, Brunello, Vino Nobile, Bolgheri) and the south (Campania, Puglia, Sicily). Then add Alto Adige, Friuli, Marche, Abruzzo and Sardinia with one key grape each. Always connect each wine to its climate and style.

What is the difference between Valpolicella Ripasso and Amarone?

Amarone is made from grapes dried for months before fermentation, giving a dry, full-bodied, high-alcohol red with dried fruit. Ripasso is ordinary Valpolicella refermented on the pomace left after making Amarone, which adds body, alcohol, tannin and some dried fruit character, but less than Amarone itself.

How long must Barolo and Barbaresco be aged?

Barolo DOCG needs at least 38 months of ageing, including 18 months in wood, and Barolo Riserva at least 62 months. Barbaresco DOCG needs at least 26 months, including 9 months in wood, and Barbaresco Riserva at least 50 months. These figures are common WSET 3 multiple-choice questions.

What is a Super Tuscan for WSET Level 3?

A Super Tuscan is a high-quality Tuscan red made outside traditional appellation rules, historically sold as a basic category and now usually as IGT Toscana. Many use Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot or Syrah, alone or blended with Sangiovese, often aged in new French oak. Bolgheri on the coast is closely linked to this style.

What does Classico mean on an Italian wine label?

Classico indicates that the wine comes from the historic, original zone of the appellation, usually its hilly heart with the best-established vineyards. Examples include Chianti Classico, Soave Classico and Valpolicella Classico. It is a geographical term and not, by itself, a guarantee of longer ageing.
