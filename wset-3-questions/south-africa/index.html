Study notes: South Africa for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 South Africa questions test four things: why the Western Cape can make fresh wines despite its warm latitude, which grapes are planted where, how the Wine of Origin system works, and what the key regions such as Stellenbosch, Swartland, Constantia, Elgin and Walker Bay produce. These revision notes follow the cause, effect and style logic that examiners reward in the short written answers, so you can explain a South African wine rather than simply recall it.

Climate: why the Western Cape is cooler than its latitude suggests

The Western Cape has a warm Mediterranean climate: hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. On latitude alone it would be too hot for many quality wines, but three factors cool it down. First, the cold Benguela Current flows up the west coast from Antarctica, chilling the air and bringing fog and sea breezes. Second, the Cape Doctor, a strong south-easterly wind that blows in summer, lowers temperatures and dries the canopy, which reduces fungal disease; on exposed sites it can also damage vines and reduce yields. Third, mountains provide altitude and a wide variety of aspects, so a producer can choose a cooler, south-facing slope (in the southern hemisphere, south-facing means less direct sun) or a warmer, north-facing one.

Rainfall falls mainly in winter, so summers are dry and disease pressure is generally low. Moving inland, away from the ocean, temperatures rise and rainfall drops sharply; in areas such as the Breede River Valley, irrigation is essential. A typical exam sentence is: the coastal areas are cooled by the Benguela Current and the Cape Doctor, so grapes ripen more slowly and keep higher acidity, giving fresher wines with more pronounced fruit.

Viticulture issues worth one line in an answer

  • Old bush vines: many old, low-yielding bush vines survive, especially Chenin Blanc in the Swartland; they are often dry-farmed and give concentrated wines.
  • Irrigation: widely used in hot inland areas to achieve high yields for inexpensive wines and for brandy production.
  • Vine viruses, especially leafroll, are a significant problem because they delay ripening; replanting with virus-free material is costly.
  • Mechanisation is limited on steep mountain slopes, where hand-harvesting raises costs.

Wine of Origin: South African labelling for WSET 3

South Africa's Wine of Origin (WO) scheme defines where grapes are grown, in a hierarchy of increasingly small units. The largest is the geographical unit, such as the Western Cape. It is divided into regions, then districts, then wards. Most quality wine comes from the Western Cape geographical unit. The WO scheme does not set rules on grape varieties, yields or winemaking in the way a European appellation does, so producers are free to plant what suits the site.

WO levelExampleWhat to remember
Geographical unitWestern CapeSource of almost all premium wine
RegionCoastal Region, Breede River Valley, Cape South CoastThe three key regions for the exam
DistrictStellenbosch, Paarl, Swartland, Robertson, Elgin, Walker BayMost labels you will meet name a district
WardConstantia, Elim, the Hemel-en-Aarde wardsSmallest unit, often a specific cool site

For labelling, a wine that states a single grape variety, a single vintage or a single WO origin must contain at least 85% of that variety, vintage or origin. Blends can name more than one variety. This 85% rule is the same figure used in many New World countries, so it is an easy point to secure in a multiple-choice question.

WSET 3 South Africa grapes: key varieties and styles

White grapes

Chenin Blanc is South Africa's most planted grape variety. It is versatile: high-yielding irrigated vineyards make simple, fruity, sometimes off-dry wines, while old bush vines on poor soils, often in the Swartland, give concentrated wines with ripe stone fruit and tropical notes, sometimes fermented and aged in old oak for texture. Its naturally high acidity keeps even ripe wines balanced. Colombard is widely planted for inexpensive wines and brandy. Sauvignon Blanc does best in cool coastal areas such as Constantia, Durbanville, Elgin and Elim, where the wines are often between the herbaceous Loire style and the riper tropical New Zealand style. Chardonnay is grown in cooler areas such as Elgin and Walker Bay and on the limestone soils of Robertson, ranging from unoaked to barrel-fermented.

Black grapes

Cabernet Sauvignon is the most planted black grape, made as a varietal or in Bordeaux blends with Merlot and Cabernet Franc, especially in Stellenbosch. Syrah, often labelled Shiraz, is widely planted: warm sites give full-bodied wines with ripe black fruit and sometimes meaty notes, while cooler sites give lighter, peppery wines. Pinotage, the South African crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, is made in styles from light and fruity to full-bodied and oaked; some producers use heavily toasted oak for a coffee and chocolate character. Pinot Noir is limited to the coolest coastal sites such as the Hemel-en-Aarde wards and Elgin. Grenache, Mourvèdre and Cinsault are increasingly used in Rhône-style blends, notably in the Swartland.

Exam technique: when a question asks you to describe a South African style, always link the style to the site. 'Chenin Blanc from old, dry-farmed bush vines in the warm Swartland gives low yields, so the wine is concentrated, with ripe stone fruit and high acidity' scores far better than 'Chenin Blanc is fruity'.

South Africa wine regions for WSET Level 3

Coastal Region

Stellenbosch is the most famous district. Its mountain slopes give a range of altitudes and aspects, and breezes from False Bay cool the vineyards in the afternoon. It is best known for full-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux blends with ripe black fruit, firm tannins and oak influence, plus Pinotage, Cape blends and Chenin Blanc. Paarl lies further inland and is warmer, suited to fuller reds such as Shiraz and to Chenin Blanc. The Swartland is hot and dry; many vineyards are old, unirrigated bush vines, and a dynamic group of producers has made it famous for Chenin Blanc and Rhône-style reds and whites. Constantia, just south of Cape Town, is cooled by the ocean on two sides and is known for Sauvignon Blanc and for its historic sweet wine made from Muscat, which is not fortified. Durbanville and Darling, near the west coast, are cool areas for Sauvignon Blanc.

Breede River Valley

This inland region is hot and dry, so vineyards depend on irrigation from the Breede River. Worcester and Breedekloof produce large volumes of inexpensive wine and wine for brandy. Robertson is the exception for quality: its limestone soils suit Chardonnay, both still and as a base for traditional-method sparkling wine, and it also makes good-value reds.

Cape South Coast

The Cape South Coast is the coolest region, which explains its focus on Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc. Elgin is a high, cool district, historically known for apple orchards, producing elegant Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir with high acidity. Walker Bay, and in particular the Hemel-en-Aarde wards near Hermanus, is cooled by the ocean and is a leading source of Burgundian-style Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. Elim, near the southern tip of Africa, is windy and cool, giving very fresh Sauvignon Blanc.

Winemaking and the South African wine business

South Africa produces wine at every price level. Large cooperatives and producer-wholesalers make high-volume wines from irrigated inland vineyards, and a significant share of exports is shipped in bulk and bottled in the destination market. At the premium level, estates in Stellenbosch, Constantia, Elgin and Walker Bay use techniques found in any quality region: careful site selection, hand-harvesting, temperature-controlled fermentation and maturation in French oak. Traditional-method sparkling wines are labelled Cap Classique. Cape blends, which combine Pinotage with international varieties, give the country a point of difference on export markets.

Worked examples: South Africa short written answers

Question 1: Explain why Elgin is suited to Pinot Noir. Model answer: Elgin is a high-altitude district close to the ocean, so it is one of the coolest parts of the Cape South Coast. Cool temperatures slow ripening, which suits early-ripening, thin-skinned Pinot Noir: the grapes keep high acidity and develop delicate red fruit rather than jammy flavours. Ocean breezes and wind also keep the canopy dry, reducing the risk of rot to which tight-bunched Pinot Noir is prone. The result is an elegant, medium-bodied red with fresh acidity and red cherry and raspberry fruit.

Question 2: Explain why the Breede River Valley produces large volumes of inexpensive wine. Model answer: the region is inland, hot and dry, so vines rely on irrigation from the Breede River. With ample sunshine and water, yields are high and ripening is easy, and the flat land allows mechanisation, which lowers costs. Large cooperatives turn the grapes into fruity, simple wines and wine for brandy, much of it exported in bulk. Robertson, with its limestone soils, is the quality exception for Chardonnay and Cap Classique.

Where South Africa fits in the WSET Level 3 exam

The Level 3 award has two separate units. The theory unit pairs 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written-answer questions, and the tasting unit asks you to assess two wines blind. You need at least 55% in each unit; because they are marked independently, a candidate who fails one unit retakes only that unit. South Africa suits the theory paper well because its climate puzzle, cool wines from a warm latitude, is easy to examine with cause and effect. In tasting practice, a Swartland Chenin Blanc and a Stellenbosch Cabernet Sauvignon are useful benchmarks: one for high acidity with ripe fruit and texture, the other for firm tannin, body and oak.

Common mistakes in South Africa answers

  • Calling the Benguela a warm current: it is a cold current that cools the west coast.
  • Treating Pinotage as a Pinot Noir clone: it is a crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault.
  • Placing Robertson in the Coastal Region: it belongs to the Breede River Valley.
  • Writing that the Wine of Origin scheme controls grape varieties: like other New World systems, it defines origin only.

Exam technique: in a South African answer, name the specific cooling factor for the site you are discussing. False Bay breezes for Stellenbosch, the Benguela Current and fog for the west coast, altitude for Elgin and the ocean for Walker Bay. A precise cause earns the mark; a vague 'it is cool' rarely does.

Frequently asked questions

Is South Africa important for the WSET Level 3 exam?

Yes. The 50 multiple-choice questions cover the whole syllabus and the 4 short written answers often focus on one region or process, so every topic, including South Africa, can come up.

Which South African regions do I need to know for WSET 3?

Learn the three main regions of the Western Cape: the Coastal Region (Stellenbosch, Paarl, Swartland, Constantia, Durbanville), the Breede River Valley (Robertson, Worcester) and the Cape South Coast (Elgin, Walker Bay and its Hemel-en-Aarde wards, Elim). For each, be able to state the climate, the key grapes and the typical style, as short written answers often ask you to compare a warm and a cool area.

What does the Cape Doctor do to vines in South Africa?

The Cape Doctor is a strong south-easterly wind that blows in summer. It cools the vineyards and dries the canopy, so it reduces the risk of fungal disease, which is why it is called a doctor. On exposed sites it can also damage leaves and shoots and lower yields. In an exam answer, link it to slower ripening, retained acidity and healthy grapes.

What is the minimum percentage for a grape variety on a South African label?

If a South African label states a single grape variety, at least 85% of the wine must come from that variety. The same 85% minimum applies to a stated vintage and to a stated Wine of Origin unit. This figure is shared with many New World countries, so it is worth memorising as a quick multiple-choice point for WSET Level 3.

Why is Swartland Chenin Blanc so highly regarded at WSET Level 3?

The Swartland is hot and dry, and many of its Chenin Blanc vineyards are old bush vines grown without irrigation. These vines naturally give low yields of small, concentrated berries. Chenin's high natural acidity keeps the wines balanced despite the heat, and many producers ferment and age in old oak for texture without strong oak flavours, producing complex, age-worthy whites.

Is South African Pinotage always heavily oaked?

No. Pinotage is made in a range of styles. Some are light, fruity and unoaked, made for early drinking; others are full-bodied and aged in oak, sometimes with heavily toasted barrels that give coffee and chocolate notes. It is also blended with international varieties in a Cape blend. For WSET 3, describe the style according to the site and winemaking choices.

What is a Cape blend?

A Cape blend is a red blend that includes Pinotage, South Africa's own crossing, usually with Bordeaux varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot or with Syrah. The name is used by producers to give South African wine an identity on export markets. Some producers use the term more broadly for blends that include other Cape varieties such as Chenin Blanc, but for the exam link it with Pinotage.

What is Cap Classique?

Cap Classique, also called Méthode Cap Classique, is the South African term for sparkling wine made by the traditional method, with the second fermentation in the bottle in which the wine is sold. Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are the main grapes, with Chenin Blanc also used. Robertson, with its limestone soils, and cool coastal areas are important sources. The styles range from fresh and fruity to more autolytic, depending on time on the lees.

Why is Stellenbosch well suited to Cabernet Sauvignon?

Stellenbosch is warm enough for late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon to ripen fully, while afternoon breezes from False Bay and the altitude of its mountain slopes moderate the heat and preserve acidity. Well-drained, granite-derived soils on the slopes also limit vigour. The result is full-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux blends with ripe black fruit, firm tannins and the structure to age, often matured in French oak.

What should I notice when tasting Pinotage for WSET 3 practice?

Pinotage is usually deep in colour with red and black fruit and can show earthy or smoky notes. Styles vary widely: light, fruity versions have soft tannins, while premium examples are full-bodied, with firm tannins and noticeable oak, sometimes with coffee or chocolate notes from heavily toasted barrels. In the tasting unit you do not need to name the grape; focus on describing structure and oak precisely.
