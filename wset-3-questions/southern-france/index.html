Study notes: Southern France for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 Southern France questions focus on how a hot, dry Mediterranean climate shapes viticulture in the Languedoc, Roussillon and Provence, which grapes are blended in the main appellations, how IGP Pays d'Oc differs from AOC wines, and how pale Provence rosé and the fortified Vins Doux Naturels are made. These notes go beyond the sample questions above and give you the cause, effect and style reasoning needed for the short written answers.

The Mediterranean climate and what it means in the vineyard

Southern France has a Mediterranean climate: long, hot, sunny summers and mild winters, with most rain falling in autumn and winter. Grapes ripen easily, so the wines tend to have high alcohol, ripe fruit and soft acidity. The main challenge is summer drought, which can stress the vines and stop ripening. Strong, dry winds are the second key factor: the Tramontane blows between the Massif Central and the Pyrenees over the Languedoc and Roussillon, and the Mistral blows down the Rhône Valley into Provence. Both cool the vineyards and keep them dry, so fungal disease is less of a problem than in most of France. This is one reason organic viticulture is relatively widespread here.

Site matters enormously. The flat, fertile coastal plain is hot and produces high yields, historically used for large volumes of inexpensive wine. The foothills inland, towards the Massif Central and the Pyrenees, are higher and cooler, with poor, well-drained soils such as schist and limestone, which limit vigour and yields. That is why most quality appellations are on hillsides. A good exam chain is: altitude lowers temperatures, so ripening is slower and grapes keep more acidity and develop more complex flavours.

Typical vineyard practices

  • Bush vines (gobelet) are traditional because they shade the grapes from the sun and need no trellis; wind and dry conditions keep the canopy healthy.
  • Old vines, especially Carignan, give low yields and more concentrated wines.
  • Low yields on poor hillside soils are a key quality factor for AOC reds.
  • Harvesting at night or early in the morning is used for whites and rosés to keep grapes cool and preserve fresh fruit.

WSET 3 Southern France grapes: the Mediterranean blend

The red wines of Southern France are usually blends, because each grape brings something the others lack. Grenache gives high alcohol, red fruit and body but can lack colour and tannin. Syrah adds colour, tannin and black fruit. Mourvèdre needs a lot of heat, so it does best in the warmest sites, and gives deep colour, high tannin and meaty, gamey notes. Carignan gives high acidity, tannin and colour, and carbonic maceration is often used to soften it and bring out fruit. Cinsault is light in colour and tannin, with fresh red fruit, and is useful in rosé.

White varieties include Grenache Blanc, Marsanne, Roussanne, Viognier and Vermentino (called Rolle in Provence). International varieties such as Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Merlot and Cabernet Sauvignon are mostly found in IGP wines, especially IGP Pays d'Oc, where they are often labelled by grape variety.

Languedoc AOC and IGP Pays d'Oc for WSET Level 3

Languedoc wines come in two broad categories, and the difference is a classic WSET 3 Southern France question. IGP Pays d'Oc covers a very large area and allows many grape varieties, higher yields and varietal labelling, so producers can make fruity, good-value wines from international grapes for export markets. AOC wines have stricter rules: defined areas, lower yields and permitted blends of mainly Mediterranean grapes.

Key Languedoc appellations

  • AOC Languedoc: the regional appellation, covering both Languedoc and Roussillon; mostly red blends, plus rosé and white.
  • Corbières: a large, hilly area of varied soils; full-bodied red blends, often with old-vine Carignan.
  • Minervois: on the slopes of the Montagne Noire; red blends with ripe fruit.
  • Fitou: one of the oldest red appellations of the region, based on Carignan and Grenache.
  • Saint-Chinian and Faugères: hillside appellations with schist soils giving concentrated reds.
  • Pic Saint-Loup and Terrasses du Larzac: cooler, higher sites north of Montpellier, known for fresher, more structured reds.
  • Picpoul de Pinet: a white wine from Picpoul grown near the coast; high acidity, citrus, an ideal match for shellfish.
  • Limoux: cool, high area in the west; known for traditional-method sparkling wines (Crémant de Limoux, Blanquette de Limoux) and still Chardonnay.

Exam technique: when asked why a producer might choose IGP Pays d'Oc rather than an AOC, give two reasons with consequences. 'IGP rules allow international varieties and varietal labelling, so the wine is easier to sell to consumers who buy by grape; higher permitted yields lower costs, so the wine can be sold at a competitive price.'

Roussillon: dry reds and Vins Doux Naturels

Roussillon, next to the Spanish border, is the hottest and driest part of Southern France, swept by the Tramontane. Old Grenache vines on poor soils are central. Dry reds are sold as Côtes du Roussillon and Côtes du Roussillon Villages, the latter from a smaller area of better hillside sites; Collioure is a dry red appellation on steep coastal terraces. Roussillon is above all famous for Vins Doux Naturels (VDN): sweet wines fortified during fermentation.

How a Vin Doux Naturel is made

  1. Very ripe grapes with high sugar are harvested, mainly Grenache for reds and Muscat for whites.
  2. Fermentation starts, converting some sugar into alcohol.
  3. Grape spirit is added part-way through fermentation (mutage); the yeasts die and fermentation stops.
  4. Natural grape sugar remains, so the wine is sweet and has a high alcohol level.
  5. The wine is either protected from oxygen to keep fresh fruit, or aged oxidatively, sometimes in glass demijohns or barrels exposed to heat, to develop dried fruit, nut and caramel notes.

Banyuls and Maury are red VDN based on Grenache; Rivesaltes covers several styles, and Muscat de Rivesaltes is a white VDN with fresh grape and orange blossom aromas. In the Languedoc, Muscat de Frontignan is another well-known Muscat VDN.

Provence: the home of pale rosé

Provence, east of the Rhône, makes mostly rosé, and it is the region that defines the pale, dry, delicate style. The Mistral and the Mediterranean keep vineyards healthy; the climate is warm, with hills and inland plateaus providing cooler sites. The main appellation is Côtes de Provence, followed by Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence; IGP Méditerranée is also used. Grenache, Cinsault and Syrah are the main rosé grapes, with Mourvèdre, Tibouren and Rolle also important.

Pale rosé is made by picking early, often at night, to keep the grapes cool and the acidity fresh. The grapes are pressed directly, or after a very short skin contact, so little colour and tannin are extracted. The juice is fermented cool in stainless steel to preserve delicate red fruit and floral aromas, and the wine is released early for drinking young. Bandol is the exception to remember: a small coastal appellation where Mourvèdre dominates red wines that are full-bodied, tannic and aged in oak for a long period before release, with good ageing potential.

Common mistake: describing Southern France as one style. The exam rewards precision: hot coastal plain versus cool foothills, IGP versus AOC, dry reds versus Vins Doux Naturels, pale rosé versus Mourvèdre-based Bandol red.

Sample short written answer: the style of a Languedoc red blend

Question: Explain how the climate of the Languedoc and the choice of grape varieties shape the style of a typical red blend from the region. (Model answer, written as you would on the paper.)

The Languedoc has a warm Mediterranean climate with hot, dry, sunny summers and low rainfall during the growing season. Grapes ripen fully every year, so they accumulate high levels of sugar, which gives wines with high alcohol and full body, and the ripe black and red fruit typical of the region. Drought can be a problem, so old bush vines with deep roots are valued because they cope with water stress. Dry winds such as the Tramontane keep the vines healthy and reduce the risk of fungal disease, which also makes organic viticulture easier.

Most reds are blends because each variety brings something different. Grenache gives alcohol, body and red fruit; Syrah adds colour, black fruit and tannin; Mourvèdre brings tannin and savoury, gamey notes; Carignan, often from old vines and frequently made by carbonic maceration, adds colour and acidity while its tannins stay soft. Cinsault gives lightness and fruit, and is useful for rosé. The result is a medium- to full-bodied red with ripe fruit, medium tannins and often a herbal, garrigue note. Cooler sites at higher altitude, closer to the hills, give fresher wines with more acidity.

Notice the structure: climate, then its effect on the grape, then its effect on the wine, then the grape varieties and what each contributes. Every sentence links a cause to a result, which is what earns the points.

Southern France in the tasting unit

At Level 3 the theory paper and the tasting are two separate units. You need at least 55% in each, and if you fail one you retake only that unit, while the other stays valid. Southern French wines are useful practice for the tasting unit because they show clear warm-climate markers. A Grenache-based blend typically has a medium ruby colour, ripe red and black fruit, sometimes dried herbs, high alcohol and medium tannins. A Syrah-dominated wine is deeper in colour, with black fruit, pepper and firmer tannins. A Provence rosé is very pale pink, dry, with delicate red fruit and medium acidity.

When you assess these wines, link your conclusions to your description: high alcohol, ripe fruit and soft acidity support a warm-climate origin and good quality if the finish is long and the fruit is balanced. Avoid guessing the region; the tasting unit rewards an accurate note and a logical quality judgement, not a correct name.

Frequently asked questions

Is Southern France important for the WSET Level 3 exam?

Which Languedoc appellations should I know for WSET Level 3?

Learn AOC Languedoc as the regional umbrella, then Corbières, Minervois, Fitou, Saint-Chinian, Faugères, Pic Saint-Loup and Terrasses du Larzac for red blends, Picpoul de Pinet for a high-acid white, and Limoux for traditional-method sparkling wines and Chardonnay. For each, remember whether it is hillside or coastal, the dominant grapes and the resulting style.

What is a Vin Doux Naturel in WSET 3 Southern France?

A Vin Doux Naturel is a sweet wine fortified during fermentation. Grape spirit is added before all the sugar has fermented, which kills the yeast and leaves natural sweetness with a high alcohol level. Red examples such as Banyuls and Maury are based on Grenache; white examples such as Muscat de Rivesaltes are made from Muscat. Some are kept fresh and fruity, others aged oxidatively.

Why is Provence rosé so pale?

Provence rosé is pale because producers limit contact between juice and skins. Grapes are picked early and cool, then pressed directly or after only a very short maceration, so little colour is extracted. A cool fermentation in stainless steel keeps delicate red fruit and floral aromas, and the wine is bottled early to be drunk young and fresh.

What makes Bandol different from other Provence wines?

Bandol is a small coastal appellation in Provence best known for red wines, not rosé. Mourvèdre, which needs a lot of heat and sunshine, dominates the blend and ripens fully on warm, sheltered terraces. The wines are deep in colour, full-bodied and tannic, with meaty and earthy notes, and must spend a long period in oak before release, so they can age well.

Why is Carignan often made by carbonic maceration in the Languedoc?

Carignan naturally has high acidity, high tannin and deep colour, but can lack fruit and finesse. Carbonic maceration, or semi-carbonic maceration, extracts colour and fruit while keeping tannins soft, giving a juicier, more approachable wine. For quality wines, old Carignan vines on poor hillside soils give low yields and much more concentration, which is why old vines are valued.

How should I structure a written answer on Southern France for WSET 3?

Start with the climate, then explain its effect on ripening, then link it to alcohol, body and fruit. Add the grape varieties and what each one brings to the blend, then mention any key winemaking choice, such as carbonic maceration for Carignan or direct pressing for rosé. Short, linked sentences score better than lists of keywords, because each point must show the cause and the effect.

Do Southern French wines come up in the WSET 3 tasting unit?

They can, because they are good examples of warm-climate wines. In the tasting unit you are not asked to name the region: you describe the wine with the systematic approach and reach a logical conclusion on quality and readiness to drink. Practising with Grenache and Syrah blends helps you recognise high alcohol, ripe fruit and the structure of these wines.

What happens if I pass the WSET 3 theory but fail the tasting?

The theory and the tasting are separate units, and you need at least 55% in each. If you fail one, you retake only that unit, and the unit you passed remains valid. Your course provider can confirm the practical arrangements for booking the new attempt.

What is the difference between Côtes du Roussillon and Côtes du Roussillon Villages?

Both are dry wine appellations of Roussillon. Côtes du Roussillon covers a wide area and includes reds, whites and rosés. Côtes du Roussillon Villages is limited to red wines from the northern part of the region, with stricter rules, and generally gives more concentrated wines. Some villages can add their own name to the label.

Why are many Languedoc wines sold as IGP rather than AOC?

IGP Pays d'Oc gives producers more freedom: they can use international grapes such as Chardonnay, Merlot or Cabernet Sauvignon, label the wine by variety and work with higher yields. This suits wines aimed at a wide market and recognisable varietal styles, while the AOC rules favour traditional blends from defined areas.
