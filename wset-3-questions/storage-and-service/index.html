Study notes: wine storage and service for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 storage and service questions test whether you understand why each rule exists, not just the numbers. Every recommendation on storing, serving, opening, decanting and preserving wine comes back to the same few enemies: heat, temperature swings, light, dry air, oxygen and taint. If you can explain how each one damages a wine, you can answer almost any multiple-choice or short written question on this topic.

The six conditions for long-term wine storage

Good long-term storage means a cool, constant temperature (roughly 10–15 °C), darkness, moderate humidity, no vibration, no strong smells and, for bottles sealed with cork, a horizontal position. Constancy matters more than the exact figure: a cellar that sits steadily at 16 °C is safer than a room that swings between 8 and 20 °C, because every swing makes the wine expand and contract and can push air past the cork.

  • Heat speeds up chemical reactions, so the wine develops too fast and loses fresh fruit; extreme heat gives cooked, jammy flavours and can push the cork up.
  • Temperature fluctuation pumps air in and out of the bottle, leading to oxidation and seepage around the cork.
  • Light, especially strong sunlight and some artificial light, can cause light strike: unpleasant cabbage or wet-wool aromas. Clear and pale bottles, light whites and sparkling wines are most at risk.
  • Very dry air dries out corks from the outside; very damp air does not harm the wine but rots labels.
  • Vibration is widely believed to disturb sediment and ageing, so racks are kept away from machinery.
  • Strong odours (paint, cleaning products, food) are best avoided around stored wine, as cork is not a perfect barrier.

Exam technique: when a question asks why a place is unsuitable for storing wine (kitchen, garage, shop window, top of the fridge), name the specific enemy and its effect. 'The kitchen is too warm and its temperature fluctuates, which ages the wine prematurely and lets oxygen in' scores; 'it is a bad environment' does not.

WSET Level 3 service temperatures: the table to learn

Service temperature changes how a wine tastes: cold suppresses aroma and sweetness and makes tannin and bitterness more noticeable, while warmth releases aroma but makes alcohol stand out and can make a wine taste flabby. This is why tannic reds are served warmer and sweet or sparkling wines colder. Learn the bands below as a set, with one example per style.

StyleService temperatureTypical examples
Sweet winesWell chilled, 6–8 °CSauternes, sweet Loire Chenin, Tokaji
Sparkling winesWell chilled, 6–10 °CChampagne, Cava, Prosecco
Light- and medium-bodied whitesChilled, 7–10 °CMuscadet, Pinot Grigio, unoaked Sauvignon Blanc, Fino Sherry
Medium- and full-bodied oaked whitesLightly chilled, 10–13 °CWhite Burgundy, oaked Chardonnay, Fumé Blanc
Light-bodied redsLightly chilled, around 13 °CBeaujolais, light Pinot Noir, Valpolicella
Medium- and full-bodied redsRoom temperature, 15–18 °CBordeaux, Rioja, Barolo, Shiraz

Note that 'room temperature' means 15–18 °C, not the 22–24 °C of a modern heated room. A full-bodied red served too warm tastes hot and soupy; a quick 15 minutes in an ice bucket or fridge is the correct fix. Sparkling wine served too warm also loses its fizz faster and is harder to open safely.

Opening sparkling wine and decanting: step by step

How to open a bottle of sparkling wine safely

A sparkling wine bottle is under several atmospheres of pressure, so the cork can cause injury. The aim is a quiet release of gas with no spilled wine, not a loud pop.

  1. Make sure the wine is well chilled: cold wine holds its carbon dioxide better.
  2. Remove the foil and keep a thumb on the cork from the moment you loosen the wire cage.
  3. Tilt the bottle at about 45° and point it away from people and fragile objects.
  4. Hold the cork and cage firmly and turn the bottle, not the cork.
  5. Let the cork ease out slowly so the gas escapes with a gentle sigh.
  6. Pour in two stages into a clean glass, letting the foam settle before topping up.

Decanting: separating sediment and aerating

Decanting has two purposes. The first is to separate a wine from its sediment, typical of vintage Port, unfiltered wines and mature full-bodied reds. The second is to aerate a young wine so its aromas open up. For sediment, stand the bottle upright for a day or more so the deposit settles, open it without shaking, then pour slowly and steadily into the decanter with a light (a torch or candle) under the neck of the bottle. Stop as soon as the sediment reaches the neck. Very old, fragile wines should be decanted just before serving, because extended air contact can make them fade quickly.

Glassware, preservation and common faults at service

Choosing and preparing glasses

The ideal glass is clear, has a stem and a bowl that tapers towards the rim to concentrate aromas. Reds generally get larger bowls, whites medium ones, and sparkling wines tall flutes or tulips that show off the bubbles and slow their loss. Glasses must be perfectly clean: traces of detergent flatten the mousse of sparkling wine and add off-aromas, and glasses stored in cardboard boxes or cupboards can pick up musty smells. Rinsing in hot water and polishing with a clean, dry cloth is the standard routine.

Keeping wine once it is open

Once a bottle is open, oxygen starts to change it, so preservation methods all work by limiting contact with air. Vacuum pumps remove air from the bottle; inert gas systems lay a protective blanket of gas over the surface of the wine; special stoppers keep the pressure in sparkling wine. Refrigerating the open bottle, red or white, slows down oxidation. Systems that pour through the cork with a needle and replace the wine with inert gas allow wines to be served by the glass for weeks.

Faults to recognise before serving

Checking each bottle before it reaches the guest is part of professional service. The main faults are listed below; at Level 3 you should be able to name each one and describe its typical smell.

  • Cork taint (TCA): musty, damp cardboard aromas, with fruit muted even at low levels.
  • Oxidation: loss of fresh fruit, browning, flat or nutty and toffee notes in a wine not made in that style.
  • Heat damage: cooked or jammy fruit, sometimes a raised cork or leaking bottle.
  • Light strike: cabbage or wet-wool notes, mostly in clear-bottled whites and sparkling wines.
  • Reduction: rotten egg, struck match or cabbage-like aromas from sulfur compounds.
  • Excess volatile acidity: vinegar or nail-varnish remover aromas.

Common mistake: tartrate crystals and the deposit in an old red are not faults. They are harmless and natural; the correct answer to a guest is to explain this and, if needed, decant the wine.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions on Storage and service are in The Wine Goat?

Is Storage and service important for the WSET Level 3 exam?

Which service temperatures do I need to know for WSET 3?

You need six bands: sweet wines well chilled (6–8 °C), sparkling well chilled (6–10 °C), light- and medium-bodied whites chilled (7–10 °C), medium- and full-bodied oaked whites lightly chilled (10–13 °C), light-bodied reds lightly chilled (around 13 °C) and medium- and full-bodied reds at room temperature (15–18 °C). Learn one example wine for each band, because questions are often phrased with a named wine.

Why do tannic red wines need to be served warmer?

Cold makes tannins feel harsher and more bitter and suppresses the wine's aromas, so a full-bodied tannic red served too cold tastes hard and closed. At 15–18 °C the tannins feel smoother and the fruit is expressive. Too warm, however, and the alcohol becomes dominant and the wine seems flabby, so room temperature in a wine sense means cooler than a heated modern room.

How do you decant a wine with sediment for WSET Level 3?

Stand the bottle upright for at least a day so the sediment settles. Open it carefully without disturbing the deposit, then pour slowly and continuously into the decanter with a light source under the neck of the bottle. Stop pouring as soon as sediment reaches the neck. Old, fragile wines should be decanted just before serving to avoid fading.

What is the difference between a wine fault and a harmless deposit?

A fault, such as cork taint, oxidation, heat damage or light strike, changes aroma and flavour in an unpleasant way and is a reason to replace the bottle. Tartrate crystals in whites and sediment in mature reds or vintage Port are natural and do not affect quality. In service, a deposit is handled by decanting or careful pouring, not by sending the bottle back.

How do you keep an open bottle of wine fresh?

Limit oxygen and keep it cold. Vacuum pumps remove air from the bottle, inert gas systems cover the wine's surface with a protective layer, and pressure stoppers keep the bubbles in sparkling wine. Putting any open bottle, red or white, in the fridge slows oxidation. Needle-through-cork systems let you pour without removing the cork, so the wine is barely exposed to air.
