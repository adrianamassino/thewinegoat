Study notes: the WSET Level 3 tasting exam and the SAT

The WSET Level 3 tasting exam asks you to describe 2 wines blind using the Systematic Approach to Tasting (SAT) and then reach conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking. The SAT is a fixed structure: appearance, nose, palate, conclusions. Marks come from using the right scale term in each box and from giving enough precise aroma and flavour descriptors, so the skill to build is accuracy and consistency, not guessing the grape or region.

Why the SAT is marked the way it is

Each line of the SAT has a set list of terms. Examiners compare your term with the one agreed by a panel of tasters, so 'medium(+)' when the answer is 'medium(+)' scores, while a range or an invented word does not. This is why discipline matters: one clear term per line, aroma descriptors grouped by type, and conclusions that follow logically from what you have described.

Appearance and nose in the WSET 3 SAT

Appearance: clarity, intensity and colour

Appearance is quick but easy marks. Clarity is clear or hazy; a hazy wine may be faulty. Intensity runs pale, medium, deep. Colour depends on the wine type, and colour plus intensity together already hint at age and grape.

Wine typeSAT colour termsWhat it often suggests
Whitelemon-green, lemon, gold, amber, brownLemon-green: young, often cool climate; gold: oak, sweetness or age; amber/brown: oxidative or very old
Rosépink, salmon, orangePink: young and fruity; salmon or orange: some age or a deliberately pale, delicate style
Redpurple, ruby, garnet, tawny, brownPurple: very young; garnet/tawny: developing or fully developed; brown: old or oxidised

Other observations, such as legs, deposit, bubbles or a slight spritz, can be noted, but they carry no reliable information on quality on their own. Thick legs suggest higher alcohol or sugar, nothing more.

Nose: condition, intensity, aromas and development

On the nose you assess condition (clean or unclean), intensity (light, medium(-), medium, medium(+), pronounced), aroma characteristics and development. Development describes the stage of ageing: youthful, developing, fully developed or tired/past its best. A youthful wine shows only primary and secondary aromas; a developing wine starts to show tertiary notes alongside fresh fruit; a fully developed wine is led by tertiary aromas.

  • Primary aromas come from the grape and fermentation: floral, green fruit, citrus, stone fruit, tropical fruit, red fruit, black fruit, herbaceous, herbal, spice.
  • Secondary aromas come from winemaking after fermentation: yeast and lees (bread, biscuit, brioche), malolactic conversion (butter, cream) and oak (vanilla, toast, clove, smoke, coconut, cedar).
  • Tertiary aromas come from ageing: oxidative notes (nuts, toffee, coffee), dried fruit, and bottle-age notes such as petrol and honey in whites or leather, earth and forest floor in reds.

Exam technique: aim for several specific descriptors spread across clusters rather than one vague word. 'Lemon, green apple, white blossom, a hint of toast and butter' gives the examiner far more to reward than 'fruity'. Use the Lexicon on the back of the SAT card as a prompt, never as a list to copy.

The palate: structure scales you must master

On the palate the SAT looks at sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, mousse for sparkling wines, flavour intensity, flavour characteristics and finish. Structural components are the hardest part for most candidates, because they must be judged separately even though they interact: high acidity can make a wine seem less sweet, and high alcohol can make it feel fuller and slightly sweeter.

ComponentScaleHow to judge it
Sweetnessdry, off-dry, medium-dry, medium-sweet, sweet, lusciousTip of the tongue; do not confuse ripe fruit with sugar
Aciditylow, medium(-), medium, medium(+), highHow much and how long your mouth waters
Tanninlow, medium(-), medium, medium(+), highDrying and grip on gums and cheeks; also note texture (ripe, soft, coarse, green)
Alcohol (still wines)low (below 11%), medium (11–13.9%), high (14% and above)Warmth at the back of the throat
Bodylight, medium(-), medium, medium(+), fullOverall weight of the wine: alcohol, sugar, extract and tannin combined
Finishshort, medium(-), medium, medium(+), longHow long the pleasant flavours last after swallowing or spitting, not the bitterness or burn

Flavour characteristics on the palate usually confirm the nose, but the palate can reveal flavours that were hidden, especially oak, dried fruit or savoury notes. Repeat the most relevant descriptors from the nose and add any new ones.

Conclusions: quality level and readiness for drinking

In the conclusions you choose a quality level (faulty, poor, acceptable, good, very good, outstanding) and justify it, then give readiness for drinking. Quality is justified with balance, length, intensity and complexity, often remembered as BLIC. A balanced wine has fruit, acidity, tannin, sweetness and alcohol in harmony; length means a long finish; intensity means pronounced aromas and flavours; complexity means many different flavours, ideally across primary, secondary and tertiary clusters.

  1. Check balance: does any element stick out (harsh tannin, burning alcohol, sharp acidity)?
  2. Check the finish: short or medium(-) rarely supports 'very good'.
  3. Check intensity of aromas and flavours: light intensity usually limits the score.
  4. Check complexity: one simple fruit note is not complex; layers and evolution in the glass are.
  5. Choose the quality term that fits all four and write a short justification using them.
  6. Choose readiness for drinking: too young, can drink now but has potential for ageing, drink now and not suitable for ageing or further ageing, or too old.

Readiness follows from structure and concentration: a wine with high acidity or firm ripe tannin plus concentrated fruit can usually improve, while a light, fruity wine with soft structure is best drunk now. Make sure your readiness agrees with your development: a 'tired' nose cannot lead to 'potential for ageing'.

Common mistakes: writing a range ('medium to medium(+)'), using your own words in the conclusions, calling a wine 'outstanding' because it is expensive-looking, and forgetting to justify quality. Another frequent slip is a long finish noted for a wine you have also called simple and dilute: keep your note internally consistent.

How to train for the WSET 3 tasting exam in 15 minutes a day

Tasting is a skill that improves with regular, short practice far more than with occasional long sessions. You do not need a new bottle every day: one bottle can serve several days with a preservation stopper, and structure training can be done with simple kitchen props.

  1. Write a full SAT note on one wine, timing yourself, then compare it with a classmate or a reference note.
  2. Calibrate one structural element per day: compare two wines side by side for acidity, then tannin, then body.
  3. Smell and name kitchen references (lemon, apple, vanilla, clove, toast, butter) to build your aroma memory.
  4. Practise the alcohol scale by checking the label after you have committed to an answer.
  5. Revise the term lists from memory until every scale is automatic.
  6. Use the app's SAT questions to test terms, clusters and conclusions between tasting sessions.

Remember that the theory paper can also include questions on the SAT and on how winemaking choices show up in the glass, so linking a tasting term to its cause (oak gives vanilla and toast, malolactic conversion gives butter, bottle age gives petrol or leather) helps in both units.

Two units, two pass marks: what the tasting unit means for you

The tasting is a separate unit of WSET Level 3, not a bonus added to the theory paper. You need at least 55% in the tasting unit on its own and at least 55% in the theory unit on its own. A brilliant theory paper cannot rescue a weak tasting, and the reverse is equally true. If you fail the tasting unit, you retake only the tasting; the theory unit you passed stays valid. This has a practical consequence for your preparation: tasting practice must start early and run through the whole course, because a palate cannot be crammed in the last week the way a list of appellations sometimes can.

The two wines are assessed independently, so each note has to stand on its own. Treat them as two small exams: complete the first note fully, then move to the second with a fresh mind, and divide your time evenly rather than polishing one note at the expense of the other.

A worked example in prose: from glass to conclusion

The method is easiest to understand on a hypothetical wine. Imagine a young, unoaked white from a cool region. Looking at it, you note that it is clear, pale in intensity and lemon-green in colour. On the nose it is clean, with medium(+) intensity, and you find lime, green apple, white blossom and a wet-stone note, with nothing from oak or lees, so it is youthful. On the palate it is dry, the acidity is high, the alcohol feels medium, the body is medium(-), the flavour intensity is medium(+) and repeats the citrus and green fruit of the nose, and the finish is medium(+).

Now the conclusions. The acidity is high but the fruit is intense enough to carry it, so the wine is balanced. The finish and the intensity are both medium(+), and the flavours are mostly primary with a few distinct layers. With what is written, good is the consistent quality level, justified by balance, length and intensity while noting that complexity is limited; very good would need a longer finish or more layers of flavour. For readiness, the high acidity and concentrated fruit could support a little ageing, so can drink now, but has potential for ageing is defensible, provided the justification names the acidity and the concentration of fruit.

Notice what makes this note strong: every scale has one term, the descriptors come from several clusters, the palate confirms the nose, and every word of the conclusion points back to an observation written above it.

Sparkling, sweet and rosé wines in the SAT

Most exam wines are still wines, but the method works for every style and you should be ready for any of them. For a sparkling wine, the palate includes the mousse, described by its texture from delicate to creamy to aggressive, and notes of bread, brioche and biscuit from contact with dead yeast belong to the secondary cluster. For a sweet wine, place sweetness carefully on its scale and remember that high acidity can make a sweet wine taste less sweet than it is; honey, apricot and marmalade often point to noble rot and belong in the descriptors, not in the conclusions. For a rosé, colour is the first clue: a pale salmon wine usually comes from very short skin contact, while a deeper pink suggests longer maceration and more red fruit. In every case the conclusions still rest on balance, length, intensity and complexity.

Sample questions with answers

Question: a wine shows a garnet rim and, on the nose, dried cherry, leather and mushroom with only a little fresh red fruit. What is its development? Answer: it is fully developed or very close to it. Tertiary aromas now lead and the fresh fruit is fading; if the fresh fruit were still dominant alongside some tertiary notes, the correct term would be developing.

Question: a red wine has high tannin, high acidity and pronounced black fruit, but the finish is short and the alcohol burns. Can it be very good? Answer: no. The burning alcohol shows a lack of balance and the short finish limits length, so two of the four criteria are missing. Acceptable or good is the consistent conclusion, however concentrated the fruit seems.

Question: you find butter and cream in a white wine. Which cluster do they belong to and what do they suggest? Answer: they are secondary aromas from malolactic conversion. They point to a winemaking choice, often with Chardonnay, and should be listed separately from the primary fruit.

Before the exam, practise reading your note in reverse: start from the conclusion and check that every word is supported above. If the conclusion mentions a long finish, the finish line must say long; if it mentions complexity, the nose must show several clusters.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions on Tasting and the SAT are in The Wine Goat?

The app has 146 multiple-choice questions on Tasting and the SAT, each with an explanation, plus short written answer questions and flashcards on the same topic.

Is Tasting and the SAT important for the WSET Level 3 exam?

Yes. The 50 multiple-choice questions cover the whole syllabus and the 4 short written answers often focus on one region or process, so every topic, including Tasting and the SAT, can come up.

How many aroma descriptors should I write in the WSET 3 tasting exam?

There is no fixed number, but aim for several precise descriptors for the nose and for the palate, covering every relevant cluster: for example citrus, stone fruit and floral for primary, plus toast or butter if oak or malolactic conversion are present, and tertiary notes if the wine is developing. Specific words like 'blackcurrant' score better than general ones like 'black fruit'.

What are the alcohol levels on the WSET Level 3 SAT?

For still wines the SAT uses three levels: low (below 11% abv), medium (11–13.9%) and high (14% and above). Fortified wines are assessed on a different scale within the fortified range. In the exam you judge alcohol from the warmth at the back of the throat and the overall weight of the wine, since you will not see the label.

What does BLIC mean for WSET 3 tasting?

BLIC stands for balance, length, intensity and complexity, the four criteria used to justify the quality level in the SAT conclusions. A wine that is balanced, has a long finish, pronounced flavour intensity and several layers of flavour can be very good or outstanding; a wine weak on several of them is likely acceptable or good. Write your quality justification using these words.

What is the difference between developing and fully developed in the SAT?

Development describes how far the wine has aged. A developing wine shows mainly fresh primary fruit with the first tertiary notes, such as a hint of leather or dried fruit. A fully developed wine is dominated by tertiary aromas and has lost most of its fresh fruit. Your readiness conclusion should agree: a developing wine often still has potential for ageing.

Do I need to identify the grape and region in the WSET 3 tasting exam?

No. The Level 3 tasting exam rewards an accurate description and sound conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking, not the identification of grape, region or vintage. Thinking about likely origins can help you check that your note is consistent, but marks come from the correct SAT terms and descriptors, so never let a guess distort what you actually taste.

Is the WSET 3 tasting a separate unit from the theory?

Yes. Level 3 has two units marked independently: theory and tasting. You need at least 55% in each unit, and a high score in one does not compensate for a low score in the other. If you fail only the tasting unit, you retake the tasting on its own and your theory pass remains valid. For this reason, tasting practice should start at the beginning of your preparation, not in the final weeks.

How should I split my time between the two wines in the WSET 3 tasting?

Divide the time evenly and treat each wine as a separate exam. Write appearance and nose quickly, spend most of your effort on the structural levels of the palate, then keep a few minutes for the conclusions and a consistency check. Your school confirms the exact time allowed. Practise at home with a timer so that a full note for one wine feels routine well before exam day.

Why does my SAT note lose marks even when the descriptors are right?

Most marks are lost on structure and conclusions rather than on aromas. Common reasons are structural levels one step too high or too low, a quality level that is not justified with balance, length, intensity and complexity, and a readiness for drinking that contradicts the development noted on the nose. Check that each scale has one term and that the conclusion follows from everything you wrote above it.

How do I describe a sparkling wine with the WSET 3 SAT?

Use the same structure and add the mousse on the palate, describing its texture from delicate to aggressive. Bread, brioche, biscuit and toast from contact with dead yeast belong to the secondary cluster. Acidity is usually high in traditional-method wines, so judge it carefully, and do not let the bubbles mislead you about body. The conclusions still rest on balance, length, intensity and complexity.

What is the difference between fruitiness and sweetness in the SAT?

Sweetness is the amount of sugar you perceive, mainly on the tip of the tongue. Fruitiness is an aroma and flavour impression: very ripe or tropical fruit can make a dry wine seem sweet. To separate them, focus on texture and the tip of the tongue, and remember that high acidity hides sugar while high alcohol can give a slight impression of sweetness. Most exam wines are dry.
