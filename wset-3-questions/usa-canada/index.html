Study notes: USA and Canada for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 USA questions focus on how ocean influence, altitude and latitude shape very different wines across California, Oregon, Washington and New York, while Canada questions centre on Ontario, British Columbia and icewine. The key to scoring is the same as everywhere in the syllabus: explain the climate cause, then the effect on the grapes, then the resulting style.

US wine law in one paragraph

An American Viticultural Area (AVA) is a defined geographical area only: unlike a European appellation, it sets no rules on grape varieties, yields or winemaking. Federal law sets minimum percentages for what the label claims, and individual states can be stricter. Wines outside any AVA can still name a state or a county.

California: climate, regions and grapes

California has a Mediterranean climate with warm, dry, sunny summers, so ripeness is rarely a problem; the challenge is keeping freshness. The main cooling influence is the cold Pacific Ocean: cold currents create morning fog and breezes that are drawn inland through gaps in the coastal hills and valleys running at right angles to the coast. The further a site is from these influences, the hotter it is. Altitude also cools, and mountain vineyards above the fog line get intense sunshine but cooler nights.

North Coast: Napa, Sonoma and Carneros

  • Napa Valley: gets warmer from south to north. Los Carneros, at the southern end near San Pablo Bay, is cool and suits Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and sparkling wine base. The valley floor AVAs such as Oakville, Rutherford and Stags Leap District are famous for ripe, full-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon blends; Calistoga in the north is warm. Mountain AVAs such as Howell Mountain and Mount Veeder give more structured, tannic Cabernet.
  • Sonoma County: Russian River Valley is cooled by fog and makes elegant Chardonnay and Pinot Noir; the Sonoma Coast is cooler still. Alexander Valley is warmer and known for Cabernet Sauvignon, and Dry Creek Valley for Zinfandel.
  • Mendocino and Lake County, further north, offer good-value wines, and the cool Anderson Valley is known for Pinot Noir and sparkling wine.

Central Coast and Central Valley

The Central Coast stretches south of San Francisco. Monterey is cooled by the ocean funnelled up the Salinas Valley. Paso Robles is warm, particularly on its eastern side, and produces ripe Cabernet Sauvignon, Zinfandel and Rhône varieties; the western hills are cooler. In Santa Barbara County the valleys run east to west, so cold air and fog flow far inland: Santa Maria Valley and Sta. Rita Hills are known for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay with bright acidity. The Central Valley, sheltered from the ocean, is hot and fertile, relies on irrigation and produces large volumes of inexpensive wine; Lodi, cooled slightly by the delta breezes, is known for old-vine Zinfandel.

Key Californian grapes and styles

  • Cabernet Sauvignon: the most planted black grape; premium examples are full-bodied with ripe black fruit, high alcohol, ripe tannins and new oak.
  • Chardonnay: the most planted white; styles range from ripe, tropical, buttery and oaky to leaner, fresher wines from cool coastal sites.
  • Zinfandel: ripens unevenly, so bunches can contain raisined and unripe berries together; it makes rich, high-alcohol reds with jammy red and black fruit, and the pale, medium-sweet rosé known as White Zinfandel.
  • Pinot Noir: from cool to moderate areas such as Russian River, Carneros, Sonoma Coast and Santa Barbara.
  • Merlot, Sauvignon Blanc (sometimes oaked and labelled Fumé Blanc), Syrah and Petite Sirah complete the picture.

Oregon, Washington and New York

Oregon: Willamette Valley Pinot Noir

Oregon's most important region, the Willamette Valley, lies between the Coast Range and the Cascades. The Coast Range gives some shelter, but the climate is cool and relatively wet, with a risk of rain at harvest, so vintage variation is significant. Pinot Noir is the flagship, typically light to medium-bodied with red cherry and raspberry fruit and high acidity, followed by Pinot Gris and Chardonnay. Southern Oregon is warmer and drier and grows a wider range of varieties.

Washington: Columbia Valley

Most Washington vineyards lie east of the Cascade Mountains, in the rain shadow, where the climate is semi-desert and continental: hot days, cool nights, cold winters and very low rainfall. Irrigation, mostly from the Columbia River and its tributaries, is essential. Northerly latitude gives long summer days, so grapes ripen fully while cool nights preserve acidity. Winter freeze is the main hazard. The sandy soils are hostile to phylloxera, so many vines are planted on their own roots. Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Chardonnay and Riesling are the key grapes; sub-regions include Yakima Valley, Walla Walla Valley (shared with Oregon), Red Mountain and Horse Heaven Hills.

New York: Finger Lakes

In New York State the Finger Lakes region is the one to know. The deep glacial lakes store heat, which moderates the cold continental climate, delays budburst until frost risk has passed and extends the autumn. Riesling is the leading quality grape, made in styles from dry to sweet with high acidity. Hybrid and native American varieties are also widely grown.

Canada: Ontario, British Columbia and icewine

Canadian quality wine is regulated by the VQA (Vintners Quality Alliance) in Ontario and British Columbia. Both provinces have cool climates with cold winters, so site selection near large bodies of water is crucial.

  • Ontario: the Niagara Peninsula is the main region. Lake Ontario moderates temperatures and the Niagara Escarpment helps circulate air, reducing frost risk. Riesling, Chardonnay, Cabernet Franc and Pinot Noir are important, along with the hybrid Vidal for icewine. Prince Edward County and Lake Erie North Shore are other regions.
  • British Columbia: the Okanagan Valley is the main region. Its southern end is very dry and warm in summer, almost desert-like, and suits Merlot and other Bordeaux varieties; the cooler north suits aromatic whites and Pinot Noir. Okanagan Lake moderates temperatures. The Similkameen Valley lies nearby.

Icewine is made from grapes left on the vine into winter and harvested and pressed while frozen, at -8 °C or colder under Canadian rules. Much of the water stays behind as ice, so only a small amount of very sweet, concentrated juice is extracted. The result is a luscious wine with intense fruit balanced by high acidity, typically made from Vidal, Riesling or Cabernet Franc.

Common mistake: describing Washington as cool and rainy because it is in the Pacific Northwest. The vineyards are east of the Cascades, in a dry, continental rain shadow; it is Oregon's Willamette Valley that is cool and wet. Always name the mountain range and which side the vineyards are on.

Exam technique for short written answers: a question such as 'explain how the climate of Santa Barbara County affects Pinot Noir' wants the chain: east-west valleys channel cold Pacific air and fog inland, so temperatures stay moderate, the grapes ripen slowly and keep acidity, giving Pinot Noir with ripe red fruit and fresh acidity. Each link in the chain is usually a mark.

Worked examples: USA and Canada short written answers

The two questions below are written in the style of a Level 3 short written answer. Read each question, plan your own answer for two minutes, then compare it with the model answer, which shows the cause, effect and style chain that earns marks.

Question 1: Explain why Cabernet Sauvignon from the Napa Valley floor is usually fuller-bodied than Pinot Noir from Los Carneros. Model answer: Los Carneros sits at the southern end of the valley next to San Pablo Bay, so cool breezes and fog keep temperatures moderate. This suits early-ripening Pinot Noir, which keeps high acidity and fresh red fruit and gives a light to medium-bodied wine. Further north, valley floor AVAs such as Oakville and Rutherford are warmer, so late-ripening Cabernet Sauvignon reaches full ripeness: high sugar gives high alcohol, and the thick skins give deep colour and ripe, firm tannins. Premium producers add maturation in new French oak, which adds body, vanilla and toast. The contrast comes from both grape and site: a thin-skinned early ripener in a cool spot, a thick-skinned late ripener in a warm one.

Question 2: Explain how the Okanagan Valley can produce both Merlot and aromatic white wines. Model answer: the valley runs north to south over a long distance. The southern end is hot and very dry in summer, with long sunny days, so late-ripening black grapes such as Merlot ripen fully and give ripe fruit and soft tannins. The northern end is cooler, so aromatic varieties ripen slowly and keep high acidity and fresh, intense fruit. Okanagan Lake moderates temperatures near the water, reducing frost and winter damage, and irrigation makes up for the low summer rainfall in the south.

Where USA and Canada fit in the WSET Level 3 exam

Level 3 is made of two separate units. The theory unit combines 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written-answer questions, and the tasting unit asks you to assess two wines blind. You need at least 55% in each unit, and they are marked independently: if you pass one and fail the other, you retake only the failed unit. USA and Canada can matter in both. In theory, expect label rules, climate explanations and region-grape pairings. In tasting, a warm-climate Cabernet Sauvignon or an oaked Chardonnay is a useful benchmark to practise on: describe exactly what you find, such as high alcohol, pronounced ripe fruit and toasty oak, and use those observations to justify your conclusion on quality.

Common mistakes in USA and Canada answers

  • Treating an AVA like a European appellation: it defines a place only, with no rules on grapes, yields or winemaking.
  • Saying Napa is cool everywhere: only the southern end near San Pablo Bay is cool; the valley warms towards Calistoga.
  • Forgetting that Zinfandel ripens unevenly, which is why harvest timing and sorting matter so much.
  • Writing that icewine grapes are affected by noble rot: the concentration comes from freezing, not from botrytis, so the fruit stays pure and the acidity high.
  • Placing the Finger Lakes in a mild climate: the region is cold and continental, and the lakes are what make viticulture possible.

Exam technique: in multiple-choice questions on unfamiliar AVA names, reason from geography. A site close to the ocean, the bay or a fog gap is cooler; a site further inland or sheltered by hills is warmer. That logic often eliminates two distractors at once.

What are the US wine labelling rules for WSET 3?

Under federal law a varietal label needs 75% of the named grape, an AVA needs 85% of grapes from that area, a state or county name needs 75%, and a vintage needs 85%, or 95% when an AVA is stated. States can be stricter: California requires 100% Californian grapes for a California label, and Oregon requires 90% of the named variety for most grapes.

Why is Washington State dry if it is in the Pacific Northwest?

Most Washington vineyards lie east of the Cascade Mountains, which block rain-bearing weather from the Pacific. The Columbia Valley is therefore in a rain shadow with a semi-desert, continental climate, hot summer days, cool nights and cold winters. Irrigation from the Columbia River is essential, and the long summer days at this latitude help grapes ripen fully.

How is Canadian icewine made?

Grapes are left on the vine into winter and picked and pressed while naturally frozen, at -8 °C or colder under Canadian rules. The water stays in the press as ice, so only a little highly concentrated, sugar-rich juice runs out. Fermentation is slow and stops with plenty of residual sugar, giving a luscious wine with intense fruit and high acidity, often from Vidal, Riesling or Cabernet Franc.

What does AVA mean on a US wine label?

AVA stands for American Viticultural Area, a legally defined grape-growing area based on geography such as climate, soil and topography. Unlike a European appellation, an AVA does not regulate grape varieties, yields or winemaking methods. If an AVA appears on the label, at least 85% of the grapes must come from it. AVAs can be very large, like Columbia Valley, or small and nested inside others, like Oakville within Napa Valley.

Which Californian regions are best for Chardonnay and Pinot Noir for WSET 3?

Look for cool, ocean-influenced areas: Los Carneros, Russian River Valley and the Sonoma Coast in the north, and Santa Maria Valley and Sta. Rita Hills in Santa Barbara County, where east-west valleys draw fog and cold air inland. Anderson Valley in Mendocino and parts of Monterey are also cool. These sites slow ripening and keep acidity, giving fresher, more elegant wines.

Do I need to know every Napa AVA for WSET Level 3?

No. Learn a handful that each illustrate a principle: Los Carneros for a cool, bay-influenced site, Oakville and Rutherford for the warm valley floor, Calistoga for the warm north and a mountain AVA such as Howell Mountain for altitude and firmer tannins. If you can explain why each one differs, you can handle unfamiliar names in a multiple-choice question by reasoning from their position in the valley.

Why are many Washington vines planted on their own roots?

The sandy soils of eastern Washington are hostile to phylloxera, so the pest has not spread widely and growers have been able to plant ungrafted vinifera vines. This avoids the cost of grafting but remains a risk if phylloxera spreads. A bigger practical threat in Washington is winter freeze, which can kill vines down to the ground; own-rooted vines can at least regrow from below the soil.

Can a wine from the USA or Canada appear in the WSET 3 tasting unit?

The tasting unit assesses how well you apply the tasting method, not your knowledge of one region, so any style covered by the course could be useful practice. You do not have to name the origin: you describe the wine accurately and reach a justified conclusion on quality and readiness for drinking. Practising on a Californian Cabernet or a Willamette Pinot Noir helps you calibrate body, alcohol and tannin.

What is the difference between icewine and late harvest wine?

Late harvest wines come from grapes left on the vine to ripen further and lose water, sometimes with noble rot. Icewine goes further: the grapes must freeze naturally on the vine, at -8 °C or colder under Canadian rules, and are pressed while frozen, so only concentrated juice runs off. Icewine is therefore sweeter and rarer, with intense fruit, high acidity and no botrytis character.
