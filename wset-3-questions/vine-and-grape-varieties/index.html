Study notes: the vine and grape varieties for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 questions on the vine and grape varieties test the biology behind every wine: which species make wine, how varieties, clones, crossings and hybrids differ, why vines are grafted onto rootstocks, how the vine is built and what each part of the grape gives to the finished wine. This topic underpins climate, vineyard management and winemaking, so understanding it makes the regional chapters far easier.

Species, varieties and clones: the key definitions

Almost all wine is made from Vitis vinifera, a European and Asian species. Within that species there are thousands of varieties, such as Chardonnay or Syrah. A clone is a vine, or group of vines, of one variety that shows particular traits, such as smaller berries, higher yield or better disease resistance, as a result of a natural mutation. Clones are still the same variety: a Pinot Noir clone is Pinot Noir. Some mutations go further and change berry colour: Pinot Gris and Pinot Blanc are colour mutations of Pinot Noir.

TermHow it is createdExample
VarietyA distinct, stable type within a species, propagated vegetativelyChardonnay, Syrah, Riesling
CloneSelected cuttings from a vine with a useful natural mutationPinot Noir clones chosen for small berries
CrossingSeedling from two parents of the same species (both vinifera)Müller-Thurgau, Pinotage; Cabernet Sauvignon is a natural crossing of Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc
HybridSeedling from parents of different species, e.g. vinifera and an American speciesVidal, Seyval Blanc
RootstockOften an American species or a cross between American species, used for its rootsRootstocks bred from Vitis riparia, rupestris and berlandieri

Common mistake: confusing a crossing with a hybrid. A crossing has two Vitis vinifera parents; a hybrid has parents from two different species. Hybrids are valued for cold hardiness and disease resistance and matter in places such as Canada and New York, where Vidal is used for icewine.

Propagation, grafting and rootstocks

Why vines are not grown from seed

A grape seed is the product of sexual reproduction, so the seedling is genetically new and different from its parent, even if both parents are the same variety. To reproduce a variety exactly, growers use vegetative propagation. Cuttings are pieces of one-year-old cane planted to root, or more often grafted; layering bends a cane from an existing vine into the soil so it roots next to the parent. Layering puts vinifera roots straight into the ground, exposing them to phylloxera, so cuttings grafted onto rootstocks are now the norm.

Phylloxera and the role of American rootstocks

Phylloxera is a tiny aphid-like insect native to North America that feeds on vine roots. Vitis vinifera has no resistance: the wounds become infected and the vine slowly dies. When phylloxera reached Europe in the second half of the nineteenth century, it destroyed most vineyards. The solution was to graft vinifera onto rootstocks from American species, which evolved alongside the pest and resist it. American species themselves make wines with unappealing flavours, so they are used only below ground. A few areas remain largely free of phylloxera, notably Chile and parts of Australia, and sandy soils are hostile to the insect, so some vines there grow on their own roots.

How growers choose a rootstock

Rootstocks are chosen for much more than phylloxera resistance. The right choice depends on the site and the style the grower wants.

  • Resistance to pests in the soil, such as phylloxera and nematodes.
  • Vigour: low-vigour rootstocks suit fertile soils and help control canopy growth; high-vigour rootstocks suit poor soils.
  • Drought tolerance for dry sites without irrigation.
  • Tolerance of lime-rich soils, such as chalk and limestone, which can cause nutrient problems.
  • Tolerance of salinity or wet, poorly drained soils.
  • Influence on how early or late the vine develops and ripens.

Grafting can be done in a nursery before planting (bench grafting) or in the vineyard. Top grafting, also called head grafting, places buds of a new variety onto an established vine's trunk, letting growers change variety quickly without replanting and losing years of production.

Structure of the vine and the grape berry

Parts of the vine and their functions

  • Roots: anchor the vine, absorb water and nutrients and store carbohydrates over winter.
  • Trunk and arms or cordons: the permanent wood that carries water and sugars and supports the canopy; it also stores carbohydrates.
  • Shoots: the green growth of the season, carrying leaves, buds, tendrils and flower clusters; when they turn woody in autumn they become canes.
  • Buds: contain next season's shoots; pruning decides how many are kept, which controls yield.
  • Leaves: carry out photosynthesis, producing the sugars that ripen the grapes.
  • Tendrils: help the vine climb by gripping supports.

What each part of the grape gives to the wine

The grape berry is the raw material of the wine, and each part contributes different components. Understanding this explains many winemaking choices: white wines are separated from their skins early, red wines are fermented with them to extract colour and tannin.

Part of the berryMain componentsEffect in the wine
PulpWater, sugars (glucose and fructose), acids (mainly tartaric and malic), some aroma precursorsSugar becomes alcohol; acids give freshness
SkinColour pigments in black grapes, tannins, aroma and flavour compounds, yeasts on the surfaceColour, structure and much of the varietal aroma
SeedsHarsh, bitter tannins and oilsMust not be crushed, or the wine becomes bitter and astringent
StemsTanninsAdd structure and fresh, herbal notes when whole bunches are used; harsh if unripe

Grape ripening: from veraison to harvest

Grape ripening begins at veraison, when berries soften and black grapes change colour. From then on sugars accumulate, acidity falls, colour deepens, tannins ripen and aromas change from green and herbaceous to riper fruit. Malic acid is broken down faster in warm conditions, which is why grapes from hot climates have lower acidity, while tartaric acid stays more stable. A grower picks when the balance of sugar, acidity, tannin ripeness and flavour suits the style of wine being made.

  1. Budburst: buds open in spring; young shoots are vulnerable to frost.
  2. Flowering and fruit set: flowers are pollinated and become berries; cool, wet or windy weather can cause poor fruit set (coulure) or uneven berry size (millerandage).
  3. Veraison: berries soften and change colour.
  4. Ripening: sugar rises, acid falls, tannins and aromas mature.
  5. Harvest: timing is chosen for the target style, from early picking for sparkling base wine to late picking for sweet wines.
  6. Dormancy: after leaf fall the vine rests over winter and is pruned.

Exam technique: in written answers, link grape biology to wine style. 'Thick-skinned varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon give deeply coloured, high-tannin wines because colour and tannin come from the skins' is the kind of cause-and-effect sentence that earns marks.

What is the difference between a crossing and a hybrid for WSET 3?

A crossing is a new variety grown from seed with two parents of the same species, usually both Vitis vinifera, such as Müller-Thurgau or Pinotage. A hybrid comes from parents of different species, typically vinifera and an American species, such as Vidal or Seyval Blanc. Hybrids are bred for cold hardiness and disease resistance and are important in cold regions like Canada and New York.

Why are European vines grafted onto American rootstocks?

Vitis vinifera roots have no resistance to phylloxera, a root-feeding insect from North America that kills the vine. American vine species evolved with the pest and resist it, so vinifera is grafted onto American rootstocks: the roots resist phylloxera, while the part above ground keeps the variety's fruit quality. Rootstocks are also chosen for vigour, drought tolerance, lime tolerance and resistance to nematodes.

What does each part of the grape contribute to wine?

The pulp provides water, sugars that become alcohol, and acids, mainly tartaric and malic, that give freshness. The skins provide colour in black grapes, tannins and most aroma and flavour compounds. The seeds contain bitter tannins and oils, so they must not be crushed. Stems add tannin and herbal notes when whole bunches are fermented.

What is a clone in viticulture?

A clone is a vine, or group of vines, of a single variety selected because a natural mutation gave it particular traits, such as small berries, higher yield, earlier ripening or disease resistance. It is propagated by cuttings so the traits are kept. A clone remains the same variety, so different Chardonnay clones are all Chardonnay; the choice of clone is one of many quality decisions a grower makes.

What happens to grapes during ripening?

From veraison, grapes soften and black varieties gain colour. Sugar levels rise, acidity falls as malic acid is broken down, especially in warm climates, tannins become riper and less astringent, and aromas move from green and herbaceous to riper fruit. Growers choose the harvest date by balancing sugar, acidity, tannin ripeness and flavour according to the wine style they want.
