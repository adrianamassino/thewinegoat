Study notes: white winemaking for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 white winemaking questions revolve around one decision: does the winemaker want to protect fresh, primary fruit, or build texture and complexity? Almost every step, from how the grapes are pressed to whether malolactic conversion is allowed, follows from that choice. These notes walk through the process in order, then cover sweet white wines and the four main style families, so you can explain each technique, its effect and the style it produces.

From vineyard to press: protecting the juice

Harvest and reception

White grapes are vulnerable to oxidation and loss of aroma once the skins break, so many producers pick at night or early in the morning and bring the fruit to the winery quickly and cool. For aromatic varieties the winemaker may use sulfur dioxide and inert gases such as carbon dioxide or nitrogen to keep oxygen away from the juice. This protective, or reductive, handling keeps fresh citrus, green fruit and floral notes.

Crushing, skin contact and pressing

Most white grapes are crushed and pressed soon after arrival because the skins contribute little that is wanted and extended contact can extract bitter phenolics. Whole-bunch pressing skips crushing: the stems create channels for the juice, the pressing is gentle and the result is a purer, lower-phenolic juice, widely used for premium whites and sparkling base wines. Pneumatic presses inflate a membrane slowly and are favoured because they are gentle and can work in an inert atmosphere. The first juice to run, the free-run juice, is the finest; later press fractions are richer in phenolics and may be kept apart.

Exam technique: if a question asks why a winemaker uses short skin contact for a white wine, the answer is to increase flavour intensity and texture in aromatic varieties, usually for a few hours at a cool temperature so that fermentation does not start. Do not say it is for colour or tannin.

Juice clarification

Before fermentation the juice is usually clarified to remove solids. The simplest method is settling: the juice is chilled and left for the solids to fall to the bottom, then the clear juice is racked off. Centrifugation and flotation are faster options used by large wineries. Very clear juice gives clean, fruity wines; leaving some solids can add complexity and help a natural fermentation, at a higher risk of off-flavours.

Fermentation choices for white wines

White wine fermentations usually run between 12 and 22 °C. Cool temperatures preserve delicate primary aromas, while the warmer end of the range gives fuller, more complex wines with less overt fruit. The fermentation vessel is the other big decision.

VesselEffect on the wineTypical examples
Stainless steelInert, easy to cool precisely; keeps primary fruit pureRiesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, many inexpensive whites
Oak barrelSome oxygen, integrates oak flavours, adds texture; harder to coolPremium Chardonnay, white Bordeaux
Concrete or clayMostly neutral flavour; stable temperature, some oxygen exchange if unlinedProducers seeking texture without oak flavour

Barrel fermentation is often preferred to only maturing a wine in oak, because oak flavours integrate better when the wine is fermented in the barrel. Small barrels in a cool cellar lose heat easily, which is why barrel fermentations can run warmer without overheating. Winemakers may use selected cultured yeasts for reliability and aroma, or ambient yeasts for complexity, accepting slower and less predictable fermentations.

After fermentation: malolactic, lees and oak

Malolactic conversion: yes or no?

Malolactic conversion (MLF) turns sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid, raising pH slightly and producing diacetyl, which smells of butter. For crisp aromatic whites such as Riesling or Sauvignon Blanc, winemakers usually block MLF by keeping the wine cool, adding sulfur dioxide or removing bacteria by filtration, so the wine keeps its tangy acidity and pure fruit. For Chardonnay, full or partial MLF is common, giving a rounder texture and buttery notes. Winemakers can blend batches with and without MLF to fine-tune the style.

Lees contact and lees stirring

Lees are dead yeast cells that settle after fermentation. Leaving the wine on the fine lees adds a creamy texture and savoury, bready or biscuit notes, and lees also absorb oxygen, giving some protection. Stirring the lees (bâtonnage) increases these effects and spreads them through the wine. Muscadet sur lie is a classic example of lees contact in a neutral, unoaked style; barrel-matured Chardonnay often combines lees stirring with oak.

Oak maturation

New oak gives vanilla, toast, smoke and spice; older barrels give little flavour but allow slow oxygen exchange that softens and adds complexity. Barrel size, toast level and the proportion of new oak are all style levers. A high proportion of new oak suits concentrated wines; it would overwhelm a delicate one.

Blending, clarification and stabilisation

For aromatic, primary-fruit whites, blending mainly keeps the wine consistent. For Chardonnay the reverse can apply: batches with different lees, MLF and oak treatments are blended for complexity. Before bottling, whites are fined and filtered for clarity, cold stabilised to prevent tartrate crystals forming in the bottle, and treated with bentonite to remove proteins that could make the wine hazy.

Sweet white wines: how residual sugar is achieved

Sweet white wines are a frequent WSET 3 short written answer topic. There are three routes to sweetness, and examiners expect you to name the method and its effect on style.

  1. Stopping fermentation before all the sugar is used: by chilling and removing the yeast with filtration or centrifugation, by adding sulfur dioxide, or by adding spirit (fortification, covered with fortified wines). German Riesling with low alcohol and some sweetness is often made by stopping fermentation.
  2. Adding a sweetening component after fermentation, such as unfermented grape juice (Süssreserve) or rectified concentrated grape must; common for inexpensive medium-sweet wines.
  3. Concentrating sugar in the grapes before fermentation: noble rot (Botrytis cinerea in humid mornings followed by dry afternoons, as in Sauternes, Tokaji and Beerenauslese), drying on the vine (late harvest), drying after picking (passito wines such as Recioto di Soave or Vin Santo) or freezing on the vine (Eiswein and icewine).

Grapes concentrated by noble rot give flavours of honey, marmalade and dried fruit with high acidity; ice wine keeps intense primary fruit and very high acidity because the grapes are not affected by rot. Fermentations of very sweet musts are slow and often stop naturally at moderate alcohol.

Common mistake: saying that oak barrels make a wine taste of oak in every case. Old barrels add little oak flavour; their main effect is slow oxygen exchange. Always state whether the barrels are new or old when you explain an oaked white.

Protective or oxidative: two philosophies for white wine

Behind every white winemaking decision sits one question: how much oxygen should the juice and wine see? Protective handling keeps oxygen away at every step, with cool fruit, sulfur dioxide, inert gas, stainless steel and full tanks. It preserves delicate primary aromas such as citrus, green fruit and flowers, and suits varieties whose appeal lies in their aroma. Oxidative handling lets controlled amounts of oxygen in, for example through barrel fermentation, lees stirring in barrel or deliberate exposure of the juice before fermentation. Fresh fruit becomes less obvious, but texture, nutty notes and complexity increase, and the wine can become more resistant to later oxidation in bottle. Most premium whites sit somewhere between the two extremes.

A sample written answer

Question: explain how winemaking choices can give a Chardonnay a full body and a complex flavour profile. Answer: fermenting in barrel at a slightly warmer temperature integrates oak flavour and encourages a rounder texture. Allowing malolactic conversion softens the acidity and adds buttery, creamy notes. Leaving the wine on its lees and stirring them adds body and bready, savoury flavours. Using a proportion of new oak adds vanilla, toast and spice, while slow oxygen exchange through the wood softens the wine and adds nutty complexity. Each sentence names one technique and one effect, which is exactly how a WSET 3 written answer collects marks.

Common mistakes: saying that all white wines avoid oxygen, forgetting that malolactic conversion lowers acidity as well as adding butter, and describing sweet wine methods without saying whether the sugar comes from concentrated grapes or from a stopped fermentation.

Why is malolactic conversion blocked in some white wines for WSET 3?

Winemakers block malolactic conversion in aromatic whites such as Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc to keep their sharp malic acidity and pure primary fruit. MLF would soften the acidity and add buttery notes that mask varietal aromas. It is prevented by keeping the wine cool, adding sulfur dioxide or removing lactic bacteria by filtration. Chardonnay, by contrast, often goes through full or partial MLF.

What does lees stirring do to a white wine for WSET Level 3?

Lees stirring, or bâtonnage, puts the dead yeast cells back into suspension. This increases the creamy, rounder texture and the savoury, bready or biscuit notes that lees contact gives, and the lees also absorb some oxygen. It is common for barrel-matured Chardonnay. Lees contact without stirring is used for neutral wines such as Muscadet sur lie.

How is noble rot sweet wine made for WSET 3?

Noble rot is Botrytis cinerea developing on ripe, healthy grapes in a climate with humid, misty mornings followed by dry, sunny afternoons. The fungus pierces the skins, water evaporates and sugar, acidity and flavour concentrate. Grapes are often picked by hand in several passes. The wines, such as Sauternes and Tokaji Aszú, show honey, marmalade and dried fruit with high acidity.

What is the difference between whole-bunch pressing and crushing white grapes for WSET Level 3?

Crushing breaks the berries before pressing, releasing juice quickly but also more phenolics. Whole-bunch pressing puts intact bunches straight into the press; the stems form drainage channels, the pressing is gentle and the juice is purer and lower in phenolics. It is slower and the press holds less fruit, so it is mostly used for premium whites and sparkling base wines.

Why are white wines cold stabilised before bottling?

Cold stabilisation chills the wine to make tartaric acid crystals form in the tank, where they can be removed, instead of later in a cold bottle. The crystals are harmless but look like glass to many consumers. It is part of the stabilisation stage, alongside bentonite fining to remove proteins that could cause haze. Some premium producers skip it and accept the occasional tartrate deposit.

What is the difference between protective and oxidative white winemaking?

Protective winemaking keeps oxygen away from the juice and wine with cool temperatures, sulfur dioxide, inert gas and stainless steel, which preserves fresh primary aromas. Oxidative winemaking lets controlled oxygen in, for example through barrel fermentation and lees stirring in barrel. It reduces obvious fresh fruit but adds texture, nutty notes and complexity. Many premium white wines combine elements of both approaches.

Why is Sauvignon Blanc usually fermented in stainless steel?

Its appeal lies in pronounced primary aromas such as citrus, gooseberry and herbaceous notes, which are easily lost through oxidation or masked by oak. Stainless steel is inert, airtight and easy to keep cool, so fermentation is slow and clean and the aromas are retained. Malolactic conversion is usually blocked for the same reason, to keep the crisp acidity that defines the style.

How do winemakers make an off-dry white wine?

The usual method is to stop fermentation before all the sugar has been converted, by chilling the wine and removing the yeast with filtration or centrifugation, sometimes helped by sulfur dioxide. The result keeps some natural grape sugar and has lower alcohol. Another method is to ferment to dryness and add sweet reserve, unfermented grape juice, before bottling, where local rules allow it.

Do all oaked white wines taste of vanilla and toast?

No. Vanilla, toast and spice come mainly from new or fairly new oak. Older barrels add little flavour; their main role is slow oxygen exchange, which softens the wine and adds complexity. The size of the barrel and the toast level also matter. When you describe an oaked white in a written answer, always say whether the oak is new or old and what effect it has.
