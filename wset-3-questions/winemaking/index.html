3 What does a pre-fermentation cold soak (4-10°C) mainly extract?

Show the answer

Answer: Colour and fruity aromas, with few tannins

Why: Without alcohol, anthocyanins and aromas dissolve, not tannins; protected with SO₂ and dry ice.

4 In carbonic maceration, what produces the first 2 degrees of alcohol inside the whole berries?

Show the answer

Answer: The grape's own enzymes (intracellular fermentation)

Why: Without oxygen, the berry's enzymes make ~2% abv; yeasts finish after pressing.

5 Where does the CO₂ in wine mainly come from?

Show the answer

Answer: From the yeasts' alcoholic fermentation

Why: Sugars → alcohol + CO₂ + heat; a little from malolactic too.

Show the answer

Answer: Antimicrobial and antioxidant

Why: SO₂ holds back unwanted microbes and protects against oxidation.

Study notes: winemaking and maturation for WSET Level 3

WSET Level 3 winemaking questions cover the techniques shared by every wine style: controlling oxygen and temperature, running the alcoholic fermentation, adjusting the must, maturing the wine, then clarifying, stabilising and packaging it. These study notes give you the general principles that sit behind the white, red, rosé, sparkling and fortified processes, explained as cause, effect and style so you can apply them to any wine in the exam.

The winemaker's toolkit: oxygen, temperature and hygiene

Oxygen is the winemaker's most important variable. Protective (reductive) winemaking keeps oxygen away with stainless steel, inert gases, full containers and sulfur dioxide, preserving fresh primary fruit. Oxidative winemaking deliberately allows some oxygen contact, for example in barrels, which softens tannins, changes colour and builds tertiary notes such as nut, toffee or leather. Too much oxygen, or oxygen at the wrong time, causes faults: loss of fruit, browning and acetaldehyde aromas.

Temperature control lets the winemaker steer fermentation speed and aroma. Hygiene matters because spoilage microbes such as acetic acid bacteria and Brettanomyces yeast thrive in dirty equipment and in wines with low sulfur dioxide or exposed to air. Sulfur dioxide is used throughout the process as an antioxidant and antimicrobial, but too much can mute aromas and is regulated by law.

Alcoholic fermentation in detail

Alcoholic fermentation is carried out by yeast, which converts sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide, releasing heat and producing many flavour compounds such as esters. Saccharomyces cerevisiae is the yeast that completes fermentation because it tolerates high alcohol. Fermentation usually stops when all the sugar has been used, when the yeast is killed by rising alcohol, or when the winemaker intervenes.

Ambient yeast or cultured yeast?

Ambient (natural or indigenous) yeasts come from the vineyard and winery. A mix of species starts the fermentation before Saccharomyces takes over, which can add complexity, but results are less predictable and the risk of off-flavours or a slow fermentation is higher. Cultured yeasts are selected strains added by the winemaker; they start quickly, ferment reliably to dryness and can be chosen for particular aromas or alcohol tolerance, at the cost of some individuality.

Stuck fermentations

A stuck fermentation stops before the wine is dry, leaving it exposed to spoilage. Common causes are temperatures that are too high (yeast dies) or too low (yeast becomes inactive), very high sugar levels, lack of yeast nutrients such as nitrogen, and high alcohol. Winemakers prevent it by controlling temperature and adding nutrients; restarting a stuck fermentation is difficult.

Must adjustments

Where grapes are not perfectly balanced, winemakers may adjust the must, within limits set by local law. In cool climates, enrichment raises potential alcohol, by adding sugar (chaptalisation), rectified concentrated grape must, or by concentrating the must. In warm climates, acidification with tartaric acid corrects low acidity; deacidification is used in cool years with very high acidity. Many regions restrict how much can be added and do not allow enrichment and acidification of the same wine.

Exam technique: when a question mentions a cool region in a poor vintage, think enrichment and possibly deacidification; when it mentions a hot region, think acidification and harvesting earlier. Always say why: ripeness of the grapes drives the adjustment.

Malolactic conversion and maturation

Malolactic conversion is carried out by lactic acid bacteria, mainly Oenococcus oeni, which convert malic acid into lactic acid and carbon dioxide. Acidity becomes softer, the wine is more microbiologically stable, and buttery diacetyl may appear. Almost all red wines go through it; for whites it is a style choice. It can be encouraged by warmth and adding bacteria, or prevented with cool temperatures, sulfur dioxide or filtration.

Inert vessels versus oak

Maturation in inert vessels such as stainless steel or lined concrete keeps the wine fresh and fruity and is usually short. Maturation in oak does two things: it adds flavour if the oak is new or fairly new, and it allows slow oxygen exchange, which softens tannins, stabilises colour in reds and builds tertiary aromas. The smaller and newer the barrel, the stronger both effects.

Toasting the inside of the barrel during coopering changes its flavour: light toast keeps more fresh wood character, heavier toast gives smoky, roasted and spicy notes. The standard Bordeaux barrique holds 225 litres; large casks and vats give very little oak flavour and slow oxidation. Oak alternatives such as staves or chips placed in a tank give oak flavour cheaply and quickly but without the gradual oxygen exchange of a barrel.

Bottle ageing

In bottle, the wine is in an almost oxygen-free environment. Over time primary fruit fades and tertiary aromas develop: dried fruit, mushroom, forest floor, honey or petrol depending on the wine. Only a small proportion of wines improve with long bottle ageing; most are made to be drunk young.

Blending, clarification, stabilisation and packaging

Blending combines batches of different grapes, vineyards, vessels or ages to achieve a consistent house style or a more complex, balanced wine. Before bottling, the wine is clarified and stabilised so that it stays clear and sound once it reaches the consumer.

  1. Sedimentation and racking: solids settle naturally and the clear wine is moved off them.
  2. Fining: an agent such as bentonite, egg white or gelatine binds with haze-forming particles or harsh tannins and drops out; animal-based agents matter for vegan labelling.
  3. Filtration: depth filtration removes larger particles; fine membrane filtration can remove yeast and bacteria for microbial stability.
  4. Tartrate stabilisation: chilling the wine to remove tartrate crystals before bottling.
  5. Protein stabilisation: bentonite fining to prevent haze, important for whites and rosés.
  6. Final sulfur dioxide adjustment and bottling with minimal oxygen pick-up.

Some premium producers choose minimal fining and filtration, believing it preserves texture and flavour, and accept a risk of deposits or instability.

Key faults to recognise are cork taint (TCA: musty, damp cardboard, muted fruit), oxidation (loss of fruit, browning, bruised apple), reduction (rotten egg or struck match, caused by lack of oxygen), Brettanomyces (leather, farmyard, plaster) and volatile acidity (vinegar or nail varnish, from acetic acid bacteria).

Common mistake: confusing fining and filtration. Fining adds an agent that binds particles and settles out; filtration passes the wine through a medium that physically traps them. Both clarify, but only fine membrane filtration reliably removes microbes.

Sulfur dioxide: why almost every wine contains some

Sulfur dioxide (SO2) is the winemaker's main safety net. It acts as an antioxidant, protecting juice and wine from browning and loss of fresh aromas, and as an antimicrobial, slowing the growth of spoilage yeasts and bacteria. It can be added at several points: on the grapes at reception, to the juice before fermentation, after fermentation and before bottling. Fermentation itself also produces a small amount. Too much SO2 can mute aromas and give a burnt-match smell, and legal limits apply, so winemakers aim for the lowest effective dose. Wines with residual sugar generally need more protection than dry wines, because sugar is food for microbes. Producers who use little or no added SO2 must work with very healthy fruit and strict hygiene, and accept a higher risk of faults.

Neither column is good or bad: each is the right answer to a different question. Exam questions often ask why a technique suits one wine and not another, so always connect the technique to the target style and to the cost of producing it.

Sample questions with answers

Question: why might a winemaker choose cultured yeast for a large-volume wine? Answer: cultured yeast gives a quick, reliable fermentation with predictable flavours and a low risk of stuck fermentation or off-flavours. For a wine that must taste the same every year and be produced at low cost, that reliability matters more than the extra complexity ambient yeast might add.

Question: a premium red is bottled without filtration. What are the benefit and the risk? Answer: the producer believes that avoiding filtration preserves texture and flavour. The risk is a deposit in the bottle and, if the wine is not microbiologically stable, possible spoilage, so the winery must be confident in its hygiene and in the stability of the wine.

Common mistakes: saying that oak alternatives give the same result as barrels (they give flavour but not the slow oxygen exchange of a barrel), forgetting that SO2 has two roles, and describing a technique without saying which style or price level it suits.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between ambient and cultured yeast for WSET 3?

Ambient yeasts are naturally present in the vineyard and winery; a mix of species can give extra complexity but the fermentation is less predictable and riskier. Cultured yeasts are selected strains added by the winemaker; they start quickly, finish reliably and can be chosen for aroma or alcohol tolerance. Both rely on Saccharomyces cerevisiae to complete fermentation because it tolerates high alcohol.

What is the difference between French and American oak for WSET Level 3?

French oak comes mainly from Quercus petraea and Quercus robur and gives subtle spice, toast and cedar with fine tannins. American oak, Quercus alba, gives stronger vanilla and coconut aromas. Both are toasted during barrel making, and the level of toast, barrel size and age change their effect. Traditional Rioja is a classic example of American oak; many premium Bordeaux and Burgundy wines use French oak.

What is the difference between fining and filtration for WSET 3?

Fining adds an agent such as bentonite, egg white or gelatine that binds with haze-forming particles or harsh tannins and settles out, so the clear wine can be racked off. Filtration passes wine through a medium that physically traps particles; fine membrane filtration can remove yeast and bacteria. Both clarify the wine, but they work differently and are often used together.

Why do some wines use screwcaps instead of corks for WSET Level 3?

Screwcaps eliminate the risk of cork taint and give consistent results from bottle to bottle. They let very little oxygen in, which keeps primary fruit fresh, so they are popular for aromatic whites such as Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc. Their limitation is that very low oxygen can encourage reductive aromas. Natural cork remains traditional for many wines intended for long ageing.

What causes a stuck fermentation for WSET 3?

A stuck fermentation stops before all the sugar is converted. Common causes are temperatures too high, which kill yeast, or too low, which make it inactive; very high sugar levels; high alcohol; and a lack of nutrients such as nitrogen. The wine is then exposed to spoilage by bacteria and other yeasts. Winemakers prevent it with temperature control and nutrient additions, because restarting fermentation is difficult.

Why is sulfur dioxide added to wine for WSET 3?

Sulfur dioxide has two roles. As an antioxidant it protects the juice and wine from browning and loss of fresh aromas; as an antimicrobial it inhibits spoilage yeasts and bacteria. It can be added at harvest, before and after fermentation and at bottling. Too much can mute aromas, and legal limits apply, so winemakers use the lowest dose that keeps the wine stable.

What are oak alternatives and why are they used?

Oak alternatives are chips, staves or powder added to wine in tanks to give oak flavours such as vanilla and toast. They are far cheaper and quicker than barrels, which suits inexpensive wines. However, they do not reproduce the slow oxygen exchange that takes place through the wood of a barrel, so they add flavour without the same softening and complexity.

What is the difference between winemaking for volume and for premium wine?

Volume wines aim for a consistent, clean style at low cost: machine harvesting, cultured yeast, short maturation in inert vessels, oak alternatives and thorough filtration. Premium wines aim for concentration, complexity and ageing potential: low yields, hand harvesting, separate handling of small batches, longer maturation in barrel and often minimal fining and filtration. Each set of choices fits its own style and price.

Why do some wines need bottle ageing before release?

Some wines, especially those with high tannin, high acidity and concentrated fruit, are harsh when young. Time in bottle lets the tannins soften, the components integrate and tertiary aromas develop. Some regions and quality levels require minimum ageing before release, and some producers hold wines back so that customers receive them closer to their best drinking window.
