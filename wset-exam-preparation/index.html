WSET exam preparation: choose your level and study with a method

In short

Good WSET preparation means three things: picking the level that matches your knowledge, studying the syllabus chapter by chapter with active recall, and rehearsing the real exam format before the day. How do you prepare for a WSET exam?

Prepare in three phases: learn the syllabus chapter by chapter, consolidate it with practice questions and flashcards every day, then rehearse the real exam with timed mock exams in the final weeks. Each phase needs a small, regular effort rather than long weekend sessions.

The WSET qualifications are taught by independent course providers, in a classroom or online, and every level ends with a closed-book exam organised by your provider. Your course gives you the structure and the textbook. What happens between lessons is up to you, and that is where most results are decided: people who review a little every day remember far more than people who reread their notes the night before.

A useful way to think about it is that the course teaches you the material once, while revision makes sure you can retrieve it under time pressure. Reading feels productive, but answering questions, recalling facts from memory and explaining ideas in your own words are what actually build exam-ready knowledge.

Quick guide to choosing

  • New to wine or spirits and want a fast, friendly start: Level 1.
  • You enjoy wine or work in a shop, bar or restaurant: Level 2 is the most popular starting point, and Level 1 is not required.
  • You hold Level 2 and want to understand why wines taste the way they do: Level 3.
  • You studied wine and now want spirits: Spirits Level 2 is usually the right entry point, since production is a separate subject.

A study method that works at every level

The most reliable method combines four habits: learn in small chapters, test yourself straight away, repeat what you forget at increasing intervals, and practise the exact exam format. These habits are backed by decades of research on memory and they suit busy schedules.

1. Learn one chapter at a time

Follow the order of the syllabus and finish one topic before moving on: grape varieties, then a region, then winemaking or distillation. Short lessons of ten to fifteen minutes are easier to fit into a day and easier to remember than a two-hour reading session.

2. Test yourself immediately

After each lesson, answer a set of questions on the same chapter. Retrieving information from memory, even when you get it wrong, strengthens it more than rereading. Always read the explanation, and especially why the other options are wrong: that is where you learn to spot traps.

3. Space your repetitions

Flashcards with spaced repetition bring back the cards you miss sooner and the ones you know later. Ten minutes a day is enough to keep grape varieties, label terms, minimum ageing periods and service temperatures fresh until the exam.

4. Rehearse the real exam

In the last weeks, sit full mock exams with the real number of questions and the real time limit. Mock exams show you where you lose marks, train your pacing and remove the surprise factor on the day.

Keep a short list of your own recurring mistakes and reread it before every mock exam. Most errors repeat: the same two regions confused, the same label term misread.

How much study time do you really need?

Use the guideline hours as a minimum: about 6 hours for Level 1, around 26–28 hours for Level 2 and about 84 hours for Level 3, including class time. Spread them over the weeks before the exam rather than concentrating them.

A practical rule is to work backwards from your exam date. For Level 2, four to six weeks of 30–40 minutes a day covers the syllabus with time left for revision and mock exams. For Level 3, plan two to three months, with daily theory sessions, one written answer every day or two and regular tasting practice. If your exam is close, focus on the chapters with the most questions and on your weakest topics instead of trying to cover everything again.

What to do in the final week

In the last seven days, stop learning new material and focus on consolidation: one timed mock exam early in the week, short reviews of your mistakes, and daily flashcards. Sleep and a clear head are worth more than one extra chapter.

  • Early in the week: sit a full mock exam and list every topic that cost you marks.
  • Middle of the week: revise only those topics, with short question sets on each one.
  • Two days before: a light second mock exam or a mixed set, then stop.
  • The day before: flashcards only, check the exam time, place and what you need to bring.
  • On the day: read every question to the end, answer everything (there is no penalty for a wrong answer at Levels 1 and 2) and keep an eye on the clock.

Common preparation mistakes

Most candidates who struggle do not lack knowledge; they lack retrieval practice and exam rehearsal. Watch out for these habits.

  • Rereading the textbook instead of testing yourself.
  • Leaving smaller topics (fortified wines, liqueurs, labelling terms, food pairing) to the end.
  • Learning grapes and regions separately instead of as pairs.
  • Never practising under time pressure before the real exam.
  • At Level 3, writing lists of keywords instead of explaining cause and effect.
  • Studying for hours on one day and nothing for the rest of the week.

How do you combine your WSET course with self-study?

Use the course for first contact with each topic and your own study for everything that makes it stick: a short preview before each class, questions and flashcards within 24 hours after it, and a weekly mixed review. The course sets the pace; self-study decides how much of it you still remember on exam day.

WSET courses come in very different shapes. Some run as one evening a week for two months, others as two or three intensive days, others again as online programmes you follow at your own pace with a tutor available for questions. The right self-study routine depends on which shape you have, because the risk is different in each case: with a weekly class you forget between sessions, with an intensive course you receive too much at once, and with an online course nothing forces you to keep going.

Course formatMain riskHow to organise your own study
Weekly classes over several weeksForgetting the previous class before the next oneQuestions on the new chapter the day after class, flashcards every day, a mixed set at the weekend
Intensive course of two or three daysToo much information in too little timeRead the first chapters before the course, then use the weeks between the course and the exam for questions, flashcards and mocks
Online course at your own paceLosing momentum without fixed datesBook the exam first, then build a study plan backwards from that date with a daily goal
Course already finished, exam laterKnowledge fading while you waitRestart with mixed questions to find gaps, then move into regular timed mocks

Whatever your format, a ten-minute preview before a class pays off. Skim the chapter headings, look at a map of the region or a diagram of the process, and answer five questions even if you get them wrong. You will arrive with questions of your own, and the class will answer them instead of washing over you.

Three candidates, three preparation stories

There is no single correct way to prepare: the same method has to fit a full-time job, a family or irregular shifts. These three typical situations show how the principles on this page translate into real weeks.

Six weeks to Level 2, with a full-time office job

A candidate with an office job and a Level 2 exam six weeks away has about 30 to 40 minutes a day and a longer slot at weekends. The plan that works is boring on purpose: flashcards on the commute, one lesson or 20 practice questions in the evening, and one longer session on Saturday for a mixed set and the mistakes review. The first full mock goes in at week four, the second at week five, the third early in week six. Nothing is crammed, so the final week is mostly review and rest.

Three months to Level 3, with young children

A parent preparing Level 3 rarely gets a quiet hour, but often gets three quiet twenty-minute blocks. The trick is to give each block a single job: one for flashcards and questions, one for a lesson, one for writing a short answer or a tasting note. Tasting happens at dinner with whatever wine is open, described aloud with the systematic approach. Weekends hold the timed elements, such as a written-answer set or a theory mock, when a partner can cover the household for two hours.

Spirits Level 2, around evening bar shifts

A bartender working late shifts studies best in the early afternoon before work and in short bursts during quiet moments. Spirits Level 2 rewards this: the principal spirits carry the most questions, and the bar itself is a revision tool. Every bottle on the back bar becomes a flashcard prompt (raw material, still type, ageing rules), and cocktails become a quick check of service knowledge. Mock exams go on days off, in the morning, when concentration is highest.

Are you ready for the exam? A readiness checklist

You are ready when your mock scores are consistently above your target grade, your list of recurring mistakes is short, and no chapter feels like a blind spot. Use this checklist in the last ten days; every "no" is a revision task, not a reason to panic.

  • I have studied every chapter of the syllabus at least once, including the short ones at the end.
  • My last two timed mock exams were above my target grade, not just above the pass mark.
  • My weakest chapter in the last mock was still at or close to the pass mark.
  • I can name the grape or raw material behind every region or category I studied without hesitating.
  • I know the label terms and key numbers of my level well enough to answer in a few seconds.
  • I finished my last mock with time to spare and checked my flagged questions.
  • At Level 3, I have written at least a dozen short answers under time pressure and marked them honestly.
  • At Level 3, I can write a complete tasting note for two wines in the time allowed.
  • I know the date, time, place or online set-up of my exam and what my course provider asks me to bring.

If two or more boxes stay empty a week before the exam, do not add new material. Spend the remaining days on exactly those gaps, with short targeted sessions and one last mock.

Can you prepare for a wine and a spirits level at the same time?

You can, but it works best when the two exams are a few weeks apart and you give each one its own daily slot. Studying both in parallel without a plan tends to blur facts that look alike, such as ageing terms used in both worlds.

The two pathways share useful foundations: tasting technique, the effect of oak, the idea of protected names and label rules. They also contain look-alike vocabulary that causes confusion, for example "Reserva" on a Spanish wine label and age statements on spirits labels, which follow very different rules. If you prepare both, keep separate flashcard decks, take the earlier exam first and use the weeks after it to switch your daily goal to the second level.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I study for a WSET exam?

As a guideline, Level 1 needs about 6 hours, Level 2 around 26 to 28 hours and Level 3 about 84 hours, including class time. In practice, most people do best with short daily sessions over several weeks: four to six weeks for Level 2 and two to three months for Level 3, ending with timed mock exams in the final fortnight.

Can I skip Level 1 and start with Level 2?

Yes. Level 1 is not a requirement for Level 2, and many people start directly at Level 2, especially if they already drink wine with some curiosity or work in hospitality. Level 3, on the other hand, is designed for people who already have Level 2 or equivalent knowledge, because it builds on the same grapes, regions and terms.

What is the best way to revise for a WSET multiple-choice exam?

Answer questions every day rather than rereading notes, and always read the explanations, including why the wrong options are wrong. Use flashcards with spaced repetition for facts such as label terms and ageing rules, review your own mistakes regularly, and sit at least two or three full timed mock exams before the real one.

Do I need to taste wine to prepare?

At Levels 1 and 2 there is no tasting exam, but tasting still helps you remember styles, grapes and regions. At Wine Level 3 tasting is assessed with two wines served blind, so regular practice with the systematic approach is essential. The app's tasting diary and, at Level 3, the blind-tasting trainer help you practise consistently.

Is the WSET exam open book?

No. WSET exams are closed-book: you cannot use notes, textbooks or devices during the paper. That is why active recall matters so much in your preparation. Answering questions from memory, reviewing flashcards and sitting timed mocks without notes train exactly the skill the exam tests, while rereading only makes the material feel familiar. Practise without looking anything up, then check your answers afterwards.

Can I prepare for WSET without taking a course?

You can do much of the learning on your own, but you still enrol with a WSET course provider to sit the exam, and many providers include the course with the exam. Self-study works best as a complement: the course gives you the structure, a tutor and guided tastings, while an app like The Wine Goat gives you the daily practice, flashcards and mock exams that make the content stick until exam day.

How do I stay motivated during WSET preparation?

Make the daily goal small enough to meet on a bad day, track your streak and chapter progress, and connect study to real bottles you open or serve. Seeing a weak chapter move from 50% to 80% in practice questions is far more motivating than counting hours. If motivation drops, shorten sessions rather than skipping them: ten minutes of flashcards keeps the habit and the memory alive.

What should I do the morning of a WSET exam?

Keep it light. Eat normally, review a short set of flashcards or your list of personal confusions for ten to fifteen minutes, then stop. Check the time, place or online set-up and what your course provider asks you to bring. Arrive or log in early so you start calm. Avoid learning anything new: last-minute details tend to push out facts you already knew well.

Is it better to study in the morning or the evening?

Study when you are most alert and least likely to be interrupted, and keep that slot consistent. Many people find mornings better for new lessons and mock exams, and evenings fine for flashcards and short question sets. If you can, sit at least one mock exam at the same time of day as your real exam, so your concentration is trained for that moment.
