WSET Level 1 study guide: everything in one page

In short

WSET Level 1 is a one-day beginner course ending with a short multiple-choice exam. To pass, know how wine is made, the main styles, about eight key grapes, how to store and serve wine and the basic food-pairing rules.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

Level 1 is the gentlest step into wine: a short course and a multiple-choice exam. This page gathers what you need to know, so you can revise in an evening.

What the course covers

  • How wine is made: grapes, yeast, fermentation; white, rosé and red.
  • The main types and styles of wine: still, sparkling, fortified; dry to sweet; light to full.
  • Key grape varieties and their typical styles.
  • Storage and service: temperatures, glasses, opening a bottle.
  • Food and wine pairing: how food changes the taste of wine.

Five rules to remember

  • Sweet food makes wine taste drier, more acidic and bitter.
  • Salt and acidity in food make wine taste softer and fruitier.
  • Chilli heat increases the burning effect of alcohol.
  • Serve sparkling and sweet wines well chilled, light reds lightly chilled, full reds at room temperature.
  • Store bottles cool, dark and at a steady temperature, still wines with a cork on their side.

One-week study plan

  • Days 1–2: how wine is made and the styles.
  • Days 3–4: the key grapes.
  • Day 5: storage and service.
  • Day 6: food pairing.
  • Day 7: practice questions and review your mistakes.

The exam in numbers

The WSET Level 1 exam is a multiple-choice paper of 30 questions, to be answered in 45 minutes. Each question has one correct option. The pass mark is 70%, so you need at least 21 correct answers; you can afford to miss up to 9. That gives you 90 seconds per question, which is generous: most questions can be answered in 30 seconds if the knowledge is automatic, leaving plenty of time to reread the ones you are unsure of.

Aim for at least 25 correct answers in your practice tests. A margin of four or five questions protects you from nerves and from the odd question on a detail you overlooked.

Serving temperatures and glasses

Service questions are among the easiest points on the paper once you know the pattern: the lighter and sweeter or more sparkling the wine, the colder you serve it; the fuller and more tannic the red, the warmer, up to room temperature. Serving a full red too cold makes the tannin taste harsher; serving a white too warm makes it taste flat and alcoholic.

WineServing temperatureGlass
Sweet winesWell chilled, about 6–8°CSmall glass
Sparkling winesWell chilled, about 6–10°CFlute, to keep the bubbles
Light and medium-bodied whites, roséChilled, about 7–10°CMedium-sized glass
Full-bodied or oaked whitesLightly chilled, about 10–13°CMedium-sized glass
Light-bodied redsLightly chilled, about 13°CLarge glass
Medium and full-bodied redsRoom temperature, about 15–18°CLarge glass

Why the glass shapes? A flute has a small surface, so bubbles escape slowly. A large glass for reds leaves space for the aromas to gather and lets air reach the wine. A medium glass for whites keeps the wine cool for longer while still allowing you to smell it.

Memory hooks for the key grapes

Grape questions are the core of Level 1. A short hook for each grape makes the profiles stick far better than reading a table several times.

  • Chardonnay, the chameleon: it changes with climate and oak, from crisp and citrus in a cool place to tropical, buttery and toasty in a warm place with oak.
  • Sauvignon Blanc, the green one: grass, green pepper and gooseberry, with high acidity and usually no oak.
  • Pinot Grigio, the quiet one: light, crisp and neutral, a wine for easy drinking rather than strong aromas.
  • Riesling, the electric one: very high acidity, floral and citrus notes, made in every style from bone dry to very sweet.
  • Cabernet Sauvignon, the heavyweight: deep colour, high tannin, blackcurrant, often with oak.
  • Merlot, the soft one: plum and black cherry with softer tannin than Cabernet Sauvignon, which is why the two are often blended.
  • Pinot Noir, the delicate one: thin skin, pale colour, low tannin, red fruit such as strawberry and raspberry.
  • Syrah / Shiraz, the spicy one: black fruit and black pepper; same grape, two names, Syrah in France and Shiraz in Australia.

The four structural words to master

Questions about describing wine rely on a few words that beginners often mix up. Understand what you physically feel for each one and the questions become simple.

Sweetness

Sweetness is the amount of sugar you taste, felt on the tip of the tongue. A wine with ripe, fruity aromas can still be completely dry. Do not confuse fruity with sweet: a Sauvignon Blanc full of passion fruit aromas is usually dry.

Acidity

Acidity makes your mouth water, like biting into a lemon or a green apple. High acidity makes a wine taste fresh and crisp; low acidity makes it taste soft or flat. Cool climates give higher acidity.

Tannin

Tannin is a drying, gripping sensation on the gums and teeth, like strong black tea. It comes from the grape skins, so it is found mainly in red wines. High-tannin reds, such as Cabernet Sauvignon, feel firm; low-tannin reds, such as Pinot Noir, feel smooth.

Body

Body is the weight of the wine in the mouth. Think of the difference between skimmed milk and whole milk. Alcohol adds a lot to body, so wines from warm climates, where grapes are riper, tend to be fuller-bodied.

Food and wine pairing: five worked examples

The pairing rules are easier to remember when you apply them to real dishes. Each example below uses one of the five rules from the summary.

  1. Chocolate cake with a dry red: the sweetness of the cake makes the wine taste drier, more acidic and more bitter. A better choice is a sweet wine that is at least as sweet as the dessert, such as a sweet fortified wine.
  2. Salty fried snacks with a sparkling wine: salt makes the wine taste softer and less acidic, and the bubbles and acidity refresh the palate after fried food. A classic success.
  3. Spicy chilli curry with a high-alcohol red: the chilli heat increases the burning sensation of the alcohol and the bitterness of the tannin. A better choice is an off-dry, lower-alcohol white such as a Riesling with a touch of sweetness.
  4. Grilled fish with lemon and a Sauvignon Blanc: the acidity of the lemon makes the wine taste less acidic and fruitier, so a high-acid wine works well. A low-acid wine would taste flat next to the dish.
  5. Mushroom risotto with a very tannic red: umami, found in mushrooms and aged cheese, makes the wine taste more bitter and more acidic. A fruity, lower-tannin red such as Pinot Noir is the safer option.

Storing leftover wine and spotting simple faults

Once a bottle is open, oxygen starts to change the wine. Reseal it with its cork or a stopper and keep it in the fridge, including reds, then let reds warm up a little before serving. Most still wines remain pleasant for a few days. Sparkling wine needs a special stopper to keep its bubbles, and even then it loses its fizz quickly.

Two faults are useful to recognise. A wine affected by cork taint smells musty, like damp cardboard or a cellar, and its fruit is dull. An oxidised wine has lost its fresh fruit, looks browner than expected and can smell stale or nutty. In both cases the wine should be replaced rather than served.

Sample questions with explained answers

These questions are our own examples in the style of a beginner exam. Answer each one before reading the explanation.

Question 1: which process turns grape sugar into alcohol? Answer: fermentation. Yeast consumes the sugar and produces alcohol and carbon dioxide.

Question 2: why does red wine have more tannin than white wine? Answer: because red wine is fermented with the grape skins, and tannin comes from the skins. White wine is usually made from juice pressed away from the skins.

Question 3: which grape is typically light in colour with red fruit and low tannin? Answer: Pinot Noir. Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah are deeper in colour with more tannin.

Question 4: what happens to a dry wine served with a very sweet dessert? Answer: it tastes drier, more acidic and more bitter, and loses fruit. The wine should be at least as sweet as the food.

Question 5: at what temperature should a full-bodied red be served? Answer: at room temperature, around 15–18°C. Too cold, its tannin tastes harsh.

Question 6: what does salt in food do to a wine? Answer: it makes the wine taste softer, with less acidity and less bitterness, which is why salty food is easy to pair.

Question 7: how does a warm climate usually change a wine compared with a cool climate? Answer: riper grapes give more alcohol, fuller body, lower acidity and riper fruit flavours.

Question 8: why should a bottle with a cork be stored on its side? Answer: so the cork stays in contact with the wine and remains moist; a dry cork can shrink and let air in, which spoils the wine.

A detailed seven-day plan

This plan assumes 20 to 30 minutes a day in the week before the exam. If your course is on a single day with the exam at the end, use the same steps in the evenings that follow your booking, then do a final review on the eve of the course.

The night before and on exam day

  • Review only your summary sheet and your mistakes from practice questions; do not start new topics.
  • Sleep properly: a short, simple exam rewards a clear head more than last-minute cramming.
  • Read each question to the end before looking at the options, and watch for words such as NOT, usually and main.
  • Answer the easy questions first, mark the doubtful ones and come back to them with the time left.
  • Never leave a question blank: there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so always choose an option.
  • Use the last minutes to check that every answer is marked in the right place.

Glossary of Level 1 terms

  • Fermentation: the process by which yeast turns sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
  • Tannin: a drying, gripping sensation from grape skins, found mainly in red wines.
  • Acidity: the freshness that makes your mouth water; higher in cool-climate wines.
  • Body: the weight of a wine in the mouth, from light to full.
  • Dry: a wine with no noticeable sugar.
  • Oaked: a wine fermented or aged in oak, often with vanilla, toast or spice notes.
  • Sparkling wine: a wine with bubbles of carbon dioxide trapped from a fermentation.
  • Fortified wine: a wine with added grape spirit, such as port or sherry.
  • Cork taint: a fault that gives a musty, damp cardboard smell.
  • Umami: a savoury taste found in mushrooms, aged cheese and soy, which makes wine taste more bitter and acidic.

Frequently asked questions

How long is the WSET Level 1 course?

It is usually a one-day course, followed by the exam the same day.

Is WSET Level 1 worth it?

Yes if you are new to wine or work in hospitality: it gives a clear, recognised foundation. If you already know the basics, Level 2 is more useful.

What do I need to memorise for WSET Level 1?

The main styles, the key grapes with their typical style, serving temperatures and the food-pairing rules. Short daily quizzes are enough.

How many questions do I need right to pass WSET Level 1?

The exam has 30 multiple-choice questions and the pass mark is 70%, so you need at least 21 correct answers. You can miss up to nine questions and still pass. Aiming for 25 or more in practice tests gives you a safety margin for exam-day nerves or an unexpected question on a detail.

Is WSET Level 1 hard?

For most people it is not. It is designed for beginners, the content is limited to the key ideas of wine and the exam gives 90 seconds per question. The main risks are confusing similar terms, such as fruity and sweet, and overlooking easy service and pairing points. A few evenings of focused revision and practice questions are usually enough.

Which serving temperatures should I memorise for WSET Level 1?

Learn the pattern: sweet and sparkling wines well chilled, about 6–10°C; light and medium whites and rosé chilled, about 7–10°C; full-bodied or oaked whites lightly chilled, about 10–13°C; light reds lightly chilled, about 13°C; medium and full-bodied reds at room temperature, about 15–18°C. The questions usually test the pattern more than exact numbers.

Do I need to taste wine to pass WSET Level 1?

There is no tasting exam at Level 1, but tasting helps you remember. Trying a Sauvignon Blanc next to a Chardonnay, or a Pinot Noir next to a Cabernet Sauvignon, makes acidity, tannin and body concrete. Even two or three side-by-side tastings during your revision week make the grape and style questions much easier.

How long should I study for WSET Level 1?

The course itself is usually a single day. For revision, 20 to 30 minutes a day over a week, or a few focused evenings, is enough for most beginners. Spend most of the time on the key grapes, the style map, serving temperatures and the pairing rules, and finish with at least one timed set of 30 practice questions.
