WSET Level 2 grapes and regions: the cheat sheet

In short

WSET Level 2 focuses on about 15 principal grapes and the regions where they make their best-known wines. For each grape, learn its key regions, its typical style and how a cool or warm climate changes it.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

Most WSET Level 2 questions link a grape to a region and a style. Learn this table well and you answer a large part of the exam.

Cool vs warm climate

Cool climates give higher acidity, lighter body and green or citrus fruit; warm climates give riper, riper-tasting fruit, more body and more alcohol. Many questions ask exactly this contrast for Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Syrah and Cabernet Sauvignon.

Regional grapes often asked

Albariño (Rías Baixas), Grüner Veltliner (Austria), Furmint (Tokaj), Torrontés (Argentina), Pinotage (South Africa), Carmenère (Chile), Zinfandel (California), Garganega (Soave), Montepulciano (Abruzzo).

How to memorise them

  • Group grapes by colour and by climate preference.
  • Practise in both directions: grape → region and region → grape.
  • Ten minutes of mixed questions every day beats one long session.

Study notes: each grape, its regions and why it tastes that way

The cheat sheet above gives you the pairs; these notes give you the reasons. For every grape you will find where it grows, how the style changes with climate and winemaking, and the clues that help you recognise it in a question. Read one grape a day and then test yourself in both directions: from the grape to the region, and from the region back to the grape.

Chardonnay

Chardonnay is a fairly neutral grape, which is why it shows the influence of climate and winemaking so clearly. In Chablis, the coolest part of Burgundy, it gives lean wines with high acidity and green apple and lemon flavours, usually with little or no new oak. Further south in the Côte de Beaune and the Mâconnais it becomes rounder, with more stone fruit, and the best wines are often fermented and aged in oak. In warm regions of California and Australia it shows peach, melon and pineapple, and many examples combine malolactic conversion with new oak for butter, vanilla and toast. It is also one of the three main grapes of Champagne.

Sauvignon Blanc

Sauvignon Blanc is aromatic, with high acidity and green, herbaceous notes such as grass, green pepper and gooseberry. It is usually fermented cool in stainless steel and drunk young. In the Loire Valley, Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé give restrained, mineral, citrus styles. Marlborough in New Zealand has made it famous for a very intense, pungent style that adds passion fruit to the green notes. In Bordeaux it is often blended with Sémillon, both for dry whites and for the sweet wines of Sauternes.

Riesling

Riesling is very aromatic, with floral and citrus notes, and keeps high acidity even when ripe. It is rarely oaked, so its fruit stays pure. In the Mosel, a cool German region with steep slate slopes along the river, it gives light, low-alcohol wines that range from dry to lusciously sweet. Alsace and the Clare and Eden Valleys in Australia make mostly dry, fuller styles, the Australian ones with distinctive lime notes. Riesling ages very well, developing honey and toast aromas, and it is used for late-harvest, noble rot and icewine sweet wines.

Pinot Grigio / Pinot Gris

This grape has a greyish-pink skin and two well-known personalities. As Pinot Grigio in north-east Italy it is picked early to give light, crisp, neutral whites with citrus and pear. As Pinot Gris in Alsace it is picked riper, giving fuller, richer wines with ripe stone fruit, sometimes with a touch of sweetness. The name on the label is a strong hint of the style.

Chenin Blanc

Chenin Blanc has high acidity, which makes it extremely versatile. In the Loire, Vouvray is made in every style from dry to sweet and sparkling, with apple, quince and honey notes. South Africa is the other key country, with fresh, fruity everyday wines and richer, sometimes oaked, versions.

Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon has small, thick-skinned grapes that give deep colour, high tannin and high acidity, with blackcurrant, green pepper and mint notes. It needs warmth to ripen fully. On the Left Bank of Bordeaux, in the Médoc, gravel soils store heat and help it ripen; there it is blended with Merlot and other grapes. Napa Valley in California and Coonawarra in Australia make riper, fuller versions, often aged in new oak. Good examples age for many years.

Merlot

Merlot ripens earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon and gives softer wines with medium tannin and plum and red fruit flavours. It dominates the Right Bank of Bordeaux, in Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, where cooler clay soils suit it better than Cabernet. In Chile and California it makes fruity, approachable reds. Its softness is the reason it is often blended with Cabernet Sauvignon: one gives structure, the other gives flesh.

Pinot Noir

Pinot Noir has thin skins, so its wines are pale, with low to medium tannin and red fruit such as strawberry, raspberry and cherry, developing earthy and mushroom notes with age. It needs a cool climate: in heat it loses its delicacy. Burgundy, especially the Côte de Nuits, is its reference region. New Zealand, Oregon and the cooler parts of California and Chile make riper, fruitier versions. It is also a key grape in Champagne.

Syrah / Shiraz

Syrah gives deep-coloured wines with black fruit and medium to high tannin. In the Northern Rhône, in appellations such as Côte-Rôtie and Hermitage, the style is structured with black pepper notes. In Australia, where it is called Shiraz, the warm Barossa Valley gives full-bodied, high-alcohol wines with ripe blackberry, chocolate and sweet spice, often aged in American oak. In the Southern Rhône it is a blending partner for Grenache.

Grenache / Garnacha

Grenache loves heat and drought. It gives wines with high alcohol, light colour, ripe red fruit and soft tannins, often with sweet spice. It is the backbone of Southern Rhône blends such as Côtes du Rhône and Châteauneuf-du-Pape, usually with Syrah and Mourvèdre. In Spain, as Garnacha, it is used in Rioja and Navarra and makes powerful reds in Priorat. It is also a favourite grape for rosé.

Tempranillo

Tempranillo gives medium to deep coloured wines with red fruit and medium acidity and tannin. It is Spain's leading quality red grape, in Rioja, often blended with Garnacha, and in Ribera del Duero, where the style is usually deeper and more concentrated. It takes oak ageing very well: in Rioja, American oak brings vanilla and coconut, and the ageing terms Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva are especially common. In Portugal it is used in the Douro under another name.

Sangiovese

Sangiovese is Tuscany's grape: high acidity, high tannin and red cherry with herbal notes. It is the main grape of Chianti and Chianti Classico, and Brunello di Montalcino is made from it alone. Its high acidity makes it a classic partner for tomato-based dishes.

Nebbiolo

Nebbiolo from Piedmont, in north-west Italy, is a paradox: pale in colour but very high in tannin and acidity, with aromas of red cherry and roses that develop earthy notes over time. Barolo and Barbaresco are its two most famous wines and need years of ageing to soften.

Malbec

Malbec gives deep-coloured wines with black fruit and medium to high tannin. Its modern home is Mendoza in Argentina, where high-altitude vineyards give sunny days and cool nights, keeping freshness and colour. Its historic home is Cahors in south-west France, where it gives firm, dark wines.

Blends you should recognise

  • Bordeaux red: Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, often with Cabernet Franc; the proportion depends on the bank.
  • Bordeaux white: Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon, dry or sweet as in Sauternes.
  • Southern Rhône: Grenache with Syrah and Mourvèdre; the same blend appears in Australia, where it is often called GSM.
  • Rioja: Tempranillo with Garnacha and small amounts of other local grapes.
  • Champagne: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier, alone or together.
  • Chianti: mainly Sangiovese, sometimes with small amounts of other grapes.

Cool, moderate or warm? A quick classification

Many questions describe a style and ask where it comes from, so it helps to sort the regions by climate. The groupings below are simplified, but they match the logic of the course and are enough to eliminate wrong answers quickly.

ClimateTypical regionsWhat it means in the glass
CoolChablis, Champagne, Loire Valley, Mosel, Marlborough, Central Otago, OregonHigh acidity, lighter body, citrus, green and red fruit
ModerateBordeaux, Côte d'Or, Northern Rhône, Rioja, Piedmont, TuscanyBalanced acidity, medium to full body, a mix of fresh and ripe fruit
WarmSouthern Rhône, Barossa Valley, Napa Valley, Central Valley of Chile, DouroFuller body, higher alcohol, softer acidity, ripe black and tropical fruit

Remember that altitude and sea breezes can turn a warm country into a source of fresh wines: Mendoza is cooled by height, Casablanca in Chile by the Pacific Ocean, and parts of South Africa by the Atlantic.

A five-step method to memorise grapes and regions

  1. Start with the eight principal grapes and write, for each one, two cool regions and two warm regions.
  2. Add one country a day, placing every region on a blank map and naming its grapes from memory.
  3. Make a flashcard for each region with the grape on the back, and another set the other way round.
  4. Every few days, sort the regions into cool, moderate and warm, and explain the effect on acidity, body and fruit.
  5. Finish each session with ten mixed questions and write down every mistake to review the next day.

Say the grape and the region together aloud, as one phrase: Barossa Shiraz, Marlborough Sauvignon, Mendoza Malbec. Linking them in speech makes the pair much harder to forget.

Sample questions with explained answers

Question: Which grape is used for red Burgundy? Answer: Pinot Noir. Burgundy's red wines come from Pinot Noir and its whites from Chardonnay; Gamay is the grape of Beaujolais, just to the south.

Question: Which region is best known for Malbec grown at high altitude? Answer: Mendoza in Argentina. The altitude gives cool nights that keep acidity and colour in a very sunny climate.

Question: A white wine is pale, light, very high in acidity, low in alcohol and medium-sweet, with floral and green apple notes. Which region is the most likely? Answer: the Mosel. A cool climate and Riesling explain the high acidity and low alcohol, and the sweetness is a common style there.

Question: Which grape is the main variety of Chianti? Answer: Sangiovese, which gives high acidity and red cherry flavours.

Question: Why is Cabernet Sauvignon planted on gravel on the Left Bank of Bordeaux? Answer: gravel drains well and stores heat, helping a late-ripening grape reach full ripeness in a moderate climate.

Question: Which grape is used to make Prosecco? Answer: Glera, grown in north-east Italy and made sparkling by the tank method.

Frequently asked questions

How many grapes do you need to know for WSET Level 2?

The course focuses on a group of principal international grapes plus a number of regionally important ones; the table above covers the main ones.

What is the best way to learn WSET Level 2 regions?

Use a map and practise grape ↔ region questions daily. The Wine Goat has interactive maps and flashcards for this.

Is there tasting in the WSET Level 2 exam?

No: the Level 2 exam is multiple choice only, though tasting in class helps you remember the styles.

Which grapes are the eight principal varieties at WSET Level 2?

The course gives the most weight to Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris and Riesling among whites, and Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot Noir and Syrah/Shiraz among reds. For each one you should know the classic regions and how the style changes in cooler and warmer places. The regionally important grapes, such as Tempranillo, Sangiovese or Albariño, are then attached to specific regions.

How many regions do I need to learn for WSET Level 2?

The course covers more than seventy geographical indications, but they are not all equally important. Start with the regions linked to the principal grapes, then add the classic regions of France, Italy, Spain and Germany, and finally the main New World areas. Learning them by country and climate, with a map, is much faster than memorising an alphabetical list.

What is the difference between a grape and a region on a wine label?

Many European wines name only the region, for example Chablis or Rioja, and you are expected to know which grapes it allows. Many New World wines name the grape, for example Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc. Exam questions often test whether you can translate a European regional name into its grape variety, so practise region-to-grape questions regularly.

Do I need to know soils for WSET Level 2?

Only at a very basic level. The idea that matters most is that some soils store heat or drain well, such as the gravel of the Bordeaux Left Bank, while others stay cooler, such as the clay of the Right Bank. Detailed soil types for each region belong to Level 3, where you will need to explain how they influence ripening.

How can I avoid mixing up Italian and Spanish red grapes?

Link each grape to one emblematic wine and one key structural feature. Sangiovese: Chianti, high acidity. Nebbiolo: Barolo, pale but very tannic. Tempranillo: Rioja, takes oak well. Garnacha: high alcohol, red fruit. Repeating these short profiles in mixed practice sessions quickly removes the confusion between them.
