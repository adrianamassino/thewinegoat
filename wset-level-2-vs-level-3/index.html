WSET Level 2 or Level 3: what really changes

In short

WSET Level 2 or Level 3? The real differences in syllabus, exam format, tasting and study time, how big the jump is and how to choose the right course.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

Level 2 teaches you what the world's main wines taste like and why. Level 3 asks you to explain it: how climate, vineyard and winemaking build each style, in writing and in a blind tasting.

The jump is real but manageable with a daily routine. Here is a clear comparison to help you choose.

Can you go straight to Level 3?

Level 2 (or equivalent knowledge) is recommended before Level 3. If you already work with wine or have studied it seriously, you may be ready – test yourself honestly first: if you can explain why a cool climate gives high acidity and what malolactic conversion does to a white wine, you have the right base.

How to prepare for the jump

  • Revise Level 2 basics in the first week: grape profiles, climate, key regions.
  • Start writing short answers early: it is the biggest new skill at Level 3.
  • Taste with a structured note every time, even at home.
  • Practise a little every day instead of cramming before the exam.

Study notes: the jump from Level 2 to Level 3 in detail

The short comparison above tells you that Level 3 is harder. These notes show exactly where the extra difficulty comes from, so you can decide which course fits you today and, if you choose Level 3, prepare for the parts that surprise most candidates. In short, the jump has three dimensions: more content, deeper explanations and two new exam skills, writing and blind tasting.

The total study time roughly triples. That is the most useful single figure for planning: if Level 2 took you four weeks of light study, Level 3 is closer to three months of regular work, or a shorter but much more intensive period.

The same topic, two levels: worked examples

The clearest way to feel the difference is to compare how a topic is examined at each level.

Example 1: Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc

A typical Level 2 question asks which grape is the signature variety of Marlborough, with four options. You either know it is Sauvignon Blanc or you do not. At Level 3, a written question might ask you to explain how the climate and winemaking of Marlborough contribute to the style of its Sauvignon Blanc, with several marks available.

A strong Level 3 answer gives one marked idea per sentence. Marlborough has a cool maritime climate, so the grapes ripen slowly and keep high acidity. Long sunny days give intense aromas while cool nights preserve freshness. A large diurnal range helps retain the green, herbaceous notes. Fermentation at cool temperatures in stainless steel keeps the pungent fruit aromas. The wine is usually bottled young without oak, so the primary passion fruit and grass flavours stay at the front. Every sentence links a cause to an effect on the style, which is exactly what the marking rewards.

Example 2: Port

At Level 2 you need to know that spirit is added during fermentation, which leaves natural sugar, and that Ruby and Tawny are two styles. At Level 3 you are expected to explain how extraction is achieved in a short fermentation, why some Ports age in large old vessels and others in small casks, how oxidative ageing turns fruity flavours into nutty, caramel notes, and how the different categories relate to quality and ageing potential.

Example 3: tasting

At Level 2 you might describe a red as having high tannin and black fruit. At Level 3 the same wine needs a full structured note: clarity and colour intensity, aroma intensity and development, sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity and characteristics, and finish, then a conclusion on quality justified by balance, length, intensity and complexity, and an assessment of whether it is ready to drink or can age.

Example 4: Champagne

At Level 2 it is enough to know that Champagne is made by the traditional method, with the second fermentation in the bottle, from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier. At Level 3 you should be able to explain why the cool climate of Champagne suits sparkling wine, since high-acid, low-sugar grapes make an ideal base, why blending across sites and vintages gives a consistent house style, how long ageing on the lees creates bread and biscuit aromas through autolysis, and how dosage decides the final sweetness level.

What changes in tasting

The Level 3 Systematic Approach to Tasting uses more precise scales. Where a beginner says acidity is medium or high, a Level 3 candidate chooses between low, medium(-), medium, medium(+) and high, and must be consistent: a wine described with high acidity and a long finish cannot logically be judged poor. The tasting exam does not ask you to name the wine; it rewards accurate, coherent descriptions. That is good news, because accuracy improves quickly with regular, structured practice.

  • Taste with a written note every time, even at home with a single glass.
  • Calibrate with reference wines: a high-acid Riesling, a high-tannin Nebbiolo, a high-alcohol Grenache.
  • Compare two wines side by side, because differences are easier to measure than absolutes.
  • Always finish with a quality judgement and say why, using balance, length, intensity and complexity.

What changes in the theory paper

The multiple-choice section feels familiar, but the questions are more detailed and the wrong options are closer to the right one. The real novelty is the short written answers, where most marks are won or lost. Candidates who passed Level 2 easily are often surprised that knowing the facts is not enough: you have to show the link between cause and effect in clear sentences.

  1. Read the question twice and underline the command word: describe, explain, compare.
  2. Check the marks for each part; they tell you how many separate points the examiner expects.
  3. Plan for one minute: list the causes and the effects you will connect.
  4. Write one idea per sentence, using precise vocabulary such as diurnal range, malolactic conversion or lees contact.
  5. Stop when you have covered the marks; extra words do not earn extra points.

What stays the same

Not everything changes. The cause-and-effect logic you learned at Level 2 remains the backbone of Level 3: climate drives ripeness, ripeness drives acidity, sugar, alcohol and flavour, and winemaking choices shape the final style. The principal grapes are still the anchors, and many Level 3 regions are simply more detailed versions of places you already know. Candidates who truly understood Level 2, rather than memorising it, find that much of Level 3 feels like zooming in on a familiar map.

Three typical profiles

A shop assistant who sells wine every day but has no formal training usually benefits from Level 2 first. It turns daily experience into a structured framework within a few weeks and gives immediate confidence with customers. Level 3 then becomes a natural second step, often a few months later.

A keen enthusiast who has already passed Level 2 with a comfortable score and tastes regularly is generally ready for Level 3. The main adjustments are building a daily study habit and practising written answers from the first week, since these are the skills Level 2 never tested.

A sommelier with several years of service but no previous qualification may consider going straight to Level 3. The deciding factor is not experience alone but an honest self-test: if the Level 2 basics are solid, starting at Level 3 saves time; if there are gaps, especially in winemaking or labelling, a quick Level 2 revision prevents problems later.

A self-test before you jump

If you can answer most of these questions in a sentence or two without notes, you are ready to start Level 3. If several feel difficult, revise Level 2 first or plan extra time at the start of the course.

  • Why does a cool climate give wines with high acidity and lower alcohol? Because slow ripening keeps acid and limits sugar.
  • What does malolactic conversion do to a white wine? It softens the acidity and can add buttery notes.
  • What is the difference between the traditional and the tank method? Where the second fermentation happens, in the bottle or in a pressurised tank.
  • Which grapes make Champagne? Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier.
  • Why is Port sweet but Fino Sherry dry? Port is fortified during fermentation, Sherry after it.
  • What grape is behind Barolo, Chianti, Rioja and Sancerre? Nebbiolo, Sangiovese, Tempranillo and Sauvignon Blanc.
  • What does noble rot do to grapes? It shrivels them and concentrates sugar and acidity, adding honey and dried fruit flavours.
  • What is the effect of sweetness in food on a dry wine? It makes the wine taste drier, more acidic and more bitter.

Common mistakes when moving up

  • Studying Level 3 like Level 2, with lists of facts and no explanation of why.
  • Leaving written practice until the last weeks, when it is the biggest new skill.
  • Tasting without writing, so there is no record to check consistency or progress.
  • Underestimating the volume: regions and winemaking options multiply, so daily spaced review becomes essential.
  • Treating the tasting unit as a bonus, when it needs 55% on its own to pass the qualification.

Fifteen to thirty minutes a day beats a long weekend session. At Level 3 the volume is too large to cram, and spaced repetition keeps earlier topics fresh while you add new ones.

Frequently asked questions

Is WSET Level 3 much harder than Level 2?

Yes: the syllabus is far more detailed and you must write explanations and pass a blind tasting. With regular daily practice most motivated candidates manage the jump.

Can I take WSET Level 3 without Level 2?

Level 2 or equivalent knowledge is recommended. Many schools accept experienced candidates directly; check with your course provider.

Should I take Level 2 or Level 3 for a career in wine?

Level 2 is a solid foundation for service and retail; Level 3 is widely seen as the professional standard for sommeliers, buyers and educators, and leads to the Diploma.

How many more hours does WSET Level 3 need than Level 2?

Level 2 is usually indicated at about 28 hours of total study, while Level 3 is about 84 hours, roughly three times as much. The difference comes from the larger syllabus, the need to explain causes and effects in writing and the tasting practice. Spreading the work over several weeks with daily sessions is far more effective than trying to fit it into a few long weekends.

Is the multiple-choice part of Level 3 similar to Level 2?

The format is similar, with 50 questions and four options each, but the content is more detailed and the wrong options are often very plausible. Questions test cause and effect across the whole syllabus, not only grape and region pairs. Regular timed practice helps you recognise patterns quickly, which matters because the written answers share the same theory paper.

Do I have to name the wines in the Level 3 tasting?

No. You describe two wines using the Systematic Approach to Tasting and reach conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking. Marks come from accurate, consistent descriptions, not from guessing the grape or region. This means steady, structured practice pays off more than a wide knowledge of famous wines.

Can a good Level 2 result predict success at Level 3?

A Merit or Distinction at Level 2 shows that your knowledge base is solid, which helps a lot with the multiple-choice section. However, Level 3 adds written answers and blind tasting, which are new skills. Candidates who start practising both early, rather than relying on their Level 2 result, usually make the transition smoothly.

Is WSET Level 3 worth it if I do not work in wine?

Many Level 3 candidates are enthusiasts rather than professionals. The course explains why wines taste the way they do in far more depth than Level 2, which changes how you choose, taste and talk about wine. It does require a real commitment of time and regular practice, so it suits people who enjoy studying and want a structured challenge, not only a certificate.

Should I wait between Level 2 and Level 3?

There is no fixed rule. Some candidates move on immediately while the material is fresh; others prefer a few months to taste more and gain experience. If you wait, keep a light routine of questions and tasting notes so that your Level 2 knowledge does not fade before the new course begins.
