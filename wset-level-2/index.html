WSET Level 2 Award in Wines: the complete guide

The most popular wine qualification in the world. Level 2 takes you through the grape varieties and regions behind the wines you see on shelves and wine lists, how labels work and how to pair wine with food. The exam is 50 multiple-choice questions: very passable with a structured plan.

What the syllabus covers

  • The eight principal grape varieties and 22 regionally important ones
  • Wines from over 70 geographical indications around the world
  • How the environment and winemaking (oak, malolactic conversion, residual sugar) shape a wine’s style
  • Sparkling wines and their methods, and fortified wines such as Port and Sherry
  • Reading wine labels: quality categories and labelling terms in the main countries
  • Storage, service and food and wine pairing
  • Describing wines with the WSET Level 2 Systematic Approach to Tasting

  • Riesling

How to study, step by step

  1. Build a grid for the 8 principal grapes

    For each one write the climate, the key regions, the typical aromas and how it changes in a cool or warm area. Most questions use this logic.

  2. Attach each region to its grape

    Learn the regions as pairs: Sancerre — Sauvignon Blanc, Barossa — Shiraz, Rioja — Tempranillo, Chianti — Sangiovese. Then add the regionally important grapes.

  3. Learn labelling terms

    Questions often test terms like Riserva, Crianza, Kabinett, Brut or AOC/DOP. Turn them into flashcards.

  4. Do full mock exams

    Train with 50 questions in 60 minutes and review every mistake: the pass mark is 55%, but aiming for 70% gives you a safety margin.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up regions in the same country (Bordeaux Left Bank vs Right Bank, Northern vs Southern Rhône)
  • Forgetting the effect of climate on the same grape
  • Confusing sparkling methods (traditional vs tank)
  • Underestimating fortified wines and labelling terms

Frequently asked questions

How hard is the WSET Level 2 exam?

It requires real study but it is very passable: 50 multiple-choice questions in an hour with a 55% pass mark. Most candidates who follow the course and practise with questions pass.

How long should I study for WSET Level 2?

WSET indicates about 28 hours in total, including 16 hours of guided learning. In practice 3–5 weeks of regular revision works well.

Do I need Level 1 before Level 2?

No. You can start directly at Level 2 — many people do.

Is there a tasting exam at Level 2?

No. You taste during the course, but the exam is only multiple choice. Blind tasting is assessed from Level 3.

What happens after Level 2?

Level 3 goes much deeper, with written answers and a blind tasting of two wines. The Wine Goat already covers Level 3 in full.

Will The Wine Goat have Level 2?

Yes. Lessons, practice questions, flashcards and mock exams for Level 2 are already in the app.

What score do I need for a Merit or Distinction at WSET Level 2?

The pass mark is 55%, which means 28 correct answers out of 50. A Merit requires 70%, or 35 correct answers, and a Distinction requires 85%, or 43 correct answers. Wrong answers do not cost points, so answer every question. Aiming for Merit in your mock exams is a good way to build a safety margin for the real exam day, when nerves can cost a few marks.

Are there questions on sparkling and fortified wines at Level 2?

Yes. Expect questions on the traditional and tank methods, on the grapes used in Champagne, on Prosecco and Cava, and on how Port and Sherry are made. These topics are small but predictable: one key technique explains each style, so they are among the easiest marks to secure if you revise them carefully.

Is WSET Level 2 mostly about memorising?

Less than many candidates expect. A large share of questions ask why a wine has a certain style, and these can be answered by reasoning from climate, ripeness and winemaking. You still need to learn grape–region pairs and labelling terms, but understanding cause and effect reduces the amount of pure memorisation and makes the facts easier to remember.

Can I prepare for WSET Level 2 on my own?

You can do a large part of the work on your own with structured notes, flashcards and timed practice questions, but the exam is taken through an approved course provider, and the course includes guided tastings that help fix styles in your memory. Many candidates combine classes with daily self-study, which is the most efficient approach.

How much tasting should I do while studying Level 2?

Tasting is not examined at Level 2, but tasting the classic styles side by side makes the theory stick: a Chablis next to an oaked Californian Chardonnay, or a Sancerre next to a Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc. Even a few comparisons during the course help you remember how climate and oak change a wine, which is exactly what many questions test.

Study notes: WSET Level 2 explained topic by topic

The WSET Level 2 Award in Wines looks like a long list of grapes, regions and terms, but it is built on a handful of ideas that repeat across the whole course. Once you see the structure, every new region becomes a variation on something you already know. These notes walk through the syllabus in the order that makes the most sense for revision, with tables you can copy into your own notebook and examples of how each topic turns into exam questions.

How the exam is marked

The exam is 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes. Each question has four options and only one is correct, and there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a question blank. Your result is a percentage, and three grades are possible on top of a fail.

ResultPercentageCorrect answers out of 50
Pass55% or more28 or more
Pass with Merit70% or more35 or more
Pass with Distinction85% or more43 or more
Failbelow 55%27 or fewer

Sixty minutes for fifty questions gives you a little over a minute per question. Most questions take twenty or thirty seconds if you know the topic, which leaves plenty of time to come back to the difficult ones. A good routine is to answer everything you are sure of in a first pass, mark the doubtful ones, and use the remaining time to think them through.

The logic behind the syllabus: three factors that make a style

Almost every wine in the course can be explained by three groups of factors: where the grapes grow, how they are grown and picked, and what happens in the winery. If you learn the effect of each factor, you can answer questions about regions you have barely revised, because the exam usually rewards reasoning rather than isolated facts.

1. The environment: climate, weather and site

Grapes need warmth and sunlight to ripen. As a grape ripens, sugar rises, acidity falls, and flavours move from green and tart to ripe and sweet. In a cool climate this process is slow, so wines keep high acidity, have lighter body and lower alcohol, and show fruit such as green apple, lemon, cranberry or red cherry. In a warm climate ripening is fast and complete, so wines have more alcohol and body, softer acidity and riper fruit such as peach, pineapple, blackberry or plum. Rivers, lakes and the sea moderate temperatures; slopes facing the sun collect more warmth; altitude cools a vineyard down.

2. In the vineyard: ripeness and harvest timing

The grower decides when to pick. Early picking keeps acidity and freshness and is used for sparkling wine bases or crisp whites. Late picking gives more sugar and riper flavours, and in some places grapes are left on the vine to concentrate even further for sweet wines. Yield also matters: vines that produce fewer grapes usually give more concentrated wines.

3. In the winery: the choices that change the flavour

When a question describes a wine and asks why it tastes that way, look for the option that names a cause. Climate, ripeness, oak and malolactic conversion explain most styles in the exam.

The eight principal grapes, cool and warm

The course gives special weight to eight international varieties. For each one you should be able to name the classic regions, describe the typical flavours and say how the wine changes in a cooler or a warmer place. The table below is a compact version of that grid.

Notice that the grape name often changes with the style: Syrah tends to be used for the peppery, cooler style and Shiraz for the riper Australian style; Pinot Grigio usually signals the light Italian style and Pinot Gris the richer Alsace style. These naming habits are useful clues in questions.

Sparkling, sweet and fortified wines

These three families make up a smaller part of the course but they produce some of the most predictable questions, because each style depends on one clear technique.

Sparkling wines

MethodWhere the second fermentation happensStyleExamples
Traditional methodIn the bottle the wine is sold inFine bubbles, bread, biscuit and toast from time on the leesChampagne, Cava, Crémant
Tank methodIn a sealed pressurised tankFresh, fruity and floral, best drunk youngProsecco, Asti

Champagne uses Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Pinot Meunier. Cava comes mostly from Catalonia in Spain. Prosecco is made mainly from the Glera grape in north-east Italy, and Asti is a sweet, low-alcohol sparkling wine from Muscat in Piedmont. Sweetness labels such as Brut (dry) and Demi-Sec (medium-sweet) are often tested.

Sweet wines

  • Late harvest: grapes are left on the vine longer so their sugar concentrates; common in Alsace and Germany.
  • Noble rot: a fungus called Botrytis cinerea shrivels ripe grapes in misty, then sunny autumn conditions, giving honey and dried apricot notes; Sauternes and Tokaji are the classic examples.
  • Icewine (Eiswein in Germany): grapes freeze on the vine and are pressed frozen, so only a small amount of very sweet juice runs out; Canada and Germany are known for it.
  • Stopping fermentation: some wines keep natural sugar because the fermentation is stopped before all the sugar is used, as in many German Rieslings.

Fortified wines

Fortified wines have grape spirit added. Timing is the key difference. In Port, spirit is added during fermentation, which stops it and leaves natural sugar, so Port is sweet and full-bodied; Ruby styles are fruity and deep in colour, Tawny styles are aged longer in wood and show nutty, caramel notes. In Sherry, from the south of Spain, spirit is added after fermentation, so the base wine is dry. Fino ages under a layer of yeast called flor and is pale, dry and light; Oloroso ages without flor, in contact with oxygen, and becomes darker with nutty, toffee notes. Sweet Sherries are made by sweetening a dry Sherry.

Storage and service

Store bottles in a cool, dark place with a steady temperature; keep bottles sealed with a cork lying down so the cork stays moist. Serving temperature is a common question:

StyleServing temperature
Sweet winesWell chilled, about 6–8 °C
Sparkling winesWell chilled, about 6–10 °C
Light and medium-bodied whitesChilled, about 7–10 °C
Full-bodied, oaked whitesLightly chilled, about 10–13 °C
Light-bodied redsLightly chilled, about 13 °C
Medium and full-bodied redsRoom temperature, about 15–18 °C

Decanting separates a wine from its sediment, typical of old reds and some Ports. A wine smelling of wet cardboard is faulty because of cork taint. Open bottles keep longer in the fridge and with as little air as possible.

Tasting with the Level 2 Systematic Approach

There is no tasting exam at Level 2, but questions can ask about the structure of a tasting note and what each term means. The approach has three stages.

  1. Appearance: clarity, intensity of colour (pale, medium, deep) and colour, for example lemon or gold for whites, ruby or garnet for reds.
  2. Nose: intensity of the aromas and their characteristics, such as fruit, floral, spice, oak or developed notes.
  3. Palate: sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity, flavour characteristics and finish, followed by a simple conclusion on quality.

Tannin is a drying, grippy feeling on the gums, not a flavour. Acidity makes your mouth water. Exam questions often check that you can tell these two apart.

Practice questions answered and explained

Question: A wine from a cool climate is most likely to have which combination? Answer: high acidity, lighter body and lower alcohol. Slow ripening keeps acid in the grape and limits sugar, so the wine has less alcohol and less body.

Question: Which technique gives bread and biscuit aromas in Champagne? Answer: ageing on the lees after the second fermentation in the bottle. The dead yeast cells slowly release flavours and texture into the wine.

Question: Why does a very sweet dessert make a dry wine taste sour? Answer: sweetness in food makes a wine seem drier and more acidic, so the wine should be at least as sweet as the dish.

Question: What is the main difference between Port and Sherry production? Answer: spirit is added to Port during fermentation, keeping natural sugar, while Sherry is fortified after fermentation, so its base wine is dry.

Question: A German label says Spätlese. What does it describe? Answer: a level of ripeness of the grapes at harvest, riper than Kabinett. It does not by itself tell you whether the wine is dry or sweet.

Who WSET Level 2 suits best

  • Wine lovers who want to understand what they buy and order, without a trade background.
  • Restaurant, bar and shop staff who need to talk confidently about the main wines on a list or shelf.
  • Hospitality and tourism students looking for a widely recognised starting point.
  • Anyone planning to continue to Level 3, which builds directly on this knowledge.

If you are not sure whether to start at Level 1 or Level 2, ask yourself whether you already drink a variety of wines and know a few grape names. If you do, Level 2 is usually the right first step; Level 1 is a shorter introduction for complete beginners.
