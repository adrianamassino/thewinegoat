The grape varieties to know for WSET Level 3

In short

The grape varieties to know for WSET Level 3: where each grows, the styles it makes and how to link grape, climate and winemaking in your exam answers.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

At Level 3 knowing the name of a grape is not enough: you have to explain why it tastes the way it does in a given place. Each variety reacts to climate, soil and winemaking choices, and the exam rewards that reasoning.

Start from the international varieties below, then add the local grapes of each region as you study it.

How to study grapes the Level 3 way

  • Learn each grape as a profile: colour, acidity, tannin, typical aromas, how it responds to oak.
  • Link it to climate: the same grape in a cool and a warm region gives two different wines – know both.
  • Add the winemaking: e.g. Chardonnay with or without malolactic conversion, lees and new oak.
  • Test yourself with mixed questions: grape → region, region → grape, style → grape.

Local grapes not to forget

Examiners love regional grapes: Albariño (Rías Baixas), Verdejo (Rueda), Assyrtiko (Santorini), Grüner Veltliner (Austria), Furmint (Tokaj), Torrontés (Salta), Pinotage (South Africa), Carmenère (Chile), Zinfandel (California), Touriga Nacional (Douro), Aglianico, Fiano and Nero d'Avola (southern Italy). Learn them with their region and their main style.

Why grapes are the starting point of every Level 3 answer

At Level 3, a grape variety is not a name to memorise but a set of characteristics that explains a wine. Each variety brings its own natural acidity, skin thickness, tannin, aromatic intensity and ripening timetable. Those characteristics decide where the grape can ripen successfully, what problems the grower has to manage and which winemaking choices make sense. When an exam question asks why a wine tastes as it does, the grape is almost always part of the answer.

This matters in both units of the qualification. In the theory unit, which combines 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written answers, grape knowledge appears in identification questions and in explanations of regional styles. In the tasting unit, which is assessed separately and also needs at least 55%, you do not have to name the grape, but knowing typical profiles helps you judge structure confidently and reach realistic conclusions. If you fail one unit, you retake only that one, so solid grape knowledge supports you twice.

How to read a grape profile

Before memorising individual varieties, learn the six questions that describe any grape. Once you can answer them for a variety, you can predict most of its behaviour in the vineyard and in the glass.

  1. When does it bud and ripen? Early-budding grapes risk spring frost; late-ripening grapes need a long, warm season to reach full ripeness.
  2. How thick are its skins? Thick skins give more colour and tannin and resist rot; thin skins give paler wines and are vulnerable in wet weather.
  3. What is its natural acidity? High-acid grapes keep freshness in warm climates and support sweet and sparkling styles.
  4. How aromatic is it? Aromatic grapes are usually made without new oak to protect their character; neutral grapes respond well to oak, lees and malolactic conversion.
  5. How much tannin does it give? This decides the structure of black grape wines and often their ageing potential.
  6. What are its vulnerabilities? Disease, uneven ripening or poor fruit set explain many regional choices.

With these six answers in place, every regional fact becomes logical. A thin-skinned, early-ripening black grape belongs in cool regions; a late-ripening, thick-skinned one needs warmth; a high-acid white can make anything from bone-dry to lusciously sweet wine.

The main white varieties in depth

Chardonnay

Chardonnay is early budding, so spring frost is a real risk in cool regions such as Chablis. It is relatively neutral in aroma, which is why it reflects both climate and winemaking so clearly. In cool climates it shows green apple, citrus and high acidity; in moderate climates, stone fruit and melon; in warm climates, tropical fruit with lower acidity. Winemakers can shape it with malolactic conversion for creamy, buttery notes, lees contact for texture and oak for vanilla and toast. It is also a key grape in traditional method sparkling wines, including Blanc de Blancs Champagne.

Sauvignon Blanc

Sauvignon Blanc is aromatic with high acidity, showing green notes such as grass, green bell pepper and asparagus alongside gooseberry, citrus and, in warmer sites, passion fruit. Its aromas are best preserved by cool fermentation in stainless steel and early bottling, which is the typical approach in Marlborough and in the Central Vineyards of the Loire, such as Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé, where the style is usually more restrained. In Bordeaux it is often blended with Sémillon, sometimes fermented and aged in oak, and the same blend is the basis of sweet wines affected by noble rot.

Riesling

Riesling is highly aromatic, with very high acidity, and it is particularly good at expressing its site. It is late ripening and has hard wood that resists winter cold, which suits cool regions with long autumns. It shows green fruit, citrus, stone fruit and floral notes, and with bottle age it develops honey, toast and petrol notes. It is rarely given new oak flavour. Its acidity allows every level of sweetness, from bone dry to lusciously sweet wines made from grapes affected by noble rot or frozen on the vine. Key regions include the Mosel and the Rheingau, Alsace, Austria and Australia's Clare and Eden Valleys, where the dry style often shows lime.

Chenin Blanc

Chenin Blanc has high acidity and moderate aroma, and it ripens unevenly, so growers often pick in several passes. That flexibility explains the extraordinary range of styles in the Loire: sparkling wines from barely ripe grapes, dry and off-dry wines such as Vouvray and Savennières, and sweet wines from shrivelled or nobly rotten grapes such as Coteaux du Layon. In South Africa, where it is widely planted, it gives both fresh, fruity wines and richer, sometimes oaked styles from old vines.

Sémillon, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and Viognier

Sémillon has thin skins and is susceptible to noble rot, which makes it the main grape of Sauternes; in Australia's Hunter Valley it is picked early to make light, low-alcohol dry wines that develop toast and honey notes with bottle age without oak. Gewurztraminer has pink skins, low to medium acidity, high alcohol and intense lychee, rose and sweet spice aromas, with Alsace as its classic home. Pinot Gris in Alsace gives rich, full-bodied wines, while the same grape as Pinot Grigio in north-east Italy usually gives light, neutral, fresh wines picked earlier. Viognier, the grape of Condrieu in the northern Rhône, is full-bodied with apricot, peach and blossom aromas, and is low in acidity, so picking at the right moment is crucial.

The main black varieties in depth

Pinot Noir

Pinot Noir buds and ripens early and has thin skins, so it suits cool to moderate climates and is vulnerable to rot. It gives pale to medium colour, red fruit such as strawberry, raspberry and red cherry, low to medium tannin and medium to high acidity, developing forest floor and mushroom notes with age. Its classic home is Burgundy, and it is an essential grape in Champagne. Strong New World examples come from Central Otago, Oregon and cooler Californian areas, and Germany grows it as Spätburgunder.

Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon buds and ripens late, so it needs a warm site to ripen fully; when underripe it shows herbaceous green bell pepper notes. Its small, thick-skinned berries give deep colour, high tannin and high acidity, with blackcurrant, black cherry, mint and cedar. It is usually aged in oak and has excellent ageing potential. On the Left Bank of Bordeaux, it thrives on warm gravel soils and is blended with Merlot and Cabernet Franc. Other key regions include Napa Valley, Coonawarra, Margaret River and Chile.

Merlot

Merlot ripens earlier than Cabernet Sauvignon and gives wines with softer tannin, medium acidity and plum, black cherry and chocolate flavours. It suits the cooler clay soils of Bordeaux's Right Bank, in Saint-Émilion and Pomerol, where it dominates the blend. It is also widely planted in Chile, Washington and northern Italy, often made in a soft, fruity style for early drinking.

Syrah or Shiraz

Syrah gives deep colour, medium to high tannin and black fruit. In the moderate climate of the northern Rhône, at Hermitage and Côte-Rôtie, it shows blackberry, black pepper, violet and savoury notes. As Shiraz in warm Australian regions such as the Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale, it is riper and fuller, with jammy black fruit, chocolate and often sweet spice from oak. Cooler Australian, New Zealand and South African sites give styles closer to the Rhône.

Grenache

Grenache is late ripening, tolerant of heat and drought and well suited to bush vines in warm, dry climates. It accumulates sugar easily, so its wines are high in alcohol, while its relatively thin skins give lighter colour and softer tannin. It shows red fruit such as strawberry and raspberry, with spice, and develops leather and dried fruit notes with age. It is the backbone of Southern Rhône blends such as Châteauneuf-du-Pape, an important grape in Priorat and Navarra, and widely used for rosé.

Tempranillo, Sangiovese and Nebbiolo

Tempranillo, whose name refers to its early ripening, has thick skins and gives medium acidity and tannin with red fruit, and it responds very well to long oak ageing. It is the main grape of Rioja and, under local names, of Ribera del Duero and Toro, and in Portugal it is used in the Douro. Sangiovese is late ripening with high acidity and medium to high tannin, red cherry, plum and herbal notes; it is the grape of Chianti Classico and of Brunello di Montalcino. Nebbiolo buds early and ripens late, needing the best sites; it gives pale colour that quickly turns garnet, yet high acidity and high tannin, with red cherry, rose and tar notes and great ageing potential in Barolo and Barbaresco.

Malbec and Zinfandel

Malbec gives deep colour, black fruit and violet, with ripe, soft tannins in the high-altitude vineyards of Mendoza, where strong sunlight and cool nights deepen colour and preserve freshness; in Cahors it is more tannic and rustic. Zinfandel, known as Primitivo in southern Italy, ripens unevenly, with raisined and unripe berries in the same bunch, and accumulates high sugar levels, so its wines are often high in alcohol with jammy fruit.

A good habit is to write one sentence per grape in the blend, explaining its contribution, whenever a written question asks about a blended wine. That simple structure usually collects most of the available marks.

Local grapes, region by region

Beyond the international varieties, each region has its own grapes that examiners like to test. Group them by country and attach one key style to each.

  • Spain: Albariño in Rías Baixas, aromatic with high acidity and peach notes; Verdejo in Rueda, fresh and herbal; Monastrell in the warm south-east, powerful and ripe.
  • Portugal: Touriga Nacional in the Douro, deeply coloured and floral, for Port and dry reds; Alvarinho in the north for fresh, aromatic whites; Baga in Bairrada, high in acidity and tannin.
  • Italy: Garganega for Soave; Glera for Prosecco; Barbera, high in acidity with low tannin; Corvina for Valpolicella and Amarone; Aglianico, late ripening and tannic, in Taurasi; Nero d'Avola in Sicily; Fiano and Greco in Campania.
  • Austria and Hungary: Grüner Veltliner, with white pepper and green fruit; Blaufränkisch, with black fruit and spice; Furmint, high in acidity and susceptible to noble rot, for Tokaji.
  • Greece: Assyrtiko on Santorini, with high acidity in a hot, windy, volcanic setting; Xinomavro in Naoussa, high in acidity and tannin; Agiorgitiko in Nemea, soft and fruity.
  • Southern hemisphere: Pinotage in South Africa; Carmenère in Chile; Torrontés in Argentina's Salta, intensely floral.
  • France beyond the classics: Melon for Muscadet; Cabernet Franc in the Loire; Gamay in Beaujolais.

Grapes that are easy to confuse

Some pairs cause repeated mistakes in multiple-choice questions. Learning the key difference between them is quicker than relearning both.

  • Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon: both in white Bordeaux, but Sauvignon gives acidity and aroma while Sémillon gives body and susceptibility to noble rot.
  • Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc: Franc is lighter, more fragrant and earlier ripening, which is why it succeeds in the Loire.
  • Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio: the same grape, but the names usually signal different styles, rich in Alsace and light in north-east Italy.
  • Grenache and Garnacha: the same grape under French and Spanish names, not two varieties.
  • Nebbiolo and Sangiovese: both high in acidity and tannin, but Nebbiolo is paler, with rose and tar, and belongs to Piedmont, while Sangiovese is Tuscan, with red cherry and herbal notes.
  • Malbec and Carmenère: both important in South America, Malbec in Argentina and Carmenère in Chile, where it was long mistaken for Merlot.

A practice question with a model answer

Question of our own: explain why Pinot Noir is grown in Burgundy and Central Otago rather than in very hot regions (4 marks).

A strong answer: Pinot Noir buds and ripens early, so it can reach ripeness in regions with a short or cool growing season. In hot climates it would ripen too quickly, losing acidity and its delicate red fruit aromas and giving jammy, flabby wines. Its thin skins give limited colour and tannin, so slow ripening is needed to develop flavour complexity before sugar levels become too high. Cool to moderate climates such as Burgundy, which is continental, and Central Otago, which has high sunshine and cool nights, preserve acidity and fresh red fruit, giving the elegant style the grape is known for.

Notice how every sentence starts from a characteristic of the grape and ends with an effect on the wine. That is the pattern to use for any grape question.

A study routine for grapes

  • Make one card per major grape with its six-question profile on one side and two contrasting regions on the other.
  • Each day, review a handful of cards and test yourself in three directions: grape to region, region to grape, style to grape.
  • When you study a region, add its local grapes to your deck and link each one to a style.
  • Once a week, write a short paragraph explaining how one grape changes between a cool and a warm region.
  • Taste whenever you can with the profile in mind: check whether the acidity, tannin and aromas match what you expected.

Frequently asked questions

How many grape varieties are there in WSET Level 3?

There is no single fixed list to memorise: the course covers the main international varieties in depth and many local grapes region by region. The key is to know their style and how place and winemaking change it.

What is the best way to memorise grapes for WSET 3?

Short daily practice with mixed questions (grape ↔ region ↔ style) plus flashcards works better than reading lists. The Wine Goat repeats the grapes you get wrong until you know them.

Do I need to know grape varieties for the tasting?

You are not asked to name the grape in the tasting exam, but knowing grape profiles helps you describe structure and reach the right quality conclusions.

Do I need to know every grape variety mentioned in the WSET Level 3 course?

You need the major international varieties in depth, with their profile, key regions and how climate and winemaking change them. Local grapes are learned region by region, usually with their main style and where they grow. Prioritise depth on the main varieties first, then build the local grapes as you study each region, using mixed questions to keep them all active.

How do grape characteristics help in the written answers?

Most written questions ask why a wine has a particular style. Grape characteristics such as acidity, skin thickness, ripening time and aromatic intensity are often the first link in that explanation. Starting a sentence with a grape characteristic and ending it with its effect on the wine is a simple, reliable way to turn knowledge into marks.

Why do some grapes have several names?

Grapes spread across countries over centuries and took local names along the way. Grenache is Garnacha in Spain and Cannonau in Sardinia; Tempranillo has several regional names in Spain and Portugal; Zinfandel and Primitivo are the same grape. Exam questions and labels may use any of these names, so it is worth learning synonyms in both directions.

Which grapes are most often confused by Level 3 candidates?

Common confusions include Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc, Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon in white Bordeaux, and Nebbiolo and Sangiovese, which share high acidity and tannin but differ in colour, aromas and region. Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio are the same grape with names that usually suggest different styles. Learning the single key difference between each pair is the fastest fix.

Will knowing grapes help me in the Level 3 tasting unit?

Yes, indirectly. You are not asked to name the grape, but knowing typical profiles helps you judge structure with confidence, for example expecting high acidity in a cool-climate Riesling or high tannin in a young Cabernet Sauvignon, and reach realistic conclusions. The tasting unit is marked separately from theory and needs at least 55% on its own.
