WSET Level 3 mock exam: free practice test

A mock exam is the closest thing to exam day: the same mix of topics, the same time pressure and the same traps. Doing several before the WSET Level 3 exam shows you where you lose marks and trains your timing for the 50 multiple-choice questions and the 4 written answers.

Try the mini mock below: 10 questions from different chapters, marked instantly. In the app you can take full timed mock exams with multiple-choice questions, short written answers and a blind tasting note, as many times as you like.

Sample questions with answers

1 Which vine species produces nearly all wine grapes?

Show the answer

Answer: Vitis vinifera

Why: V. vinifera is the main Eurasian species, used to make wine for several thousand years.

2 In which year after planting does the first crop usually come?

Show the answer

Answer: The third year

Why: Young pre-grafted vines are often protected by plastic sleeves; irrigation is sometimes allowed for establishment.

3 Why does white juice usually spend little time on the skins?

Show the answer

Answer: To reduce the risk of oxidation

Why: Whole-bunch pressing limits contact further, gently, potentially giving purer, more delicate wines.

4 In red winemaking, when does pressing normally happen?

Show the answer

Answer: After fermentation

Why: Skins stay in the vat to extract colour and tannin. In reds MLF is standard practice.

5 Between which towns are the Northern Rhône vineyards?

Show the answer

Answer: Between Vienne in the north and Valence in the south

Why: They are a small share of Rhône production but home to many premium wines.

6 How are inexpensive Spanish reds often made to enhance fruit?

Show the answer

Answer: Unoaked, with semi-carbonic maceration

Why: The best almost always see oak, partly new; traditionally American, now French too.

7 What climate do most German wine regions have?

Show the answer

Answer: Cool continental

Why: Baden in the south is noticeably warmer. The long cool ripening gives sugar while retaining acidity.

8 What may appear on the label of a US wine outside any AVA?

Show the answer

Answer: A state (e.g. California) or county (e.g. Napa County) name

Why: Federal laws set minimum percentages for variety, vintage and AVA; many states have their own laws. Non-AVA rules are often more lenient.

9 In which towns must Sherry be matured?

Show the answer

Answer: Jerez, Sanlúcar or El Puerto

Why: Current rule: Jerez, Sanlúcar or El Puerto. A reform awaiting EU approval would allow ageing anywhere in the DO, except Fino in Sanlúcar (that is Manzanilla).

10 What are the four key geographical features of Chile's vineyards, west to east?

Show the answer

Answer: Pacific Ocean, Coastal Range, Central Valley, Andes

Why: Vineyards span over 900 km north-south but rarely 100 km east-west.

How to run a full WSET Level 3 mock exam at home

A mock exam only works if it feels like the real thing. Reading questions on the sofa with notes nearby measures your memory with help, not your readiness. The routine below turns an ordinary evening into a realistic rehearsal of the theory paper and, when you have wines available, of the tasting unit too.

  1. Choose a time when you can work for the full duration without interruptions, ideally at the same time of day as your real exam.
  2. Clear the table: no notes, no phone, only pens, paper and a watch or timer you can see.
  3. Print or open the questions and set the timer for the full paper before reading anything.
  4. Write your answers to the 50 multiple-choice questions on a separate sheet, so you can mark them afterwards without seeing your first instinct circled on the question.
  5. Write the four short answers by hand. Typing is faster and hides the real problem of handwriting under time pressure.
  6. Stop when the timer stops, even if a question is unfinished. The unfinished answer is valuable information.
  7. If you include tasting, pour two wines that someone else has chosen and covered, and write a full SAT note for each within a fixed time.

Ask a friend to choose and cover the wines for your mock tasting. If you poured them yourself, your conclusions will be shaped by what you already know, and the exercise loses most of its value.

This is a long session, which is exactly the point. Concentration is a skill, and the last written answer on exam day is written by someone who has already been working for well over an hour. If you cannot set aside a whole block, split the mock into a theory session and a tasting session on two different days, but keep each part complete and timed.

How to mark your mock and review it properly

The mark is the least interesting result of a mock exam. What matters is why you lost each mark, because each cause has a different remedy. Keep an error log: a simple table where every wrong or doubtful answer is recorded with its cause. After two or three mocks, patterns appear that no single score can show you.

Cause of the errorHow you recognise itRemedy
Knowledge gapYou did not know the fact at allStudy the topic, then answer ten questions on it within the week
ConfusionYou mixed up two similar regions, grapes or termsBuild a side-by-side comparison table of the two
MisreadingYou knew the answer but missed NOT, main or typicallyUnderline qualifiers in every practice question for a week
Incomplete written answerCorrect points, but sub-parts missing or unexplainedPlan every answer in keywords before writing
TimingLast questions rushed or blankPractise with a stricter time split; use a visible timer
Tasting calibrationStructural levels one step offTaste with a partner and compare levels wine by wine

Give each mock the same review routine: mark, log, choose the three most frequent causes and turn them into actions for the coming week. A mock that is not reviewed is mostly a lost evening.

Mini case study: three mock exams, three lessons

Consider a typical candidate, whom we will call Sara, preparing for WSET Level 3 alongside a full-time job. Her first mock, taken six weeks before the exam, gave a strong multiple-choice result but weak written answers: she knew the regions, yet her answers were lists of facts with no explanation. Her error log showed that most lost marks were in explain questions where she had described instead of explaining.

For the next two weeks she wrote one short answer a day, forcing herself to include a because in every sentence. Her second mock showed clear progress in the written answers but revealed a new problem: she ran out of time and left the last sub-part blank. She then fixed a time limit for each written question and started using a visible timer in practice.

Her third mock, two weeks before the exam, was balanced across all parts, and the remaining errors were mostly confusions between neighbouring regions, such as the different appellations of the Northern Rhône. She spent the last days on comparison tables. The lesson is that each mock revealed a different weakness, and none of them would have been visible from reading notes alone.

More sample questions, answered and explained

Here are ten further questions in the style of the multiple-choice section, written as text so you can test yourself before reading each answer. Cover the answer, decide, then check the explanation.

Question 1: Which grape is used for the white wines of Sancerre? Answer: Sauvignon Blanc. Sancerre lies in the Central Vineyards of the Loire, where a cool continental climate gives wines with high acidity, light to medium body and green fruit and herbaceous notes, usually without noticeable oak.

Question 2: What is the main grape of Barolo? Answer: Nebbiolo. It gives wines that are often pale in colour but high in acidity and tannin, with aromas of red cherry, roses and, with age, tar and dried fruit. The firm structure explains why Barolo requires long ageing before release.

Question 3: What is the purpose of the solera system in Sherry production? Answer: fractional blending, so that young wine refreshes older wine over many years. The result is a consistent house style from year to year rather than a vintage-dated wine.

Question 4: How does Prosecco usually obtain its bubbles? Answer: by the tank method, with the second fermentation in a sealed pressurised tank. The method is relatively quick and preserves the fresh, fruity and floral primary aromas of the Glera grape, without the autolytic notes of long lees ageing.

Question 5: Why does a large diurnal range benefit grapes in warm regions? Answer: warm days allow sugar to accumulate and the grapes to ripen, while cool nights slow the loss of acidity and help preserve aromatic compounds. The resulting wines combine ripe fruit with freshness.

Question 6: What conditions does noble rot need? Answer: humid, misty mornings that encourage the fungus to develop, followed by dry, sunny afternoons that stop it turning into harmful grey rot. These conditions are found in Sauternes, for example, where rivers create morning mists.

Question 7: How does tawny Port differ from ruby Port in its maturation? Answer: tawny is aged for long periods in small wooden casks, where gentle oxidation turns the colour towards brown and develops nutty, caramel and dried-fruit notes. Ruby styles are aged in large vessels to keep their deep colour and fresh red and black fruit.

Question 8: Which soil is the region of Coonawarra known for? Answer: terra rossa, a red soil over limestone, which is associated with structured Cabernet Sauvignon showing intense blackcurrant and often mint or eucalyptus notes.

Question 9: Which grape is mainly responsible for Tokaji Aszú? Answer: Furmint, a variety with high acidity that is susceptible to noble rot. The high acidity balances the very high sweetness of the wines and allows them to age for a long time.

Question 10: Why are the vineyards of Mendoza usually irrigated? Answer: the climate is very dry, and rainfall alone cannot support the vines. Water from Andean snowmelt is used, while high altitude gives cool nights that help retain acidity in Malbec and other varieties.

A sample short written answer, with the points that score

Practice question of our own: describe and explain the winemaking choices that create the style of a premium oaked Chardonnay from a moderate climate. Before reading the model points, try to plan your answer in keywords for three minutes.

  • Fermentation in oak barrels integrates oak flavours and gives a rounder texture than fermentation in stainless steel.
  • Maturation in new oak adds secondary aromas such as vanilla, toast and smoke; a share of older barrels keeps oak in balance with the fruit.
  • Malolactic conversion softens the acidity and can add a buttery note, adding to the richness of the style.
  • Lees contact and lees stirring add body, a creamy texture and savoury or biscuit notes.
  • The use of whole-bunch pressing or careful pressing limits phenolic extraction and keeps the wine fine.
  • The moderate climate allows ripe stone-fruit flavours while keeping enough acidity for balance and ageing potential.

Each bullet is a separate, explained point. An answer that simply said oak, malolactic and lees would mention three techniques but explain none of them, and would score only a fraction of the marks available.

Reading your mock score

A mock score is a snapshot, not a prediction. Questions written for practice are never identical to exam questions, and your score will vary with the mix of topics. Use it to compare yourself with yourself over time and to decide where to work, rather than to guess your final grade.

  • Below 55% in a part: that part is the priority for the coming week, whatever your overall result.
  • Just above 55%: you are close to the line; aim to build a safety margin, because exam-day nerves cost marks.
  • Comfortably above 55% everywhere: keep the routine, focus on reducing careless errors and on the completeness of written answers.
  • A big gap between multiple choice and written answers: the knowledge is there, the technique is not; practise explaining.
  • A falling score between two mocks: check the topic mix first, then whether fatigue or timing caused the drop.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a free WSET Level 3 mock exam?

Yes: the mini mock on this page is free, and the app includes mock exams with multiple-choice questions, written answers and tasting.

How long is the WSET Level 3 theory exam?

The theory exam combines 50 multiple-choice questions and 4 short written answer questions; check your course provider for the exact timing of your session.

How many mock exams should I do before WSET 3?

At least three or four full mock exams, spaced out, with a thorough review after each one.

How realistic are online WSET Level 3 mock exams?

A good mock reproduces the structure of the exam: multiple-choice questions across the whole syllabus, short written answers and a blind tasting note. The questions are written for practice, so they are never the real exam questions, but the format, the time pressure and the types of trap are similar. Their real value lies in training your timing and revealing your weak topics.

Should I include tasting in my mock exam?

Yes, whenever possible. The tasting unit is marked separately and needs 55% on its own, so it deserves rehearsal under the same conditions: two wines poured and covered by someone else, a fixed time and a complete SAT note for each. Doing it after the theory paper also trains you to taste well when you are already tired.

What should I do after a bad mock exam result?

Do not repeat a full mock straight away. Review it carefully, record each lost mark in an error log with its cause, and pick the two or three most frequent causes. Spend the next week working on them with short daily sessions, then take another mock to check the improvement. A weak early mock is useful because it shows you where to work.

Can I do a mock exam in my second language?

You can, and it can help if you will sit the exam in a language that is not your first. Practising in the exam language trains you to read questions precisely and to write technical terms correctly. The Wine Goat offers its questions in English, Italian, French and Spanish, so you can also compare an explanation in two languages when a term is unclear.

How long should I spend reviewing a mock exam?

Plan at least as long as the theory paper itself, and often more. Marking takes little time; the valuable part is understanding every wrong or doubtful answer, comparing your written answers with the expected points and turning the patterns into a plan for the next week. Candidates who review thoroughly usually improve faster than those who simply take more mocks.
