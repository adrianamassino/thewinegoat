WSET Level 3 Award in Wines: the complete guide

The qualification where wine stops being a list of facts and becomes a chain of causes. Level 3 asks you to explain why a wine tastes the way it does, to judge its quality and to describe it blind. The exam has a theory paper with multiple-choice questions and short written answers, and a tasting of two wines: demanding, but very manageable with a method.

The exam at a glance

Theory paper
50 multiple-choice questions + 4 short written answers (25 marks each), 2 hours
Tasting
2 still wines tasted blind, 30 minutes
Pass mark
55% in each theory part and 55% in the tasting
Study time
About 84 hours: 32.5 of guided learning (exams included) + 51.5 of private study
Prerequisites
None required; Level 2 or equivalent knowledge is recommended
Grades
Pass 55% · Merit 65% · Distinction 80% (at least 65% in each paper)

The two units are sat in the same session and in the same language. Each one is marked on its own, so a strong tasting cannot rescue a weak theory paper, and inside the theory unit the multiple-choice part and the written part must each reach 55%. A candidate who passes one unit and fails the other retakes only the failed unit.

What the syllabus covers

The course is built on six learning outcomes: five in the theory unit and one in the tasting unit. Here they are in plain words.

  • Outcome 1 – How the vineyard and the winery shape still wines: the vine and its needs, climate and weather, soils, vineyard management and hazards, and every winemaking choice from crushing to closure.
  • Outcome 2 – The still wines of the main regions of the world: where they are, their grapes, their styles and the natural, human, legal and commercial factors behind their style, quality and price.
  • Outcome 3 – Sparkling wines: Champagne, Crémant, Cava, Prosecco, Asti, Sekt and the best New World examples, with the production methods and labelling terms.
  • Outcome 4 – Fortified wines: Sherry, Port and fortified Muscats, how they are made and how their categories differ.
  • Outcome 5 – Advising customers and staff: recommending wines, storage and service, common faults, food and wine pairing and the risks of drinking too much.
  • Tasting unit – Describing a still wine accurately with the Level 3 Systematic Approach to Tasting and judging its quality and readiness for drinking.

More than half of the multiple-choice paper and 70 of the 100 written marks come from the regions, but the regions can only be explained with outcome 1: every written question asks you to use vineyard and winery knowledge to justify a style. In practice, questions 1 and 2 of the written paper draw on outcomes 1 and 2, question 3 adds a recommendation for a customer, and question 4 brings in sparkling or fortified wines.

How to study, step by step

  1. Learn outcome 1 before the regions

    Climate, ripening, yields, oak, malolactic conversion, lees: these are the causes you will quote in every region. A week spent on them makes the rest of the course faster.

  2. Build one card per region

    Climate and its causes, main grapes, key winemaking choices, label terms, typical style and price level. The same six headings, every time, turn the regions into a system.

  3. Answer multiple-choice questions every day

    Short daily sets fix the facts and train you to spot plausible wrong options. Review every mistake the next day and again a week later.

  4. Write one short answer a day

    Two or three sub-questions, timed, on paper. Mark them against key points: one correct, explained idea per mark. This is the skill most candidates neglect.

  5. Taste with full notes

    Write a complete note for every wine you taste, with the official terms and a justified conclusion. Calibrate your levels of acidity, tannin and body with a teacher or a study partner.

  6. Rehearse the real exam

    From the second half of the course, sit full timed papers: 50 questions and 4 written answers in two hours, then two wines in 30 minutes. Aim for 65% or more in every part to keep a margin.

Common mistakes

  • Revising only multiple choice: the written part needs 55% on its own
  • Listing facts without the because: written answers reward explained points
  • Learning regions one by one without linking them to climate and winemaking
  • Ignoring the marks per sub-question: 6 marks usually means 6 separate points
  • Writing tasting notes with personal words instead of the standard terms
  • Leaving sparkling, fortified wines and service to the last week: they are worth easy marks

Try 3 sample questions

These questions come from The Wine Goat question bank. They are our own practice questions, written in the style of the exam; they are not official or past exam questions.

1 How do wind machines protect a vineyard from frost?

Show the answer

They draw warmer air down from above to keep the ground above freezing — On frost nights the air a few metres up is warmer than the dense, cold air at ground level. The fans mix it down; some machines also include heaters.

2 A producer in the Barolo area wants to plant a black grape on their coolest site. Which choice is the most logical?

Show the answer

Dolcetto, because it ripens earlier than Nebbiolo and Barbera — Dolcetto ripens early, so it can succeed on cooler sites. Nebbiolo gets the best south-facing slopes, Barbera ripens late and Moscato is a white grape.

3 Which of these Sherries is naturally sweet rather than sweetened?

Show the answer

Pedro Ximénez — PX is made from sun-dried grapes whose very concentrated must ferments only a few degrees before fortification. Cream, Pale Cream and Medium are dry Sherries with a sweetening component added.

A short written answer with a model answer

Port.

Compare ruby and tawny Port: how each is matured and how each tastes. (6 marks)

Model answer

Ruby styles are matured for a short time in large old oak vats or stainless steel, which limits contact with oxygen. They keep a deep ruby colour and intense primary black fruit (one point for the vessel, one for the style). Within the family, LBV comes from a single year and spends four to six years in wood before bottling, while Vintage Port is bottled young, unfined and unfiltered, and develops in bottle for decades (one point each). Tawny styles are aged for a long time in small casks, where slow oxidation turns the colour to tawny and replaces fresh fruit with dried fruit, walnut, caramel and coffee (one point). Age-indicated tawnies (10, 20, 30, 40 years) are blends whose average age and character match the label, and they are ready to drink on release (one point).

Points that score marks: ruby: large old vats or steel, little oxygen; deep colour, primary black fruit; LBV single vintage, 4–6 years in wood; Vintage bottled early, unfined, ages in bottle, sediment; tawny: long ageing in small casks, oxidative; tawny colour, nuts, caramel, dried fruit; age statements = average age, ready when bottled

A tasting note example

Here is the kind of note the tasting unit rewards, written for an imaginary off-dry Riesling Kabinett from the Mosel. Every line uses one standard term, and the conclusions quote the description.

  • Appearance: clear; pale; lemon-green.
  • Nose: clean; medium(+) intensity; green apple, lemon, lime, white blossom, a wet-stone note; youthful.
  • Palate: off-dry; high acidity; low alcohol; light body; medium(+) flavour intensity with the same citrus and green fruit; medium(+) finish.
  • Quality: very good, because the sweetness is balanced by the high acidity, the fruit is intense and pure and the finish is long for such a light wine.
  • Readiness for drinking: can drink now, but has potential for ageing, because the acidity and the concentration of fruit will carry it for years while honey and toast develop.

Frequently asked questions

How hard is WSET Level 3?

It is a big step from Level 2: the syllabus covers the main wine regions of the world in depth, the written answers ask you to explain causes and the tasting is assessed blind. It is very achievable with regular study, daily practice questions, written answers marked on key points and plenty of tasting with full notes.

How many hours should I study for WSET Level 3?

The indicated total is about 84 hours: 32.5 hours of guided learning, including the exams, and 51.5 hours of private study. Many candidates spread the work over 10 to 12 weeks; aiming for Merit or Distinction usually means more private study, especially written answers and tasting.

Do I need Level 2 before Level 3?

It is not compulsory, but Level 2 or an equivalent level of knowledge is recommended. If you go straight to Level 3, make sure you already know the main grapes, regions and label terms, and discuss your level with your course provider before enrolling.

What is the pass mark for WSET Level 3?

You need at least 55% in each part of the theory unit, the multiple-choice questions and the written answers, and at least 55% in the tasting unit. An overall 65% gives a Merit, and 80% with at least 65% in each paper a Distinction.

How long is the WSET Level 3 exam?

The theory paper lasts two hours for 50 multiple-choice questions and four written questions worth 25 marks each. The tasting lasts 30 minutes for two still wines. Both units are sat in the same session and in the same language.

Can I resit only one unit?

Yes. If you pass one unit and fail the other, you resit only the failed unit, in the same language. There is no limit on the number of resits, but a resit candidate can reach a Merit at most.

Do I have to identify the wines in the tasting?

No. You describe two still wines with the Level 3 Systematic Approach to Tasting and reach conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking. Precise structural levels, relevant descriptors and consistent conclusions earn the marks.

Are the sample questions on this page official?

No. They come from The Wine Goat question bank: original practice questions written in the style of the exam. They are not official questions and not past exam papers.

What comes after WSET Level 3?

The Level 4 Diploma in Wines, which requires the Level 3 Award in Wines (or the Level 3 Award in Wines and Spirits) for entry. It is studied in six units and takes about 500 hours of study.

How the final grade is calculated

Once both units are passed, the qualification is awarded with one overall grade. Distinction requires both a high aggregate and no weak paper, so a candidate with an excellent theory result and a modest tasting can still finish with a Merit.

Overall resultGrade
80% or more, with at least 65% in each paperPass with Distinction
65% to 79%Pass with Merit
55% to 64%Pass
45% to 54%Fail
44% or lessFail unclassified

Two practical consequences follow. First, a resit is possible as many times as needed, but a candidate who resits a failed unit can reach Merit at most, so passing both units at the first attempt matters if you are aiming for a Distinction. Second, a candidate who passes one unit receives a record of that result, and the final grade is issued when the other unit is passed in the same language.

Candidates who are under the legal drinking age, or who choose not to taste alcohol, can study the course and sit the theory unit; they receive a record of that unit, but the full qualification requires the tasting unit.

Where the 84 hours go

The indicated total of about 84 hours includes 32.5 hours of guided learning with a tutor, two and a half of which are the exams, and 51.5 hours of private study. For most candidates private study is the variable part: people who start with Level 2 fresh in their memory need less, and anyone aiming for Distinction needs more. A realistic plan spreads the work over 10 to 12 weeks, with short daily sessions rather than long weekends.

What happens in the tasting unit

The tasting lasts 30 minutes for two still wines chosen from the styles in the syllabus. You are not asked to name the grape or the region: marks come from accurate levels of sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol and body, from relevant aroma and flavour descriptors and from conclusions that follow logically from what you wrote. Because the wines can come from anywhere in the syllabus, the best training is broad: taste the classic styles of every major region at least once, with a complete note each time.

Who Level 3 suits

  • Restaurant, shop and import staff who need to explain wines and recommend them with confidence.
  • Enthusiasts who enjoyed Level 2 and want to understand style, quality and price in depth.
  • Hospitality and wine-tourism professionals who want a respected intermediate qualification.
  • Future Diploma candidates: Level 3, or the Level 3 Award in Wines and Spirits, is the entry requirement for the Diploma.

Last-month checklist

  • I can explain the climate, grapes and style of every major region without notes.
  • I score 65% or more in each part of a timed theory paper: multiple choice and written answers.
  • I write my plan for each written question in keywords before I start.
  • I can write a complete tasting note for two wines in 30 minutes, with justified conclusions.
  • I know the sparkling methods, the Sherry and Port categories and the main label terms by heart.
  • I have checked the date, place, timetable and materials with my course provider.
