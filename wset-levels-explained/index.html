WSET levels explained: from Level 1 to the Diploma

In short

All WSET wine levels explained: who Level 1, 2, 3 and the Level 4 Diploma are for, how each exam works, how hard they are and which one to start with.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

WSET wine qualifications go from a one-day introduction to the Level 4 Diploma, one of the most respected wine qualifications in the world. Each level adds depth and a new kind of assessment.

Here is how they compare and how to choose where to start.

The four levels at a glance

Which level should you start with?

  • Never studied wine? Level 1 or go straight to Level 2 if you are motivated.
  • You already know the main grapes and regions? Level 2 is a quick start, Level 3 if you work in wine.
  • You hold Level 3 and work in the trade? The Diploma is the professional benchmark.

How the difficulty grows

Levels 1 and 2 test recognition: multiple-choice questions only. Level 3 adds written explanations and a blind tasting. The Diploma is a multi-unit programme that takes many months, with a much larger study load.

Study notes: each WSET wine level in detail

The overview above gives the big picture. These notes go level by level: what each course covers, how its exam is built, what score you need, how much study it realistically takes and what kind of thinking it asks for. They also include a worked example showing how the same wine is treated at each level, profiles to help you choose where to start, and sample questions in the style of each level. The WSET also offers qualifications in spirits and sake; this page covers the wine pathway only.

WSET Level 1 Award in Wines

Level 1 is an introduction for complete beginners and for people starting a job in hospitality. It is usually taught in a single day and ends with the exam the same day. The course explains how wine is made, the main types and styles of wine, a small set of key grape varieties and their typical styles, how to store and serve wine and the basic principles of food and wine pairing. It also introduces a simple way of describing wine by looking, smelling and tasting.

The exam is a multiple-choice paper of 30 questions to be answered in 45 minutes. The pass mark is 70%, which means at least 21 correct answers. The questions are direct and test recognition: which grape typically gives a certain style, which serving temperature suits a wine, what a sweet dessert does to a dry wine. There are no written answers and no tasting assessment. With a little revision after the course, most motivated candidates find the exam manageable.

WSET Level 2 Award in Wines

Level 2 is the first level that gives a real overview of the wine world. It suits enthusiasts who want structure and trade staff in shops, restaurants and distribution. The course is short, but most candidates add a few weeks of personal revision. It covers the factors that influence style and quality, from climate to winemaking, the principal grape varieties and a wider set of regional varieties, the main wine regions of the world, sparkling, sweet and fortified wines, how to read labels and how to pair food and wine.

The exam has 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes. You pass with 55%, achieve a Merit with 70% (35 correct answers) and a Distinction with 85% (43 correct answers). Like Level 1, it tests recognition and understanding through multiple choice only, but the questions are more numerous and more specific: a typical question links a region to its grape and style, or a technique to its effect on the wine.

WSET Level 3 Award in Wines

Level 3 is where the qualification becomes demanding. It is aimed at serious enthusiasts and wine professionals, and it is widely regarded as a professional standard in the trade. The course is longer and goes deeper into viticulture, winemaking and the regions, with an emphasis on explaining why a wine tastes the way it does. A common estimate is around 84 hours of study in total, including the course, personal study and tasting practice.

The exam has two units marked separately. The theory unit combines 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written-answer questions, each broken into sub-parts. The tasting unit asks you to assess two wines blind and write a full tasting note for each using the Systematic Approach to Tasting, ending with conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking. You need at least 55% in each unit, and better results can earn a Merit or a Distinction. If you fail one unit, you normally resit only that unit.

WSET Level 4 Diploma in Wines

The Diploma is an expert-level qualification for people who work in wine or plan to. It is structured in six units: D1 wine production, D2 wine business, D3 still wines of the world, D4 sparkling wines, D5 fortified wines and D6, an independent research assignment. It is commonly estimated at around 500 hours of study and many candidates take 18 months or more to complete it. Successful candidates can use the post-nominal DipWSET.

The Diploma requires Level 3 as a starting point and expects analysis rather than description: you explain the reasons behind style, quality and price, compare examples and reach conclusions. For people who want to go further still, the Master of Wine is awarded by a separate organisation, the Institute of Masters of Wine.

The same wine at four levels: a worked example

The best way to understand how the levels differ is to see how one topic grows. Take Chablis, the cool-climate Chardonnay from northern Burgundy.

  • Level 1: Chablis is a white wine made from Chardonnay. A typical question asks you to match Chardonnay to its range of styles, from fresh and citrus to rich and oaked.
  • Level 2: Chablis is a cool-climate Chardonnay with high acidity, green apple and citrus fruit, usually unoaked or with little new oak. A typical question asks which style is expected from Chardonnay grown in a cool climate.
  • Level 3: you explain the style. Chablis is the most northerly part of Burgundy, with a cool continental climate, so grapes ripen slowly and keep high acidity; spring frost is a major risk and producers use measures such as sprinklers or heaters; many producers avoid new oak to preserve the fresh fruit, while some premier and grand cru wines see oak and age well. In the tasting unit you might describe such a wine and justify its quality.
  • Diploma: you analyse and evaluate. Why do some producers choose oak and others not? How do frost losses affect volumes and prices? How do the different classifications influence quality and value, and how does the wine compete with cool-climate Chardonnay from other countries?

The facts barely change from one level to the next. What changes is what you are asked to do with them: recognise, then understand, then explain, then weigh and judge. This is why candidates who learn Level 3 regions as chains of cause and effect find the Diploma much easier to start.

Which level fits you? Four profiles

The complete beginner in hospitality

Marco has just started as a waiter and has never studied wine. He needs confidence to recommend a bottle and to serve it correctly. Level 1 gives him exactly that in one day: styles, key grapes, service and simple pairing. If he enjoys it, Level 2 is the natural next step a few months later.

The curious enthusiast

Claire drinks wine regularly, reads about it and already knows several grapes and regions. Level 1 would mostly repeat what she knows, so she starts directly with Level 2, which organises her knowledge and adds the regions she has never explored. After a year of tasting widely, she may consider Level 3.

The trade professional

Javier works in a wine shop and holds Level 2. Customers ask him why one Rioja costs more than another and why some wines age better. Level 3 is the right choice: it teaches him to explain style and quality, and the blind tasting trains him to describe wines precisely, which helps every day at work.

The career builder

Anna has Level 3 with a Merit and works as a sommelier. She wants a qualification that shows expert knowledge, including the business side. The Diploma suits her, provided she can plan for a long programme over many months. Before enrolling, she strengthens her Level 3 foundations and starts tasting weekly with written notes.

Can you skip a level?

Many people skip Level 1 and start with Level 2, especially if they already drink wine with some curiosity. Starting with Level 3 without Level 2 is possible when you already have equivalent knowledge, but it is a big step: the course assumes you know the principal grapes and regions and moves quickly. The Diploma, by contrast, requires Level 3. If you are unsure, take a short level test and try a set of Level 2 questions: if you score well without studying, you are probably ready for Level 3.

How much time to leave between levels

TransitionTypical gapWhat to do in between
Level 1 to Level 2From a few weeks to a few monthsTaste the key grapes side by side, read labels in shops
Level 2 to Level 3Several months is commonTaste widely across regions, practise describing wines with a structured method
Level 3 to DiplomaOften six months to a year or moreWeekly blind tasting with written notes, regional notes with causes and effects, reading trade news

There is no fixed rule. The gap matters less than what you do in it: regular tasting and short daily revision make the next level feel like a continuation instead of a fresh start.

Common misconceptions about the WSET levels

  • Level 1 is just tasting wine. In reality it has a short exam that tests clear knowledge of styles, grapes, service and pairing.
  • Level 2 is easy because it is only multiple choice. The 50 questions are specific, and confusing similar regions or grapes is the main cause of lost marks.
  • Level 3 is Level 2 with more regions. The written answers and the blind tasting change the nature of the exam: you must explain and describe, not only recognise.
  • You must identify the wine in the Level 3 tasting. You are assessed on an accurate description and justified conclusions, not on naming the grape or region.
  • The Diploma is just more facts. The main change is depth of analysis, plus a business unit and a research assignment.
  • You need to work in wine to take any level. All levels are open to enthusiasts; only the time commitment changes.

Sample questions in the style of each level

These are our own example questions, written to show how the style of questioning evolves. They are not taken from any exam.

Level 1 style. Question: which grape is known for a light colour, red fruit and low tannin? Answer: Pinot Noir. It is a thin-skinned black grape, so it gives paler wines with less tannin than Cabernet Sauvignon or Syrah.

Level 2 style. Question: which style would you expect from Sauvignon Blanc grown in Marlborough, New Zealand? Answer: a dry, high-acid white with pronounced aromas of passion fruit, gooseberry and green pepper, usually unoaked. Marlborough's sunny days and cool nights keep acidity and intense varietal aromas.

Level 3 style. Question: explain why many producers of premium Barolo use large old oak casks rather than small new barrels. Answer: Nebbiolo already has high tannin and high acidity, so producers often want to avoid adding the extra oak tannin and strong vanilla and toast flavours of new barrels. Large old casks allow slow oxygen exposure, which softens the tannin over time, while preserving the grape's red cherry, rose and tar aromas. Some modern producers do use small barrels, which give a rounder style with more noticeable oak.

Diploma style. Question: to what extent does the use of oak determine the quality and price of Barolo? An answer would weigh several factors, including site, vintage, producer reputation, ageing requirements and market demand, give examples of different approaches and reach a reasoned conclusion. It is an essay-type argument rather than a list of facts.

Glossary of WSET level vocabulary

  • Award: the name used for Levels 1, 2 and 3 in wines; the Diploma is a separate, larger qualification.
  • Unit: a part of a qualification that is examined and graded separately, as in the two units of Level 3 or the six units of the Diploma.
  • Systematic Approach to Tasting (SAT): the structured method for describing appearance, nose, palate and conclusions, used from Level 2 onwards and assessed in writing from Level 3.
  • Blind tasting: tasting a wine without seeing the label; at Level 3 the goal is accurate description and justified conclusions, not identification.
  • Merit and Distinction: higher grades awarded for stronger results, for example at Levels 2 and 3.
  • Resit: taking a failed exam or unit again; at Level 3 you normally resit only the unit you failed.
  • Course provider: the school that teaches the course and organises the exam session.
  • DipWSET: the post-nominal letters that Diploma graduates can use.

Checklist before you enrol

  • You know why you want the qualification: personal interest, a first job in hospitality, a promotion or a career change.
  • You have tried a few practice questions to check whether you are starting at the right level.
  • You have estimated the study time you can give each week and compared it with the level's typical load.
  • For Level 3 and the Diploma, you have planned regular tasting practice, not only theory.
  • You have checked the course format, the exam date and the resit arrangements with your course provider.
  • You have a simple daily revision habit ready, such as 15 minutes of questions or flashcards.

Frequently asked questions

How many WSET wine levels are there?

Four: Level 1, Level 2, Level 3 and the Level 4 Diploma in Wines.

Do I have to take the WSET levels in order?

Level 3 is recommended after Level 2 or equivalent knowledge, and the Diploma requires Level 3. Many people skip Level 1.

Which WSET level is enough to work in wine?

Level 2 is a good foundation for service and retail; Level 3 is widely seen as the professional standard.

What is the difference between WSET Level 2 and Level 3?

Level 2 is examined only by 50 multiple-choice questions and tests whether you recognise and understand grapes, regions and styles. Level 3 adds four short written answers and a blind tasting of two wines, so you must explain why wines taste as they do and describe them accurately. It needs much more study and tasting practice: around 84 hours in total is a common estimate.

Can I start WSET at Level 3?

It is possible if you already have knowledge equivalent to Level 2, but it is a demanding start. The Level 3 course assumes you know the principal grapes, the main regions and how to describe a wine, and it moves quickly. If you are unsure, test yourself on a set of Level 2 questions: a comfortable score without revision suggests you are ready for Level 3.

How long does it take to go from WSET Level 1 to the Diploma?

It depends on your pace, but a common path takes several years. Level 1 is a single day, Level 2 a short course with a few weeks of revision, Level 3 several months of study and tasting, and the Diploma usually 18 months or more on its own. Leaving time between levels to taste widely makes each step easier.

Is WSET Level 1 necessary before Level 2?

No. Level 1 is a good introduction for complete beginners and new hospitality staff, but it is not a requirement for Level 2. If you already know the main styles of wine and a few grapes, starting with Level 2 is common and usually more useful. Take a short level test if you are unsure where you stand.

Which WSET levels include a tasting exam?

Levels 1 and 2 have no tasting exam, although tasting is part of the courses. Level 3 includes a tasting unit in which you assess two wines blind and write a full note using the Systematic Approach to Tasting. The Diploma includes several tasting exams with many more wines and a stronger focus on judging quality and readiness for drinking.
