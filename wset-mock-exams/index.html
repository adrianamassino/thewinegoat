WSET mock exams: how to practise the real exam before the day

In short

A WSET mock exam is a full practice paper sat with the real number of questions and the real time limit. Sitting two or three mocks in the final weeks, then reviewing every mistake, is the most effective way to find weak topics, train your pacing and walk into the exam without surprises.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

You can know the syllabus well and still lose marks in the exam room: a question read too fast, a topic you never revised, a clock you forgot to watch. Mock exams exist to remove those risks before they count.

This page explains why mock exams work, how to sit one properly, how to review the result, what the format of each WSET level looks like, and how The Wine Goat simulates them for wine and spirits.

Why do mock exams matter for WSET?

Mock exams test what you can retrieve under time pressure, not what you recognise in your notes. They reveal your weak chapters, train your pacing and make the real exam feel familiar.

Practice questions by chapter are excellent while you learn, but they give you a hint: you know the topic before you read the question. In the real exam, a question on Sherry follows one on Riesling and another on storage. A mock exam mixes the whole syllabus exactly as the paper does, so it measures the knowledge you can actually use on the day.

Mock exams also give you a realistic score. If you consistently reach 70% or more in Level 2 mocks, you have a comfortable margin over the 55% pass mark. If you hover around the pass mark, you know you need to act on your weak topics before the exam, not after.

What is the format of each WSET exam?

Levels 1 and 2 are multiple-choice papers with a fixed number of questions and minutes; Wine Level 3 combines multiple-choice questions, short written answers and a blind tasting. A good mock reproduces exactly that.

Every question carries one mark at Levels 1 and 2 and there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave a question blank. At Level 3, the theory and tasting units are marked separately: a strong theory score cannot compensate for a weak tasting, or the other way round.

How to sit a mock exam in real conditions

Treat the mock like the real exam: one sitting, the real time limit, no notes, no phone and no pausing. The closer the conditions, the more useful the result.

  • Choose a quiet moment when you will not be interrupted for the whole duration.
  • Set the real time limit and do not stop the clock, even for one question.
  • Close your notes, textbook and other apps; the exam is closed book.
  • Answer every question, flagging the ones you are unsure about to revisit at the end.
  • Check the clock at the halfway point: at Level 2 you should be around question 25 after 30 minutes.
  • For Level 3 written answers, write in full sentences as you would on the day, not in notes.

Do your mock at the same time of day as your real exam if you can. Your concentration curve is part of what you are training.

How to review a mock exam

The review is where the progress happens: spend at least as long reviewing a mock as you spent sitting it. Classify every mistake, then fix the cause, not just the question.

Sort your mistakes into three types

  • Knowledge gaps: you did not know the fact. Go back to the lesson for that chapter and add the fact to your flashcards.
  • Confusions: you mixed up two similar things, such as two regions, two methods or two label terms. Build a small comparison of the pair.
  • Reading errors: you knew the answer but missed a word such as "not", "minimum" or "main". Slow down on the question stem.

Look at the chapter, not only the score

A total of 68% hides very different situations. Losing marks evenly across the syllabus means you need broad revision; losing half of them in one chapter means a targeted session will lift your score quickly. Check your results by topic and plan the next few days around the two or three weakest ones.

Read the explanations of correct answers too

Some correct answers were lucky guesses. If you hesitated, read the explanation anyway and learn why the other options are wrong: that is how you will recognise the same trap next time.

When should you sit mock exams?

Sit your first full mock about three weeks before the exam, then one per week, and a final light one two or three days before. Earlier than that, chapter questions are more useful because you have not covered the whole syllabus yet.

Time before the examWhat to do
3 weeksFirst full mock: a baseline score and a list of weak chapters
2 weeksSecond mock after targeted revision; compare scores chapter by chapter
1 weekThird mock; aim for a safety margin above the pass mark
2–3 daysA light mixed set or a final mock, then only flashcards and your mistakes list

At Level 1 the timeline is shorter: two timed mocks in the last few days are usually enough. At Wine Level 3, add timed written answers every week and practise two-wine blind flights, describing each wine fully with the systematic approach.

Mock practice for Level 3 written answers and tasting

At Wine Level 3, a multiple-choice mock is only half of the rehearsal. Practise the written answers and the tasting under time pressure too, because they are where most marks are won or lost.

Written answers

Each short written answer is divided into parts, and the marks for each part tell you how many separate ideas the examiner expects. Practise reading the question twice, underlining the command words (describe, explain, compare) and planning your points before writing. Then mark yourself honestly against marking points: one mark for each correct, explained idea, nothing for a keyword without its reason.

Weak answerStronger answer
Cool climate. High acidity.The cool climate slows ripening, so the grapes keep high acidity, giving a fresh, light-bodied wine.
Oak. Vanilla.Maturation in new oak barrels adds vanilla and toast flavours and allows slow oxygen contact, which softens the tannins.

Blind tasting

For the tasting unit, pour two wines without looking at the labels and write a complete note for each in a set time: appearance, nose, palate and conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking. Check every structural element (sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol, body, flavour intensity, finish) and justify your quality assessment with balance, length, intensity and complexity.

Common mistakes with mock exams

Mock exams only help if they are honest. These habits make them less useful.

  • Sitting the same mock again and again until you remember the answers rather than the reasons.
  • Pausing the clock or checking notes, which gives you a score you cannot trust.
  • Looking only at the final percentage and skipping the review.
  • Taking the first mock too late to act on the results.
  • At Level 3, skipping the written section because it takes longer to mark.
  • Panicking after one low score: a mock is a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Pacing plans for each exam format

Know your checkpoints before you start: where you should be at the halfway mark and how much time you keep for flagged questions. A pacing plan prevents both rushing and running out of time.

At Level 3, the split between multiple choice and written answers is personal, but it must be decided in advance and practised. A common approach is to work through the multiple-choice questions at a steady pace, flag the doubtful ones, and give the written answers a fixed share of the time with a rough budget per question in proportion to its marks. Check your provider's instructions for the exact exam duration and rehearse with that number.

What your mock score tells you

Read a mock score as a range, not a promise: the real exam can be a few points higher or lower. The safer your margin above the threshold you want, the more confident you can be.

How to mark your own Level 3 written mock

Mark your written answers against marking points, one idea at a time, and be strict: a mark only counts if the idea is correct, relevant to the question and explained. Self-marking is a skill in itself, and it improves quickly with practice.

  • Read the question again and note how many marks each part carries: that is roughly how many separate ideas you need.
  • Compare your answer with the model answer and its marking points, line by line.
  • Tick a point only if your sentence states it clearly; a keyword without the reason does not count.
  • Circle any idea you wrote that is correct but does not answer the question; it earns nothing and costs time.
  • Rewrite the weakest part in two or three sentences, then file it with your mistakes to revisit in a week.

Mini case: one week to a Level 1 exam

A candidate with Wine or Spirits Level 1 in seven days and little time can still use mocks well. Day one: a first 30-question mock, untimed if needed, simply to see which chapters are weak. Days two to five: 15–20 minutes a day of lessons and questions on those chapters, plus flashcards. Day six: a second mock under real timing, 45 minutes, aiming well above 70%. Day seven: only flashcards and the list of mistakes. Two mocks, reviewed properly, are enough at this level.

Frequently asked questions

How many mock exams should I do before a WSET exam?

Two or three full mocks in the final three weeks are enough for most candidates at Level 2, provided you review each one carefully. At Level 1, two timed mocks in the last days usually suffice. At Level 3, combine weekly theory mocks with regular timed written answers and blind tastings of two wines.

What score in a mock exam means I am ready?

Aim for a safety margin above the pass mark. At Level 2, consistently scoring 70% or more in mocks gives you room for nerves on the day; at Level 1, aim for 80% or more because the pass mark is already 70%. At Level 3, check that both the multiple-choice and the written sections are comfortably above 55%.

Are WSET mock exams the same as the real exam questions?

No. Mock exams in The Wine Goat are written by our own team to reproduce the format, timing and difficulty of the real exam, but they are not past papers. Their value lies in practising the syllabus under realistic conditions and finding your weak topics, not in memorising specific questions.

How long is the WSET Level 2 exam?

The WSET Level 2 exam, for both wine and spirits, is 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes, which gives you 72 seconds per question. You need 55% to pass, 70% for merit and 85% for distinction. There is no penalty for wrong answers, so answer every question.

Should I repeat the same mock exam?

Repeating a mock straight away mostly tests your memory of the answers. It is better to sit a fresh mix of questions each time and to revisit your mistakes separately. In The Wine Goat, each mock draws a new set from the whole syllabus, and your mistakes come back in your personal review.

Can I do a mock exam for the Level 3 written answers?

Yes. The Wine Level 3 mock in The Wine Goat includes 4 short written answers alongside the 50 multiple-choice questions. After writing your answers, you compare them with model answers and marking points, which shows whether you explained each idea clearly enough to earn the mark.

How do I simulate WSET exam conditions at home?

Choose a quiet place and a time you will not be interrupted, set the real time limit, and put away notes, books and your phone. Answer every question without pausing the clock. If your real exam is on a computer, practise on a screen; if it is on paper, try noting your answers by hand. Small details like these make the real day feel familiar.

What if my mock exam scores are getting worse?

First, check whether the mocks are comparable: a new mock may cover chapters you studied long ago, which is exactly what it should reveal. Look at the scores by chapter rather than the total. A drop usually comes from older topics fading, which means your flashcard and mixed-question reviews need more time. Fix those chapters, then sit another mock a few days later.

How long should I spend reviewing a mock exam?

At least as long as the mock itself: about an hour for a Level 2 mock, and more for Level 3 written answers. Go through every wrong answer and every lucky guess, classify the mistake, read the explanation and decide one concrete action for each topic. A rushed five-minute review wastes most of the value of the mock.

Should I take a mock exam the day before the real exam?

Usually not. A full mock the day before can tire you and shake your confidence if one score is lower than expected. Sit your last full mock two or three days before the exam, then use the final day for light flashcards and your list of personal confusions. Rest is part of the preparation.

Are mock exams useful for WSET Level 1?

Yes, even though the exam is short. Level 1 needs 70% to pass, so careless mistakes matter more than at higher levels. Two timed mocks of 30 questions in 45 minutes in the final days show whether any chapter is weak and train you to read every question carefully. Review each mistake straight away; at this level, most are quick to fix.
