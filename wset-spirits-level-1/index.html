WSET Level 1 Award in Spirits: the complete guide

In short

WSET Level 1 Award in Spirits is a short beginner course on how spirits are made, the main spirit categories and the basics of tasting and cocktails. The exam is 30 multiple-choice questions in 45 minutes, with no marks lost for wrong answers, and you need 70% (21 correct answers) to pass.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

Level 1 is the gentlest way into the world of spirits. In a few hours you learn to tell a pot still from a column still, explain the difference between Bourbon and Scotch, read the age terms on a bottle of Cognac and balance a simple cocktail.

This guide explains who the course is for, exactly how the exam works, what each part of the syllabus covers, and how to revise efficiently so that 70% feels comfortable rather than tight.

What is the WSET Level 1 Award in Spirits?

The Level 1 Award in Spirits is an entry-level qualification that gives a sound but simple introduction to spirits. It is aimed at people with little or no knowledge who need to talk about spirits with confidence: new bar staff, restaurant teams, shop assistants and curious drinkers. By the end you should be able to name the principal categories, say what they are made from and how, and explain the key words found on their labels.

The guideline study time is about six hours, all of it guided by an educator and including the 45-minute exam. Many providers run it as a single day in the classroom; others teach it online over a few short sessions. There are no entry requirements.

Who should take Level 1?

  • People starting a job in a bar, restaurant, hotel or shop that sells spirits.
  • Staff who handle spirits as part of a wider drinks range and need the basics fast.
  • Enthusiasts who want a structured first step before deciding on Level 2.
  • Anyone who prefers to build confidence with an easier exam before a longer course.

If you already work with spirits every day, you may find Level 1 too basic and can enrol directly at Level 2, which is also designed for people without prior qualifications.

The Level 1 Spirits exam: format and pass mark

The exam is a closed-book paper of 30 multiple-choice questions, each with four options and one correct answer. Every correct answer is worth one mark and wrong answers do not cost marks, so never leave a question blank.

The 70% pass mark is the highest of the three spirits levels. It sounds generous because the questions are simple, but it leaves room for only nine mistakes. Precision on facts such as minimum ages and raw materials makes the difference.

How the 30 questions are split

The exam follows the three learning outcomes of the syllabus. Two thirds of the paper is about the spirit categories, so that is where your revision time should go.

Syllabus part 1: how a spirit is made

Every spirit is made in four steps: the raw material is processed to give a sugary liquid, yeast ferments the sugar into alcohol, distillation concentrates that alcohol, and finally the spirit is finished after distillation. Remember the key idea: fermentation creates alcohol, distillation only concentrates it. Questions on this point are classic.

There are two kinds of still. A pot still works in batches and produces spirits at a lower strength, which keep a lot of flavour from the raw material. A column still runs continuously. Distilled to a lower strength it can still give a flavourful spirit; distilled to a very high strength it gives a light or neutral spirit, such as the base for vodka. The rule to learn: the higher the distillation strength, the less flavour remains.

The syllabus then asks where flavour, sweetness and colour come from:

  • Flavour: the raw material, fermentation, the strength of distillation, ageing in new or used oak (or no ageing at all), blending, adding botanicals by redistillation or maceration, adding natural or artificial flavourings.
  • Sweetness: sugar added by the producer. Distillation itself never makes a spirit sweet.
  • Colour: time in oak, caramel colouring, or other natural and artificial colours. Unaged spirits are colourless unless something is added.
  • Dilution: spirits are reduced to bottling strength with pure water, which does not add flavour.

Syllabus part 2: the main spirit categories

This is the core of the course and the source of 20 questions. For each category, learn four things: the raw material, how it is made, any legal rules, and the words on the label. The table below summarises the essentials in a compact form you can revise from.

Other whiskies are covered more briefly. Irish whiskey is typically light and smooth, often a blend of pot and column whiskeys. Canadian whisky is usually a light blend of several grains. Japanese whisky follows a model close to Scotch, with malt and blended styles. You also need to recognise the main flavoured spirits: flavoured vodka, spiced rum, bitter spirits and liqueurs, plus vermouth, a wine flavoured with botanicals including something bitter, then lightly sweetened and fortified.

Two traps appear again and again. First, Bourbon does not have to come from Kentucky; it can be made anywhere in the United States. Second, a gold Tequila is not necessarily aged: joven or oro Tequila is usually unaged spirit with colour added, while reposado and añejo spend time in oak.

Syllabus part 3: tasting, storage and cocktails

The last five questions cover practical skills. A balanced cocktail weighs four elements against each other: strong (the base spirit), weak (water from ice, juices, sodas), sweet (syrups, liqueurs) and sour or bitter (citrus juice, bitters). Know the important ingredients too: fresh fruit juices, simple syrup and cocktail bitters.

Learn the main cocktail families by their structure rather than by memorising recipes. Spirit-forward drinks such as the Old Fashioned, Manhattan or Martini are mostly spirit. Short sours like the Daiquiri or Whisky Sour balance spirit, citrus and sugar. Highballs such as the Cuba Libre lengthen a spirit with a mixer. Long sours such as the Tom Collins or Mojito are sours topped with soda.

The published specification also lists basic storage and bar equipment. Spirits keep best upright, away from heat and direct light; a bottle stored badly loses aroma or develops dull, tired flavours over time. Recognise standard tools such as the jigger, Boston shaker, mixing glass, bar spoon, muddler and the Hawthorne, julep and fine strainers, and the common glasses: Martini or cocktail glass, Margarita glass, tumbler or Old Fashioned, highball or Collins, and shot glass.

Tasting basics for Level 1

There is no tasting exam at Level 1, but the course teaches a short, structured way of describing a spirit, and tasting is the best memory aid there is. Taste in a room without strong smells, in good natural light, against a white background, with a clean palate. A glass with a wide bowl and a narrower rim concentrates the aromas.

Pour a small measure of about 1.5 cl. First look at the colour, from colourless through pale lemon and gold to amber and brown. Then smell it and judge how intense the aromas are, from neutral to pronounced, and which families they belong to: fruit, flowers, grain, spice, herbs, vegetal notes or oak. Next add the same amount of water, which tames the alcohol and opens the aromas, and smell again. Finally taste: note the flavours, whether it is sweet, how it feels in the mouth, and how long and complex the finish is. Spit if you are tasting several samples.

A one-week study plan

Level 1 does not need weeks of preparation. Six or seven short sessions after the course are enough for most people.

  • Day 1: the four steps of production and the two stills. Explain them aloud in your own words.
  • Day 2: brandy and vodka. Learn the Cognac age terms until you can recall them instantly.
  • Day 3: Scotch and American whiskies. Make your own comparison of single malt, blended Scotch, Bourbon, Tennessee and rye.
  • Day 4: rum and Tequila, including what each Tequila label term really means.
  • Day 5: gin, other flavoured spirits, liqueurs and vermouth.
  • Day 6: cocktail balance, families, equipment and glassware, storage.
  • Day 7: a full timed mock exam of 30 questions in 45 minutes, then review every mistake.

Common mistakes at Level 1

  • Thinking distillation creates alcohol. It concentrates the alcohol already made by fermentation.
  • Mixing up the stills: the column still is continuous and can reach neutral strength; the pot still works batch by batch.
  • Forgetting that colour and sweetness can be added. Caramel and sugar are allowed in several categories.
  • Assuming Bourbon must be from Kentucky or that Tennessee whiskey is just Bourbon with a different name; the charcoal filtering is the key difference.
  • Reading too fast. Questions often hinge on one word such as 'minimum', 'must' or 'not'.

After Level 1: what next?

Level 1 is recommended preparation for Level 2, although not required. If you enjoyed the course, move on while the basics are fresh: Level 2 builds directly on the same categories and adds production detail, many more spirits and the classic cocktail families. How to read a spirits label: four worked examples

Many Level 1 questions are really label questions in disguise: they give you a term and ask what it guarantees. Working through imaginary labels is an efficient way to revise, because each term has to be placed in its category.

Cognac VSOP

The word Cognac tells you the spirit comes from grapes grown in a defined area, was double distilled in pot stills and spent at least two years in oak. VSOP adds a minimum of four years for the youngest brandy in the blend. It tells you nothing about sweetness, but you can expect that a little sugar and caramel may have been added, which is common in the category.

Single Malt Scotch Whisky

Single means one distillery, not one barrel. Malt means the only cereal is malted barley and the spirit comes from pot stills. Scotch means it was distilled and aged in Scotland for at least three years. Whether it tastes smoky depends on whether peat was used to dry the barley, and the label may or may not say so.

Tequila 100% agave, reposado

100% agave means every bit of sugar fermented came from blue agave, so the agave character is fuller than in a plain Tequila, which needs only 51%. Reposado means the spirit has rested in oak for a period, which softens it and gives a light golden colour without hiding the cooked agave and pepper notes.

London Dry Gin

Juniper is the dominant flavour, as in every gin, and the botanical flavours come from redistillation. The term describes how the gin was made and can be used anywhere in the world. It does not mean the gin was made in London, and it does not describe an age.

Making the most of the course tastings

The course recommends tasting at least nine benchmark spirits in a set order: a vodka, two Cognacs of different ages, a blended Scotch, a straight Bourbon, a white and a golden rum, a 100% agave Blanco Tequila, a London Dry Gin, and optionally a liqueur. Each one is chosen to illustrate a principle you will be tested on.

  • Vodka shows what neutral means: very little aroma, the texture and warmth of alcohol and almost nothing else.
  • Two Cognacs side by side show what extra time in oak does: deeper colour, more dried fruit and spice, a longer finish.
  • Blended Scotch and Bourbon contrast used and new oak: the Bourbon's vanilla and coconut are much more obvious.
  • White and golden rum show that colour and oak influence can vary within one category, and that colour alone is not a reliable guide to age.
  • Blanco Tequila shows the raw material speaking clearly: cooked agave, pepper, a herbaceous edge.
  • London Dry Gin shows how botanicals, with juniper leading, can create flavour on a neutral base.

After each tasting, write two or three words in your notes next to the category facts. Linking a fact to a sensation is the most reliable memory aid there is, and it makes descriptive questions much easier.

Exam technique for 30 questions in 45 minutes

The best approach is two passes. On the first pass, answer every question you are sure of and mark a provisional answer for the others. On the second pass, return only to the doubtful ones. This avoids spending five minutes on one hard question while easy marks wait at the end of the paper.

  • Read the whole question before looking at the options, then predict the answer in your head.
  • Underline key words: minimum, must, not, only, usually.
  • Eliminate options from the wrong category: a Cognac age term will not answer a Tequila question.
  • Change an answer only if you find a concrete reason; first instincts on familiar facts are usually right.
  • Check the end of your answer sheet or screen: every question must have one answer and only one.

Storage and service: details that bring easy marks

Storage questions are short and predictable. Unopened bottles keep best upright, at a cool and steady temperature, away from direct sunlight. Once a bottle is open, the spirit slowly reacts with the oxygen in the air and loses freshness. This happens faster in liqueurs with a lower alcohol content, and in any bottle that is more than half empty, because there is more air above the liquid. In a bar, that means rotating stock, closing bottles promptly and not leaving half-empty liqueurs on the back bar for months.

For equipment, remember what each tool is for rather than its shape. The jigger measures, the bottle pourer controls the flow, the muddler crushes fruit or herbs, the Boston shaker shakes, the Hawthorne strainer strains from the shaker, the fine sieve catches ice shards, and the mixing glass, bar spoon and julep strainer work together for stirred drinks.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions are in the WSET Level 1 Spirits exam?

The exam has 30 multiple-choice questions to answer in 45 minutes. Each question has four options and one correct answer, worth one mark. There is no negative marking, so you should always give an answer. About 20 questions cover the spirit categories, 5 cover production and 5 cover tasting, storage, service and cocktails.

What is the pass mark for WSET Level 1 Spirits?

You need 70%, which means at least 21 correct answers out of 30. Level 1 is graded pass or fail only, with no merit or distinction. Because the threshold is high, accuracy on details such as minimum ageing periods, raw materials and label terms is important, even though the questions themselves are fairly simple.

Is WSET Level 1 Spirits hard?

Most people find it approachable. The course takes about six hours and the questions test clear facts rather than interpretation. The challenge is the 70% pass mark, which allows only nine mistakes. A week of short, active revision, including at least one timed mock exam, is usually enough to pass comfortably.

Is there a tasting exam at Level 1?

No. Tasting is part of the course and you will usually try around nine benchmark spirits, but the exam itself is multiple-choice only. If you are under the legal drinking age or choose not to taste alcohol, you can still follow the course and pass the qualification without tasting.

Should I take Level 1 or go straight to Level 2?

Take Level 1 if you are new to drinks and want a quick, gentle start or a certificate for a new job. If you already work with spirits or have a wine qualification, Level 2 is usually the better choice: it is open to beginners and goes much deeper into production, categories and cocktails.

What does XO mean on a Cognac label?

XO is the oldest of the three main Cognac age terms. In current course materials, an XO must contain brandy aged for at least ten years, compared with at least four years for VSOP and two years for VS. The age refers to the youngest brandy in the blend, so many bottles contain older spirits too.

How long should I study for WSET Level 1 Spirits?

The guideline is about six hours including the class and exam. Most learners add four to seven short revision sessions of 20 to 30 minutes. Focus on the spirit categories, which make up two thirds of the exam, and finish with a full timed practice test.

Can I take WSET Level 1 Spirits online?

Many approved course providers offer Level 1 online, either as live virtual classes or as self-paced learning, sometimes with tasting samples sent out in advance. The exam is still supervised, either in person or remotely depending on the provider. Check how the exam is organised before enrolling, and make sure you can taste the recommended samples alongside the lessons.

What spirits are covered in WSET Level 1?

The course covers brandy with Cognac as the main example, vodka, Scotch and American whiskies (Bourbon, Tennessee and rye), a brief look at Irish, Canadian and Japanese whiskies, Caribbean rum, Tequila, and flavoured spirits: gin, flavoured vodka, spiced rum and liqueurs. Vermouth, cocktail balance, equipment, glassware and storage complete the syllabus.

Is WSET Level 1 Spirits worth it?

It is worth it if you need a quick, recognised foundation: for a new job in hospitality or retail, or to test your interest before a longer course. It gives structure to knowledge you may have picked up informally. If you already work with spirits daily, Level 2 is usually better value for your time, since it is open to beginners and goes much deeper.

What happens if I fail WSET Level 1 Spirits?

You can simply book a resit with your course provider, and there is no limit on the number of attempts. Use your result as a guide: review the area where you struggled, usually the spirit categories, practise with timed questions until you are consistently above 80%, and then sit the exam again while the material is fresh.

Is there a separate pass mark for each section of the Level 1 exam?

No. Your result is based on the total number of correct answers out of 30, and you need 21 to pass. There is no minimum per section. However, because 20 questions come from the spirit categories, it is very hard to reach 70% without solid knowledge of that area, so give it most of your revision time.
