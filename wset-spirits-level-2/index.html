WSET Level 2 Award in Spirits: the complete guide

In short

WSET Level 2 Award in Spirits is the core professional course for bar, retail and wholesale staff, open to beginners. The exam is 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes: 55% is a pass, 70% a merit and 85% a distinction, with questions split 14 on production, 23 on the principal spirits, 7 on flavoured spirits and aromatised wines and 6 on service and cocktails.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

Level 2 is where spirits knowledge becomes genuinely useful at work. You learn not only what Cognac, Mezcal or rye whiskey are, but how they are made, what the words on the label guarantee, and how to recommend or mix them with confidence.

This guide breaks the syllabus down by exam area, shows how many questions each area is worth, flags the classic traps and gives you a six-week plan to aim for merit or distinction rather than a bare pass.

What is the WSET Level 2 Award in Spirits?

The Level 2 Award in Spirits is an intermediate qualification that gives the focused product knowledge needed in bar service, retail and wholesale. Successful candidates can describe the principal spirits and aromatised wines, explain how they are made, and recommend spirits and liqueurs to suit a customer's taste.

Despite the name, you do not need Level 1 first. The course is written for people with little or no prior knowledge, although Level 1 or some practical experience makes it easier. The guideline workload is about 26 hours: 15 hours guided by an educator, including the one-hour exam, plus 11 hours of private study. The course recommends tasting around 29 spirits, from three Cognacs of different ages to gins, rums, Tequilas, Mezcal and liqueurs.

The Level 2 Spirits exam: format and grades

The exam is a closed-book paper of 50 multiple-choice questions, each with one correct answer, to be completed in one hour. Each correct answer earns one mark and there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so always answer every question.

With 72 seconds per question, time is rarely the problem; accuracy is. Questions are often built on precise details such as minimum ageing periods, legal percentages or the meaning of a label term. A pass can be reached with a broad overview, but merit and distinction require you to know the details of every category.

Almost three quarters of the exam comes from the first two areas. Production in particular is worth more than many candidates expect: 14 questions on stills, fermentation and oak can decide the difference between merit and distinction.

Area 1: how spirits are produced (14 questions)

Every spirit passes through four stages: processing the raw material, fermentation, distillation and post-distillation operations. A raw material gives the distiller two things: carbohydrates that will become alcohol, and congeners, the flavour compounds that give character. Some raw materials contain sugar ready to ferment (grapes, apples, sugar cane); others store their carbohydrate as starch (grains, potatoes) or inulin (agave) and must be converted into sugar first, by malting, cooking or added enzymes.

Fermentation turns sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and creates more congeners on the way. Distillation then heats the fermented liquid, collects the vapour and condenses it, concentrating ethanol and selecting the congeners the distiller wants to keep. The run is divided into heads (volatile, harsh compounds), hearts (the spirit that is kept), tails (heavy, oily compounds) and liquid waste. The higher the strength a spirit is distilled to, the lower its aroma intensity.

Learn the two still types as systems, not as lists of parts:

Pot stillColumn still
Way of workingBatch: filled, distilled, emptied, cleanedContinuous: fed and run without stopping
Key partsHeat source, pot, still head, swan neck, lyne arm, condenser, drainHeat source, plates, bubble caps, downcomers
Typical methodTwo distillations: the first makes low wines of about 25–30% abv; the second separates heads, hearts and tails, with previous heads and tails added backMany simultaneous distillations, one on each plate; heads, hearts and tails drawn off at different heights
Style producedMedium to pronounced aroma intensityFrom neutral to pronounced, depending on strength and design

After distillation, the spirit may be matured in oak or kept in inert vessels such as stainless steel or glass. For oak, the key choices are the age of the barrel, what it held before and how warm the warehouse is. Flavour can also be added by redistillation, maceration or flavourings. Blending brings consistency and complexity. Finally the spirit is diluted with water, may be coloured, sweetened or filtered, and is bottled.

Area 2: the principal spirits (23 questions)

This is the largest area. For every category, learn the raw material, the legal production rules, the main styles and the label terms.

Fruit spirits. Cognac comes from a defined area of France, mostly from Ugni Blanc grapes, is double distilled in pot stills and must spend at least two years in oak. Learn the sub-regions that appear on labels (Grande Champagne, Petite Champagne, Borderies, and Fine Champagne for a Grande and Petite Champagne blend with at least half Grande Champagne) and the age terms: VS at least two years, VSOP four, and XO, Napoléon or Hors d'âge older still, with XO now set at ten years. Armagnac uses Ugni Blanc, Folle Blanche and Baco, is usually column distilled to a low strength (double pot distillation is allowed), and has two extra label terms: Blanche, unaged and rested in inert vessels, and vintage. Calvados is made from apples and pears and aged in oak. Grappa is an Italian spirit made from grape pomace, typically unaged. Pisco from Peru and Chile is made from aromatic grapes and is typically unaged.

Whiskies. In the USA, Bourbon needs at least 51% corn and Rye at least 51% rye, both distilled to a low strength and matured in charred new oak barrels. Tennessee whiskey is made in Tennessee and filtered through maple charcoal before ageing, a step known as the Lincoln County Process. Know the label terms: Straight (at least two years in new oak, nothing added), proof (twice the abv), sour mash, single barrel and small batch. All Scotch must be distilled and matured in Scotland in oak for at least three years. Malt whisky is 100% malted barley from pot stills; grain whisky is column distilled from malted barley and other grains; blends combine both. Learn the regions: Speyside, Highland, Lowland, Islay and Campbeltown, plus terms such as single, age statement, cask strength and non-chill filtered. Ireland is known for triple pot distillation, Canada for blends of corn whiskies and flavouring whiskies, and Japan for malt, grain and blended whiskies on a Scottish model.

Rum. Rum is made from sugar cane juice or molasses, in pot or column stills, aged or unaged. Its styles are easiest to learn through production traditions:

Agave spirits. Tequila is made from blue agave grown in specified states, mainly Jalisco. The cores, called piñas, are usually steam cooked to convert inulin into sugar. Plain 'Tequila' needs only 51% of its sugars from agave; 'Tequila 100% agave' uses agave only. Blanco is unaged; joven or oro is usually unaged and softened and coloured with additives; reposado rests at least two months in oak; añejo at least one year and extra añejo at least three years in oak barrels. Mezcal, mostly from Oaxaca, often uses espadín agave, and its piñas are traditionally roasted in pits, giving a smoky character. Its classes are Mezcal, Artisanal Mezcal and Ancestral Mezcal, from least to most traditional.

Vodka. Any raw material can be used. The spirit is distilled to at least 95% abv in the USA or 96% in the EU in column stills, may be charcoal filtered, and comes in neutral or characterful styles. Poland, Russia, Sweden and Finland are traditional producers.

Area 3: flavoured spirits, liqueurs and vermouth (7 questions)

Flavour can be added naturally, by maceration, redistillation or bought-in natural flavourings, or artificially. Gin is the most important category. Juniper must dominate, usually supported by coriander seed, citrus peel, angelica root and orris root. In the EU the base is neutral spirit. Botanicals are added by redistillation in one of three ways: steeped in the diluted spirit in the still, held in a basket above it, or distilled separately and blended. London Dry Gin is a method, not a place: flavour comes from redistillation only, and afterwards nothing may be added except water, neutral spirit and a very small amount of sugar. Old Tom is a sweeter style.

Learn the other families too: aniseed spirits such as pastis and absinthe, which owe their flavour to anethole from aniseed, star anise or fennel; bitters made with quinine, gentian or bitter orange, used as aperitifs, digestifs or cocktail bitters; spiced rum and flavoured vodka; and liqueurs, which must be sweetened and can carry any flavour, even cream. Vermouth is an aromatised wine: a dry base wine with distilled alcohol, botanical extracts, sugar and sometimes caramel.

Area 4: the bar and cocktail families (6 questions)

Balance is the same idea as at Level 1: strong against weak, sweet against sour or bitter. Know your workstation (ice well, speed rail, sink, drip mat), your tools (jigger, shaker, mixing glass, bar spoon, Hawthorne and julep strainers, fine sieve, muddler, channel knife, ice scoop) and the purpose of each glass, from coupe to highball. A six-week study plan

  • Week 1: production. Draw a pot still and a column still from memory and explain each part.
  • Week 2: fruit spirits. Build a comparison of Cognac and Armagnac, then add Calvados, Grappa and Pisco.
  • Week 3: whiskies. Separate American, Scotch and other countries; learn every label term.
  • Week 4: rum, Tequila, Mezcal and vodka.
  • Week 5: gin, aniseed spirits, bitters, liqueurs, vermouth, and the cocktail families.
  • Week 6: two or three timed mock exams of 50 questions, plus a review of every error.

Common traps at Level 2

  • Confusing low wines (first distillation) with the final spirit from the second distillation.
  • Believing the hearts are collected first: heads come first, then hearts, then tails.
  • Thinking London Dry Gin must be made in London.
  • Mixing up Straight (an American whiskey term) with Single (one distillery, used in Scotch).
  • Forgetting that Mezcal can be unaged too, and that smoke comes from roasting, not from oak.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions are in the WSET Level 2 Spirits exam?

There are 50 multiple-choice questions, to be answered in 60 minutes. Each has one correct answer and is worth one mark, with no penalty for wrong answers. The split is 14 questions on production, 23 on the principal spirits, 7 on flavoured spirits, liqueurs and aromatised wines, and 6 on bar equipment and cocktails.

What is the pass mark for WSET Level 2 Spirits?

The pass mark is 55%, or 28 correct answers out of 50. A merit needs 70% (35 correct) and a distinction 85% (43 correct). Results of 45 to 54% are a fail, and 44% or below is a fail unclassified. If you pass, you cannot resit to improve the grade, so aim high the first time.

Is WSET Level 2 Spirits hard?

It is a clear step up from Level 1 in the volume of detail. The questions are not tricky, but they test precise facts: legal percentages, ageing periods, still parts and label terms. With the recommended 11 hours of private study and a few timed mock exams, most candidates pass, and a well-organised learner can reach merit or distinction.

Do I need WSET Level 1 before Level 2 Spirits?

No. The Level 2 course is designed for people with little or no prior knowledge of spirits, so you can enrol directly. Level 1 is recommended preparation, and some experience behind a bar or in a shop helps, but neither is required. If you start directly at Level 2, review the four production steps early.

Is there a tasting exam in WSET Level 2 Spirits?

No. The exam is entirely multiple-choice. Tasting is still a big part of the course, with around 29 recommended samples, and it helps you remember the categories. A few questions may ask which description best fits a spirit, such as a colourless spirit with floral and grape aromas, so tasting with a method pays off.

Which cocktails do I need to know for WSET Level 2 Spirits?

Learn four families: highballs (Cuba Libre, Moscow Mule), sours (Daiquiri, Whisky Sour, Pisco Sour, Tommy's, with Sidecar, Margarita and Cosmopolitan as variations), Collins drinks (Tom Collins, Vodka Collins, with Mojito and Paloma as variations) and spirit-forward drinks (Old Fashioned, Manhattan, Martini, Sazerac, Negroni). Know the structure, not exact measures.

How long should I study for WSET Level 2 Spirits?

The official guideline is about 26 hours: 15 hours of guided learning including the exam, and 11 hours of private study. Spreading your revision over four to six weeks, with short daily sessions and a full mock exam each week near the end, works better than cramming.

What does 'Straight' mean on an American whiskey label?

Straight means the whiskey has been matured for at least two years in new oak barrels and has had no colour or flavour added. It applies to Bourbon and rye whiskey. If a straight whiskey is younger than four years, its age must be stated on the label. It is one of the most frequently tested label terms.
