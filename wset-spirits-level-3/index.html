WSET Level 3 Award in Spirits: the complete guide

In short

WSET Level 3 Award in Spirits tests whether you can explain how production choices shape a spirit's style and quality. The theory exam has 50 multiple-choice questions and 10 short written answers in 2 hours 10 minutes; the tasting exam asks you to assess two spirits blind in 30 minutes, 25 marks each. You must score at least 55% in all three parts, and your grade is their average: 55% pass, 65% merit, 80% distinction with no part below 65%.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

Level 3 is the most demanding spirits qualification before diploma-level study. Knowing that Bourbon matures in new charred oak is no longer enough: you need to explain what that oak does to colour, aroma and texture, recognise the result in a glass, and judge whether the spirit is good, very good or outstanding.

This guide sets out the exam structure precisely, explains how the grading and the distinction rule work, lists what can appear in the blind tasting, and shows how to answer the 'explain' questions that decide most grades.

What is the WSET Level 3 Award in Spirits?

The Level 3 Award in Spirits is an advanced qualification that develops a thorough understanding of how spirits are made and why they look, smell and taste as they do. It combines three skills: knowing the facts about the world's spirits, describing and explaining production choices, and tasting accurately enough to identify a spirit and assess its quality.

It suits people whose job involves choosing or explaining spirits: buyers, bar managers, sales and brand teams, educators and serious enthusiasts. Passing Level 2 or having equivalent knowledge is recommended before enrolling. The workload is substantial: around 84 hours in total, of which 32½ hours are guided learning (including 2 hours 40 minutes of exams) and 51½ hours are private study.

The exam structure

Level 3 is assessed by two units sat at the same session: a theory exam and a tasting exam. The theory exam itself has two parts.

PartFormatMarksTimePass mark
Theory part 150 multiple-choice questions50Shared 2 h 10 (about 40 minutes suggested)55% = 28 marks
Theory part 210 short written answers: six of 5 marks, three of 10, one of 2080Shared 2 h 10 (about 80 minutes suggested)55% = 44 marks
Tasting2 spirits served blind50 (25 per spirit)30 minutes (about 15 per spirit)55% = 28 marks

In the theory exam you manage your own time. A sensible plan is the one usually recommended: 40 minutes for the multiple-choice questions, 80 minutes for the written answers and 10 minutes kept in reserve for planning. Spending too long on the multiple-choice part is the most common way to run short on the 20-mark question.

The multiple-choice questions cover the whole syllabus: 10 marks on the principles of production, 15 on the selected global spirits and 25 on the other named global spirits. The written answers only cover the principles of production and the selected global spirits.

How grading and the distinction rule work

You must reach 55% in the multiple-choice part, the written part and the tasting separately. Fail any one and you do not receive the award, however well you did elsewhere. If you pass one unit and fail the other, you only resit the unit you failed.

Your final grade is the simple average of the three percentages: 55% to 64% is a pass, 65% to 79% a merit and 80% or more a distinction. The distinction carries one extra condition: no single part may be below 65%.

ExampleMultiple choiceWritten answersTastingAverageResult
A70%60%62%64%Pass
B84%72%70%75.3%Merit
C90%82%78%83.3%Distinction
D96%84%64%81.3%Merit: tasting below 65%
E92%52%80%74.7%Fail: written part below 55%

Example D shows why the tasting cannot be neglected: an excellent theory score does not compensate for a weak tasting if you want a distinction.

What the syllabus covers

The theory is organised in three blocks of increasing breadth but decreasing depth.

  • Principles of production: carbohydrates and their conversion, yeast and bacteria, fermentation styles, the physics of distillation (volatility, reflux, rectification), pot, column and hybrid stills, oak and inert vessels, blending, filtration, colour, sweetness, alcohol adjustment and packaging.
  • Selected global spirits, studied in depth: Scotch whisky, American whiskey, Cognac and Armagnac, sugar cane spirits of the Caribbean (especially Martinique and Guadeloupe, Cuba and Jamaica), Tequila and Mezcal, vodka, gin, and how flavours and colours are added from sources other than oak.
  • Other named global spirits, for multiple-choice only: other American whiskeys, Irish, Canadian and Japanese whiskies, shōchū, baijiu, soju, Brandy de Jerez, Pisco, South African brandy, Grappa, Calvados and other fruit spirits, more Caribbean rums, cachaça, bacanora, raicilla, sotol, flavoured vodka, aniseed spirits, aquavit, bitter spirits, cocktail bitters, genever, spiced rum, liqueurs, non-alcoholic spirits and aromatised wines.

The third block is worth half the multiple-choice marks. Many candidates focus on the famous categories and lose easy points on baijiu aroma types, the difference between Peruvian and Chilean Pisco, or how aquavit is flavoured.

Short answers and 'explain' questions

A short answer question asks you to state, describe or explain. 'State' and 'describe' need accurate facts. 'Explain' needs cause and effect: what the producer does, what physically or chemically happens, and how that changes the style or quality of the spirit. Only topics flagged in the syllabus can be asked as 'explain' questions; two notable exclusions are the species of oak and the distillation of agave spirits.

Examiners reward each correct, relevant point, so structure matters more than elegant prose. A reliable pattern for each point is: action, mechanism, result. For example, a question on why a tall column with many plates gives a lighter spirit might be answered like this: more plates mean more reflux and more rectification; heavier congeners condense and fall back rather than reaching the top; the spirit collected is therefore higher in strength and lower in aroma intensity, with a lighter body.

  • Read the command word and the marks: a 10-mark question needs around ten distinct, explained points.
  • Name the spirit and the stage: raw material, fermentation, distillation or post-distillation.
  • Link every fact to style or quality; a fact without its effect earns less.
  • Use precise vocabulary: reflux, congeners, esters, heads and tails, rancio, extraction, oxidation.
  • For the 20-mark question, jot a quick plan by production stage before writing.

The blind tasting: what can come up

The two samples are served in neutral bottles and come only from a defined list. Knowing the list narrows the possibilities and lets you practise efficiently.

TypePossible categoriesUseful clues
Scotch whiskySingle malt, blendedMalt and cereal notes; smoke if peated; sherry-cask dried fruit
American whiskeyBourbon, ryeSweet vanilla and coconut from new oak; spice and pepper for rye
Grape spiritsCognac, ArmagnacDried fruit, floral notes, rancio with age; Armagnac often more rustic
Sugar cane spiritsFrom molasses, from cane juiceToffee and treacle versus grassy, vegetal freshness
Agave spiritsTequila, MezcalCooked agave, pepper, herbaceous notes; smoke points to Mezcal
GinJuniper-forward, contemporaryPine-like juniper leading, or other botanicals pushed forward

Each sample is worth 25 marks: 2 for appearance, 5 for the nose, 10 for the palate and 8 for the conclusions. The palate carries the most marks, so make sure you comment on every element of it.

How to approach each blind sample

Work through the sample in a fixed order every time, so nothing is forgotten under pressure. Begin with appearance: clarity, the depth and hue of colour, and anything unusual such as clouding when water is added. On the nose, check the spirit is clean, judge its intensity on a scale from neutral to pronounced, and group what you find by origin: aromas from the raw material, from fermentation and distillation, and from oak and maturation. A small addition of water often reveals more.

On the palate, note sweetness, flavour intensity, texture and warmth, any bitterness, and confirm the flavours you found on the nose, adding new ones. Then judge the length of the finish and how complex it is. For conclusions, build a short argument before naming a quality level: is the spirit balanced, intense, long, complex and expressive of its category? Two or three well-chosen reasons support a level such as good, very good or outstanding far better than a single word. Finally, identify the category and type.

Typical errors are describing aromas without saying where they come from, giving a quality level with no reasoning, and over-identifying: a long list of guesses earns nothing, while an accurate description earns most of the marks even when the identification is wrong.

A study plan for Level 3

  • Months 1–2: production principles and the selected spirits. After each chapter, write two practice 'explain' answers and compare them with your notes.
  • Month 2–3: the other named spirits, using flashcards and short multiple-choice drills, since this block is worth 25 marks.
  • Throughout: taste at least twice a week from the examinable list, in pairs that are easy to confuse, such as Bourbon and rye, Cognac and Armagnac, molasses and cane-juice rum.
  • Final four weeks: full timed papers, a 20-mark question every few days, and blind flights of two spirits in 30 minutes.

Common mistakes at Level 3

  • Treating it like Level 2: lists of facts score poorly in the written part.
  • Neglecting the 25 multiple-choice marks on the other named spirits.
  • Running out of time on the written part after lingering on multiple choice.
  • Tasting without a fixed method, so the palate or the quality reasoning is incomplete.
  • Forgetting that every part must reach 55%, and that a distinction needs 65% in each.

Worked example: building a 10-mark 'explain' answer

The fastest way to understand what examiners want is to see an answer take shape. Take a typical question: explain how the choice of oak vessel can influence the style of a matured spirit. Ten marks means about ten distinct, explained points, and the cleanest structure follows the vessel's design, its previous use and how it is used.

  • Size: a smaller barrel has more wood surface per litre of spirit, so extraction of colour and oak flavour is faster.
  • Toasting: heating the staves breaks down wood compounds, creating sweet spice, vanilla and toasted notes in the spirit.
  • Charring: a heavy char creates a layer of carbon that removes some harsh, sulfury compounds and gives colour and sweet caramel-like notes.
  • New oak: it has the most to give, so it brings pronounced vanilla, coconut and spice quickly, as in American whiskey.
  • Refill casks: each use leaves less to extract, so the spirit's own character stays more visible and maturation can last longer without overwhelming it.
  • Previous contents: a cask that held a fortified wine releases some of it back, adding dried fruit and nutty notes.
  • Oxygen: oak lets small amounts of oxygen in, which over time develops more complex aromas and, with long ageing, rancio.
  • Warehouse temperature: heat speeds up the exchange between wood and spirit, so the same cask matures faster in a hot climate.
  • Humidity and evaporation: losses of water and alcohol concentrate the spirit and influence its final strength and texture.
  • Finishing: moving a spirit into a second cask for a short period adds a layer of flavour on top of the original maturation.

Notice the pattern in each line: a choice, what happens, and the effect in the glass. A list of cask types without their effects would score far less, even if every fact were correct.

The selected global spirits: where 'explain' questions come from

The written part covers only the principles of production and the selected global spirits, so this is where your deepest preparation should go. For each category, link two or three production choices to their effect on style.

SpiritProduction choices worth explaining
Scotch whiskyPeat level in malting, fermentation length, still shape and reflux, cut points, cask type and previous contents, blending of malt and grain
American whiskeyMash bill (corn, rye, wheat, malted barley), sour mash, distillation strength, new charred oak, warehouse position and temperature
Cognac and ArmagnacGrape variety, distilling on the lees, double pot still versus column, the age and size of casks, blending and the house style
Caribbean sugar cane spiritsCane juice versus molasses, fermentation length and esters, pot stills with retorts versus columns, tropical maturation
Tequila and MezcalCooking method (ovens, autoclaves, pits), extraction of sugars, 100% agave or not, rest in oak
VodkaRaw material, column design and distillation strength, filtration, texture-building choices
GinBase spirit, botanical recipe, maceration versus vapour infusion, what is added after redistillation

Remember the two exclusions: species of oak and the distillation of agave spirits will not be asked as 'explain' questions. They can still appear in multiple choice.

Half of the multiple-choice marks come from this block, and it contains several spirits that are new to most candidates. A short orientation table is the best starting point before going deeper.

Treat this block like a vocabulary list: one card per spirit, with origin, raw material, key process and one label term. Short daily drills of 10 to 15 questions are more effective here than long reading sessions.

Time management in the theory paper

The theory exam rewards discipline. With 2 hours 10 minutes for 50 multiple-choice questions and 80 marks of written answers, a fixed plan prevents panic.

TimeTask
0:00-0:05Read all ten written questions and note two or three key words beside each
0:05-0:45Multiple choice, two passes; flag doubts and move on
0:45-1:00The 20-mark question, starting with a plan by production stage
1:00-1:45The three 10-mark questions, then the six 5-mark questions
1:45-2:10Return to flagged multiple choice; add missing points to written answers

Starting the written part with the 20-mark question protects the most valuable marks. Writing bullet-style sentences is perfectly acceptable as long as each one makes a complete, explained point.

A candidate with three months and a full-time job

Consider a bar manager aiming for Level 3 in twelve weeks. About four and a half hours a week covers the private study recommended, if used well: three weekday sessions of 45 minutes for theory, one weekend session of an hour and a half for tasting and a written answer, and short question drills on the commute. In the last three weeks, one full timed theory paper per week and a blind flight of two spirits every few days turn knowledge into exam performance.

Frequently asked questions

What is the format of the WSET Level 3 Spirits exam?

There are two units sat at the same session. The theory exam lasts 2 hours 10 minutes and has 50 multiple-choice questions plus 10 short written answers worth 80 marks. The tasting exam lasts 30 minutes and asks you to assess two spirits served blind, each worth 25 marks. You must pass all three parts.

What is the pass mark for WSET Level 3 Spirits?

You need at least 55% in each part: 28 of 50 in the multiple-choice questions, 44 of 80 in the written answers and 28 of 50 in the tasting. The final grade is the average of the three percentages: 55% for a pass, 65% for a merit and 80% for a distinction.

How do you get a distinction in WSET Level 3 Spirits?

Your average across the multiple-choice part, the written part and the tasting must be 80% or more, and no individual part may be below 65%. A very high theory score therefore cannot make up for a tasting result of, say, 64%. Balanced preparation across all three parts is the key.

Which spirits can come up in the Level 3 blind tasting?

Only spirits from a defined list: single malt or blended Scotch, Bourbon or rye whiskey, Cognac or Armagnac, sugar cane spirits made from molasses or from cane juice, Tequila or Mezcal, and gin in a juniper-forward or contemporary style. You write a full description, assess quality with reasons and identify each spirit.

What are 'explain' questions in WSET Level 3 Spirits?

They are short written questions that ask why a production choice affects a spirit's style or quality. A good answer links what the producer does, what happens in the process and the resulting effect in the glass. Only topics flagged in the syllabus can be asked this way; oak species and agave distillation are excluded.

Can I take WSET Level 3 Spirits without Level 2?

Level 2 or an equivalent level of knowledge is recommended rather than strictly required. If you have strong practical experience, discuss it with your course provider before enrolling. Without Level 2 knowledge, the volume of facts and the pace of the course can be overwhelming, so many candidates take Level 2 first.

How long does it take to prepare for WSET Level 3 Spirits?

The guideline is about 84 hours, including 51½ hours of private study. In practice most candidates spread this over three to four months, with regular tasting and timed practice. The written answers and blind tasting take longer to master than facts, so start practising them early.

What if I do not drink alcohol?

Candidates who are under the legal age or choose not to taste alcohol cannot sit the tasting unit. They can still complete the theory and receive a record of achievement for it, but they will not be awarded the full Level 3 qualification, which requires both units.

Is WSET Level 3 Spirits much harder than Level 2?

Yes. The workload is roughly three times larger, about 84 hours against 26, and the skills are different. Level 2 asks what a spirit is and how it is made. Level 3 asks why production choices change style and quality, in written answers, and adds a blind tasting with a quality judgement. Facts alone are not enough to pass.

How should I practise for the Level 3 Spirits tasting exam?

Taste in pairs from the examinable list, with the bottles hidden, and give yourself 15 minutes per sample. Write a full note every time, link aromas to the raw material, processing or oak, and justify your quality level with two or three reasons. Compare your notes with a classmate or educator, and repeat the confusable pairs, such as Bourbon and rye, most often.

Do I need to know baijiu and shōchū for WSET Level 3 Spirits?

Yes, for the multiple-choice part. Baijiu, shōchū and soju belong to the other named global spirits, which are worth 25 of the 50 multiple-choice marks. You need their key production choices, styles and main label terms, such as the four baijiu aroma types or the meaning of Honkaku Shōchū, but they will not appear in the written answers.

How many marks is the 20-mark question worth in the Level 3 exam?

It is worth 20 of the 80 marks in the written part, a quarter of that part on its own. That is why it should be planned before writing and answered early in the session. A good answer is organised by production stage, makes around twenty distinct points and links each one to its effect on the spirit's style or quality.

Can I use bullet points in the Level 3 short answers?

Yes. Examiners mark the content, not the style, and award marks for each correct, relevant point. Short, complete sentences in bullet form are often clearer than long paragraphs. Make sure each bullet contains a full idea, ideally a cause and its effect, rather than a single word that leaves the examiner to guess what you meant.
