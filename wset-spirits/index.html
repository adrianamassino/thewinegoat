WSET Spirits courses explained: Levels 1, 2 and 3 compared

In short

The WSET spirits pathway has three levels: Level 1 is a short introduction tested by 30 multiple-choice questions, Level 2 is the working knowledge most bar and retail staff need (50 questions, graded up to distinction), and Level 3 adds written answers and a blind tasting of two spirits. Most people with no background start at Level 1 or go straight to Level 2; Level 3 assumes Level 2 knowledge.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

If you serve, sell or simply love spirits, the WSET spirits qualifications give you a structured way to learn what is in the glass and why it tastes the way it does. The three levels are very different in depth, exam style and time commitment, so choosing the right starting point saves you both effort and frustration.

This guide compares the three levels side by side, explains who each one is built for, and gives practical advice on how to prepare, whichever level you pick.

What are the WSET spirits courses?

The WSET spirits courses are a three-step series of qualifications covering how spirits are made, the main categories of the world, tasting technique and cocktail basics. Each level builds on the one below: Level 1 names the categories, Level 2 explains how they are produced and labelled, and Level 3 asks you to explain why production choices change style and quality.

The courses are taught by independent, approved course providers around the world, in a classroom or online, and every level ends with a closed-book exam. You cannot sit the exam on your own: you enrol with a provider, follow the course and take the exam they organise. Two numbers stand out. The pass mark falls from 70% at Level 1 to 55% at Levels 2 and 3, because the questions become much harder; and the study time jumps sharply at Level 3, which is roughly three times the workload of Level 2.

Level 1: a short, friendly introduction

Level 1 Award in Spirits is a one-day style introduction for people with little or no knowledge of spirits. It covers the four steps of production (processing the raw material, fermentation, distillation and what happens after distillation), the difference between pot and column stills, and the key facts about the main families: brandy with Cognac as the example, vodka, Scotch and American whiskies, Caribbean rum, Tequila, gin, other flavoured spirits and liqueurs. It also introduces a simple tasting method and how to balance a cocktail.

The exam is 30 multiple-choice questions in 45 minutes, and you need 21 correct answers (70%) to pass. Most questions, 20 of the 30, test the spirit categories. If you work front of house and need to answer customer questions with confidence, Level 1 is enough to get started. Read the full Level 1 guide for the syllabus and a study plan.

Level 2: the working professional's level

Level 2 Award in Spirits is the level most bartenders and retail staff aim for. Despite the bigger jump in content, it is still open to beginners: no previous qualification is required. It explains production in detail (still parts, heads, hearts and tails, oak versus inert vessels), adds many more categories (Armagnac, Calvados, Grappa, Pisco, Irish, Canadian and Japanese whiskies, Mezcal, cachaça, aniseed spirits, bitters, vermouth) and covers bar equipment and the main cocktail families.

The exam has 50 multiple-choice questions in one hour. A pass needs 55%, a merit 70% and a distinction 85%. Questions are split by area: 14 on production, 23 on the principal spirits, 7 on flavoured spirits and aromatised wines, and 6 on service and cocktails. See the Level 2 guide for a six-week plan.

Level 3: description, explanation and blind tasting

Level 3 Award in Spirits is a serious step up. You are no longer asked only what a spirit is, but why it looks, smells and tastes as it does. The theory exam combines 50 multiple-choice questions with 10 short written answers worth 80 marks, all in 2 hours 10 minutes. A separate 30-minute tasting exam asks you to describe two spirits served blind, assess their quality and identify what they are.

You must pass the multiple-choice part, the written part and the tasting, each with at least 55%. Your final grade is the average of the three: 55% for a pass, 65% for merit and 80% for distinction, with the extra condition that no part may fall below 65% for a distinction. The Level 3 guide explains the written questions and the blind tasting in detail.

Which level should you choose?

Start from what you need the qualification for, not from how ambitious you feel. These situations cover most learners:

  • You are new to drinks and starting a bar or shop job: Level 1 gives you the vocabulary quickly. Move on to Level 2 within a few months while the basics are fresh.
  • You already pour or sell spirits every day: go straight to Level 2. Your practical experience will cover much of Level 1, and Level 2 is the one employers tend to ask about.
  • You hold a wine qualification but are new to spirits: Level 2 is usually the right start. Tasting skills transfer, but spirits production is a separate subject.
  • You buy, train others or work for a producer: aim for Level 3, after Level 2 or equivalent experience. Expect to write structured explanations and to taste blind.
  • You cannot or do not wish to taste alcohol: Levels 1 and 2 can be passed without tasting. At Level 3 you can complete the theory and receive a record of achievement, but the full award needs the tasting unit.

A common mistake is to treat Level 1 as compulsory. It is not: Level 2 is designed for people with little or no prior knowledge. Level 1 is still useful if you want a gentle start or a quick certificate for a new job.

How to prepare for any WSET spirits exam

The best preparation is active: you remember facts far better when you retrieve them than when you reread them. These habits work at every level.

  • Learn the syllabus structure first. Each exam is weighted by area. At Level 2, 23 of 50 questions are about the principal spirits, so that is where most of your time should go.
  • Use numbers as anchors. Minimum ageing periods, raw material percentages and distillation strengths are easy marks once memorised: Cognac VS at least two years, VSOP four, XO ten; Bourbon at least 51% corn; vodka distilled to 95–96% abv.
  • Compare pairs. Many questions test differences: Cognac vs Armagnac, Tequila vs Mezcal, Bourbon vs Tennessee, single malt vs blended Scotch. Build small comparison tables of your own.
  • Practise with timed questions. At Level 1 you have 90 seconds per question, at Level 2 only 72. Timed practice makes the real exam feel calm.
  • Taste with a method. Even where there is no tasting exam, tasting helps you remember: a smoky single malt or a grassy cane-juice rum is easier to recall than a sentence in a book.
  • Review your mistakes. Keep a short list of every question you got wrong and why. Revisit it the day before the exam.

Common mistakes when choosing and studying

  • Underestimating Level 3: the written answers require you to explain cause and effect, not list facts.
  • Studying only the famous categories: liqueurs, vermouth and bitters are smaller topics but still bring questions.
  • Confusing label terms: 'Straight', 'Single', 'Reposado' and 'VSOP' all describe different things in different categories.
  • Leaving cocktails to the last day: at Levels 1 and 2 they are easy, reliable marks if you know the families.
  • Ignoring the pass mark at Level 1: 70% leaves room for only nine wrong answers out of 30.

Three learner profiles: which path fits you?

The right route depends on your job, your deadline and how much you already know. These three typical cases show how candidates with different starting points can plan their spirits study, and where each one usually loses time.

A new bartender with six weeks before a busy season

Someone who has just started behind a bar needs vocabulary fast and confidence with customers. Level 1 can be completed in a single day, and a week of short revision is enough to reach the 70% pass mark. The smarter plan, though, is often to enrol directly at Level 2: six weeks at 30 to 40 minutes a day covers the 11 hours of private study, and the cocktail families, bar tools and label terms are exactly what a new team member uses every shift. The risk for this profile is to learn only the drinks served at work and to neglect production, which is worth 14 of the 50 Level 2 questions.

A wine professional moving into spirits

Sommeliers and wine retail staff already know how to taste with a method, describe intensity and judge length. What they lack is distillation and the legal frameworks of each spirit category. Level 2 is the natural starting point: tasting skills carry over, and the new material is mostly technical. A common mistake in this profile is to rely on wine instinct for oak. In spirits, a new charred barrel, a hot warehouse or an ex-Sherry cask can change a spirit in a few years far more than oak changes most wines, and questions test those effects precisely.

A buyer, trainer or brand ambassador

People who choose spirits for a list, train staff or represent producers need to explain why one spirit differs from another, not just name it. For them, Level 3 is the meaningful target. If Level 2 is not already in hand, plan both back to back: Level 2 over five or six weeks, a short break, then three to four months for Level 3, with regular blind tasting from the first week. The time-consuming part of Level 3 is not memorising facts but learning to write structured explanations and to taste blind under time pressure.

How long to leave between levels

The best gap between two levels is short enough that the basics are still fresh and long enough to absorb them. As a rough guide, one to three months between Level 1 and Level 2 works well, because Level 2 reuses the same categories in more depth. Between Level 2 and Level 3, many candidates benefit from a few months of practical experience, tasting widely and reading labels at work, since Level 3 assumes that the Level 2 facts are automatic.

  • Gap too long: you will need to relearn the four production stages and the main label terms before the new course starts.
  • Gap too short: you may start Level 3 still unsure of Level 2 details such as Straight, cask strength or the Tequila age categories, and the course pace will feel brutal.
  • Ideal habit between levels: ten minutes a day of mixed review questions keeps old material alive with very little effort.

Exam day: what to expect at each level

All three spirits exams are closed book and supervised by your course provider. At Levels 1 and 2 you answer multiple-choice questions, either on a computer-readable answer sheet or on a computer or other device, depending on how your provider runs the exam. Nothing is deducted for wrong answers, so the only real strategy is to answer every question and flag the doubtful ones for a second look.

Level 3 is a different experience. The theory paper lasts 2 hours 10 minutes and you organise your own time between multiple choice and short answers. For the tasting unit, you pour your own two samples before the start, and it is your responsibility to make sure each sample goes into the right glass; you may not assess or note anything while pouring. You then have 30 minutes for both samples, with a spittoon and water on the table.

A pre-enrolment checklist

Before booking any spirits course, run through these points. They prevent the most common disappointments.

  • Check that you meet the age rules: candidates must be over the legal minimum age for buying alcohol in the country of the exam, or be studying as part of a recognised full-time programme, or have consent from a parent or guardian.
  • Decide whether you will taste. Tasting is optional at Levels 1 and 2 but required for the full Level 3 award.
  • Ask how the exam is delivered: on paper or on a device, in class or under remote supervision, and when results are released.
  • Block study time in your calendar before the course starts, especially for Level 2 (11 hours) and Level 3 (51½ hours) of private study.
  • If English is not your first language and you plan to sit Level 3 in English, a strong reading and writing level matters for the short answers.
  • Plan a few tasting sessions with friends or colleagues to share bottles; it makes the recommended samples far more manageable.

Spirits and wine qualifications side by side

The spirits and wine courses share the same three-level logic and the same idea of systematic tasting, but the subject matter overlaps less than people expect. Wine courses focus on grape growing, winemaking, regions and food pairing; spirits courses focus on raw materials, fermentation, distillation, maturation and mixing. Many professionals take both, because bars, restaurants and shops sell both.

If you hold a wine qualification, the main transferable skills are tasting discipline, the vocabulary of intensity and length, and a feel for how oak and oxidation behave. The new skills are the physics of stills and the very specific legal rules of each spirit category. Frequently asked questions

How many WSET spirits levels are there?

There are three spirits qualifications: Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3 Award in Spirits. Level 1 is an introduction, Level 2 gives the product knowledge needed for bar and retail work, and Level 3 adds explanation of style and quality plus a blind tasting exam.

Do I need Level 1 before WSET Level 2 Spirits?

No. Level 2 Award in Spirits is designed for people with little or no prior knowledge, so you can enrol directly. Level 1 is helpful if you want a gentle first step or a quick certificate, but many bartenders and retail staff with daily experience of spirits start straight at Level 2 and do well.

Can I go straight to WSET Level 3 Spirits?

Level 3 recommends that you have passed Level 2 or have an equivalent level of knowledge. If you think your experience already covers Level 2, talk to your course provider before enrolling. Level 3 assumes you know the main categories and their rules, and spends its time on explanation, quality and blind tasting.

Which WSET spirits level is best for bartenders?

For most bartenders, Level 2 is the sweet spot. It covers production, the principal spirits and their label terms, flavoured spirits, vermouth, bar equipment and the main cocktail families, all at a depth that helps behind the bar. Level 3 suits people who buy spirits, train teams or want to understand quality in depth.

Is there a tasting exam in WSET spirits courses?

Only at Level 3. Levels 1 and 2 are assessed by multiple-choice questions alone, although tasting is part of the course. At Level 3 you taste two spirits blind in 30 minutes, write a full description, assess the quality with reasons and identify the category and type of each one.

How long does it take to study for WSET spirits?

The official guidelines are about 6 hours in total for Level 1, about 26 hours for Level 2 including 11 hours of private study, and about 84 hours for Level 3 including more than 50 hours of private study. Spreading Level 2 over four to six weeks and Level 3 over three to four months works well for most people.

Can I retake a WSET spirits exam if I fail?

Yes. You can resit an exam if you are unsuccessful and there is no limit on the number of attempts. You cannot resit an exam you have passed just to improve your grade. At Level 3, if you passed either the theory or the tasting, you only need to resit the part you failed.

What does the WSET spirits pass mark mean in practice?

At Level 1 you need 21 correct answers out of 30. At Level 2 you need 28 out of 50 to pass, 35 for merit and 43 for distinction. At Level 3 you need at least 55% in the multiple-choice part, the written part and the tasting separately, and your final grade is the average of the three.

Do WSET spirits qualifications expire?

No. Once awarded, a spirits qualification does not expire and you do not need to renew it. That said, product knowledge does fade without use, and categories evolve: new label terms appear and rules are updated, as with the XO age for Cognac. If you are moving up a level after a long gap, review the basics before the new course begins.

Is there a minimum age to take WSET spirits courses?

Candidates must normally be over the legal minimum age for buying alcohol in the country where the exam takes place. Younger candidates can take part if they study as part of a recognised full-time programme or have consent from a parent or guardian. Candidates under the legal age do not taste alcohol, so at Level 3 they can only complete the theory unit.

Can I take a WSET spirits course online?

Many approved course providers offer online or blended courses, especially at Levels 1 and 2, with tasting samples sent to you or bought locally. Exam arrangements vary by provider, from classroom exams to remotely supervised ones. Level 3 includes a blind tasting exam, so check carefully how and where the tasting unit is organised before enrolling.

Is WSET spirits useful for a career in the drinks trade?

Yes, especially in bars, retail, wholesale, distribution and brand roles. Level 2 is widely recognised as proof of solid product knowledge for front-line staff, while Level 3 signals that you can explain style and quality, which matters for buyers, trainers and ambassadors. The qualification is most valuable when combined with practical experience and regular tasting.

Can I study wine and spirits courses at the same time?

You can, but it is demanding. The subjects share tasting skills, yet the content is largely separate: vineyards and winemaking on one side, distillation and maturation on the other. If you have a full-time job, taking them a few weeks apart is usually more effective, and finishing one exam before starting serious revision for the other avoids mixing up similar terms.
