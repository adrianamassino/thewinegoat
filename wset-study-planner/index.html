Plan Your WSET Exam Preparation, Week by Week

In short

Start from your exam date, count the weeks you have, and split them into three phases: learn the syllabus, consolidate with practice questions, then revise with timed mock exams. A short daily goal (20–45 minutes depending on the level) beats long weekend sessions, and the last two weeks should be about mistakes and mocks, not new content.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

A good WSET study plan is not a long list of chapters: it is a calendar that works backwards from the exam date and tells you what to do today. This page shows how to build one, with weekly templates for Wine Levels 1, 2 and 3 (and notes for spirits), a sensible daily goal, the three phases of revision and a clear plan for the final two weeks.

How do you build a WSET study plan from your exam date?

Count the weeks between today and the exam, subtract one week as a buffer, and divide the rest into three phases: learning (about half), consolidation (about a third) and final revision (the remainder). Then turn each week into a short daily goal.

Working backwards protects you from the most common planning failure: spending too long on the first chapters and rushing the last ones. It also makes the workload visible. If you have 84 hours of study to fit into six weeks, you will see straight away that you need about two hours a day, and you can decide whether to start earlier or accept a lighter target.

Step by step

  • Write down your exam date and your target grade (pass, merit or distinction where the level awards them).
  • Note the guideline study time for your level and the hours your course provides in class.
  • Count the weeks available and keep the last week free as a buffer for illness, work or a chapter that takes longer.
  • Assign chapters to the learning phase, roughly in syllabus order, with heavier chapters (regions, principal spirits) given more days.
  • Schedule one practice-question session per chapter and, from the consolidation phase on, one mock exam per week.
  • Convert the weekly load into a daily goal you can actually keep on a busy day.

What daily goal should you set?

Set a daily goal you can meet on your worst day, then exceed it when you have time. For most people that means 15–20 minutes for Level 1, 30 minutes for Level 2 and 45–60 minutes for Level 3.

A daily goal works better than a weekly one because memory depends on frequency. Thirty minutes a day for six days gives you six rounds of retrieval; three hours on Sunday gives you one. A small goal also keeps your streak alive on hectic days, which matters more for motivation than it sounds.

If you miss a day, do not double the next one. Simply meet the normal goal and move on; the plan has a buffer week for exactly this.

The three phases of WSET revision

Every good plan moves from learning new content, to using it in practice questions, to rehearsing the exam itself. The balance shifts as the date approaches.

PhaseShare of timeMain activitiesSign you are ready to move on
1. LearnAbout 50%Lessons chapter by chapter, new flashcards, 10–20 questions after each chapterEvery chapter studied once
2. ConsolidateAbout 30%Mixed practice questions, review of mistakes, contrast cards for confusions, first mock examMixed-topic scores stable above the pass mark
3. RehearseAbout 20%Weekly or twice-weekly timed mocks, targeted revision of weak chaptersMock scores comfortably above your target grade

Notice that practice questions start in the first phase, not at the end. Testing yourself straight after a lesson shows you what did not stick while the chapter is still fresh.

Spirits Levels 1 and 2

Spirits plans follow the same logic. For Level 1, one week on production and categories and one on tasting, service and cocktails is enough. For Level 2, give the principal spirits (whiskies, rum, agave spirits, fruit spirits, vodka) the largest share, since they make up almost half the exam, and do not neglect production, which accounts for more than a quarter of the questions.

How should you use the last two weeks before the exam?

Use the last two weeks to rehearse the exam and fix mistakes, not to learn new chapters. Two or three timed mock exams, a thorough review of every error and short daily flashcard sessions are the best use of this time.

  • Day 14 to day 8: one full mock under real timing, then spend the next two days on the chapters behind the mistakes. Repeat once.
  • Day 7 to day 3: a final mock, mixed practice questions on weak chapters only, flashcards every day.
  • Day 2: light review of label terms, numbers and your list of personal confusions. No new material.
  • Day 1: rest, check the exam time and location or online setup, sleep well. A tired brain loses more marks than one forgotten fact.
  • For Level 3: keep tasting until the end, writing full notes on every wine, but stop testing new regions in the last few days.

Common planning mistakes

Most plans fail because they are too ambitious, too back-loaded or too passive.

  • Starting with long sessions and burning out by week three.
  • Reading every chapter before testing anything, so mistakes appear only at the end.
  • Giving every chapter the same time, although regions and principal spirits carry far more questions.
  • Leaving the first mock exam to the final week, when there is no time left to act on the result.
  • No buffer: one bad week and the whole calendar collapses.

Short on time: how to compress a plan

When the exam is closer than the comfortable window, keep every phase but shorten each one, and cut by priority rather than by chapter order. Never cut the mock exams or the review of mistakes; cut extra reading and nice-to-have detail first.

Planning around an intensive or weekend course

If your course is condensed into a few days, split your private study into three blocks: a light start before the course, consolidation straight after it, and a rehearsal phase before the exam. The days of the course itself are for listening, tasting and taking notes, not for extra study.

  • Before the course (one to two weeks): read the first chapters and answer a few questions on each, so the course feels like a second pass rather than a first contact.
  • During the course: note every point the tutor stresses and every question that surprised you, and turn them into flashcards in the evening.
  • Straight after the course: chapter-by-chapter questions to find what did not stick, with the heavier chapters first.
  • Before the exam: mixed sets, at least two timed mocks and the review of your mistakes.

Planning a second attempt

If you need to resit an exam or a unit, do not restart the whole plan from the beginning. Start with a full mock to see what is still secure, then build a shorter plan around the chapters, or the unit, that cost you marks.

A second attempt usually needs four to six weeks for a multiple-choice paper: one week of diagnosis and targeted lessons, two or three weeks of mixed questions with heavy work on the weak chapters, and a final phase of timed mocks. For a Level 3 unit, focus the plan on that unit only: daily written answers marked against marking points for the theory, or two to three full tasting notes a week for the tasting. Keep your old flashcards running throughout, so the topics you already knew stay safe.

A weekly review ritual

Once a week, take ten minutes to check the plan against reality. Small adjustments every week prevent the large, discouraging rewrites that make people give up.

  1. Compare what you planned with what you did, without judgement.
  2. Check your scores by chapter: which two chapters are weakest?
  3. Move one session next week onto each of those chapters.
  4. Check the flashcard queue: if it keeps growing, add fewer new cards next week.
  5. Confirm the next mock date and protect it in your calendar.

Keep your weekly review on the same day each week, for example Sunday evening. The habit matters more than the exact day.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I study for WSET Level 2?

The guideline is about 28 hours for Wine Level 2, including class time, and about 26 for Spirits Level 2. Spread over four to six weeks, that is roughly 30 minutes a day plus a few longer sessions for mock exams. Aiming above the 55% pass mark, around 70% in mocks, gives you a comfortable safety margin.

How long should I study for WSET Level 3?

The guideline total is about 84 hours for Wine Level 3, of which a large part is private study. Most people need ten to sixteen weeks at 45 to 60 minutes a day. Reserve regular time for short written answers and tasting notes from the start, because they are assessed separately from the multiple-choice questions.

Can I prepare for WSET Level 1 in a week?

Yes, for most people. Level 1 has a guideline of about six hours of study and a short exam of 30 multiple-choice questions in 45 minutes. A week of 20-minute sessions covering the principal grapes, service temperatures and food and wine pairing, followed by one or two timed mock exams, is usually enough.

When should I take my first mock exam?

Take your first full mock once you have covered most of the syllabus, around two thirds of the way through your plan. Doing it then leaves time to act on the result. Earlier, use shorter sets of practice questions after each chapter; later, take a timed mock every week until the exam.

What if I fall behind my study plan?

Do not try to catch up by doubling your daily goal. Keep the normal goal, use your buffer week, and cut low-value activities such as rereading notes. If you are still behind, shift time from the chapters you score well on to the ones you score poorly on. Moving the exam date is better than burning out.

Is it better to study every day or at weekends?

Every day, even briefly. Memory depends on how often you retrieve information, so six short sessions beat one long one of the same total length. Keep weekends for longer tasks such as mock exams, written answers or tastings, and use weekdays for flashcards, lessons and practice questions.

How many hours a week should I study for WSET Level 3?

Spread over about twelve weeks, Level 3 usually means five to seven hours of private study a week, on top of any class time. In practice that is 45–60 minutes most days, with a slightly longer slot for timed written answers or tasting. Weeks spent on large regions and winemaking can be heavier, and the final weeks shift towards mocks and reviewing mistakes.

Can I prepare for two WSET levels at the same time?

It is possible but rarely efficient. If you must, for example Wine Level 2 and Spirits Level 1 within a few weeks, give each exam its own daily slot and its own flashcard deck, and prioritise the earlier exam until it is done. Avoid preparing two levels of the same subject at once: the higher level assumes the lower one is already secure.

How do I plan if I do not have an exam date yet?

Pick a provisional date that matches the comfortable window for your level and plan backwards from it, as if it were fixed. A date, even a provisional one, turns open-ended study into a plan with phases and a daily goal. Once you book the real exam, adjust the phases. Without a date, it is easy to stay in the learning phase indefinitely.

How do I keep a study plan with shift work?

Plan by sessions rather than by days of the week. Decide how many sessions you need each week, then place them when your rota is published, preferring times when you are rested. Keep flashcards for short breaks and commutes, and put mock exams on days off. A minimum daily goal of ten minutes on working days keeps the memory and the habit going.

Should I plan rest days in my WSET study plan?

Yes. One light or free day a week makes a plan easier to keep and gives you a natural buffer when life gets in the way. On a rest day, a few minutes of flashcards is enough to keep reviews from piling up. Avoid long breaks of several days in the final month, when regular contact with the material matters most.
