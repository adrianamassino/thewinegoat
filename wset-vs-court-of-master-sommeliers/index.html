WSET or Court of Master Sommeliers: which certification is right for you?

In short

WSET or Court of Master Sommeliers? The differences in focus, levels, exams and careers, and how to choose the wine certification that fits your goals.

Updated 2026-11-15 · The Wine Goat

Both are respected worldwide, but they train for different jobs. WSET builds broad wine knowledge and analytical tasting for the whole trade; the Court of Master Sommeliers trains restaurant sommeliers, with service at its heart.

This comparison helps you choose – or combine them.

How to choose

  • You want to work on a restaurant floor: the Court’s service focus is a natural fit.
  • You work in retail, import, marketing or education, or you study for passion: WSET fits better.

Two philosophies of wine education

The easiest way to understand the difference between WSET and the Court of Master Sommeliers is to ask what each one is trying to produce. WSET is an education and qualification body whose wine courses aim to give anyone in or around the trade a structured understanding of wine: how it is grown and made, why styles differ, how to describe a wine precisely and how to judge its quality. Its graduates work in retail, import and distribution, marketing, journalism, education, hospitality and production, and many simply love wine.

The Court of Master Sommeliers is an examining body built around one profession: the restaurant sommelier. Its exams test whether a candidate can stand on a restaurant floor and deliver: know the wines and other beverages on a list, identify wines by tasting them blind, and serve them correctly and gracefully to guests. Theory, tasting and service are all assessed, and service is not optional.

Neither approach is better in absolute terms. They are built for different jobs, and that is why so many professionals end up holding qualifications from both. This guide explains the practical differences so you can choose a starting point that fits your goals, whether you live in an English-speaking market or in a country with its own strong sommelier tradition.

The WSET path in more detail

WSET's wine qualifications form a ladder of four levels. Level 1 is a short introduction with a multiple-choice exam. Level 2 builds a broad knowledge of grape varieties and regions and is also assessed by multiple choice. Level 3 is the first level that asks you to explain why wines taste as they do, with two separate units: a theory unit, combining 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written-answer questions, and a tasting unit of two wines tasted blind. Each unit needs at least 55% to pass, and if you fail one you retake only that unit. The Level 4 Diploma is a professional qualification divided into several units covering production, business and the wines of the world, including sparkling and fortified wines, with demanding theory and tasting exams.

Two features define the WSET experience. First, the teaching is structured and comprehensive: each level covers the whole world of wine at a set depth, so nothing is left to chance. Second, tasting is analytical: you describe a wine with the Systematic Approach to Tasting and reach conclusions about its quality and readiness for drinking. At Level 3 you are not asked to name the grape or region; the skill being tested is accurate description and logical evaluation.

For many people, WSET's main strength is its portability. The same qualifications are taught by approved providers in many countries and languages, which makes them a common reference across the international trade. Holders of the Diploma can also go on to apply for the Master of Wine programme, run by a separate institute, which is one of the most demanding qualifications in the wine world.

The Court of Master Sommeliers path in more detail

The Court's qualifications also form a ladder, usually described in four stages. The Introductory Sommelier Certificate combines a short intensive course with a theory exam. The Certified Sommelier Examination adds the elements that make the Court distinctive: a theory test, a blind tasting of wines using the Court's deductive method, and a practical service exam in which candidates serve wine to examiners acting as guests. The Advanced Sommelier Certificate raises the level of all three parts. The Master Sommelier Diploma, the final stage, is famously demanding and has a small number of successful candidates each year; its tasting part requires identifying several wines blind within a strict time limit, and its theory and service parts are equally rigorous.

Several points are worth noting. Theory at the Court covers not only wine but also spirits, beer, sake and other beverages a sommelier is expected to know, plus a sommelier's practical knowledge of the trade. Tasting is deductive: the candidate works from observation to a conclusion about grape variety, origin and vintage. And at the higher levels, candidates usually present much of their knowledge aloud, in front of examiners, rather than only in writing. The exact formats change from time to time and differ between the Court's organisations in different parts of the world, so always check current details directly with the organisation before you enrol.

Analytical tasting versus deductive tasting

The difference in tasting philosophy is one of the most important for your preparation, because it changes how you practise every day.

AspectWSET analytical tastingCourt deductive tasting
GoalDescribe the wine precisely and judge quality and readiness for drinkingDescribe the wine and identify grape, origin and age
Typical outputA complete written note with structured conclusionsA spoken or written description leading to an identification
What earns creditAccurate levels, specific descriptors, justified conclusionsAccurate description and a logical, correct identification
Time pressureA set time for a small number of winesAt higher levels, several wines in a short, strict time
Skill it buildsConsistent, evidence-based evaluationFast recognition of classic styles under pressure

Both methods share the same foundation: careful observation of appearance, nose and palate. A candidate trained in WSET's analytical method usually finds deductive tasting easier to learn, because the description is already disciplined. Moving in the other direction, a sommelier trained to identify wines often has to learn to slow down and to justify quality explicitly, rather than jumping to the name of a wine.

What a service exam involves

The service component is the clearest difference between the two paths. WSET's wine qualifications do not test table service. Court exams do, and they treat it as a profession with its own standards. In a typical service exam, candidates may be asked to open and pour a sparkling wine safely and elegantly, decant a red wine and explain why, recommend wines to match a menu, answer questions about drinks on a list and handle a guest's request with tact. Examiners judge accuracy, technique, hospitality and composure.

If you already work on a restaurant floor, service practice fits naturally into your daily job. If you do not, preparing for a service exam requires access to a realistic setting, practice with colleagues or mentors and many repetitions. That practical requirement is one reason why the Court suits people working in restaurants more than enthusiasts studying at home.

Sommelier courses in your own country

In many countries the word sommelier refers first to national courses rather than to the Court. In Italy, associations such as AIS offer multi-level courses that lead to a sommelier title and cover tasting, theory, food pairing and service, with a strong focus on Italian wines. In France, state diplomas for the hospitality trade, such as the sommellerie qualifications taught in hotel schools, train professional sommeliers, alongside the activities of national sommelier associations. In Spain, sommelier training is offered by hospitality schools and sommelier associations, often combining theory, tasting and service practice. Internationally, the Association de la Sommellerie Internationale brings together national associations and organises competitions.

These courses usually share the Court's focus on service and the local market, and they are often taught in the local language with a strong emphasis on national wines. WSET, by contrast, gives equal structure to every region of the world. Comparing a national sommelier course with WSET is therefore similar to comparing the Court with WSET: decide whether your priority is service on the floor or broad, transferable wine knowledge, then pick the course that matches.

Five mini case studies

The waiter who wants to become head sommelier

A young waiter in a fine-dining restaurant wants to run the wine list one day. Service exams are the logical goal, because that is what the job involves. A sensible route is to build theory first, for example with WSET Level 2 and then Level 3, while practising service daily at work, and then to sit the sommelier exams once the theory is solid. The analytical discipline from WSET makes the later deductive training faster.

The retail assistant

A shop assistant in a wine store wants to advise customers with more confidence. No table service is involved, so WSET Level 2 and then Level 3 give exactly what is needed: grape and regional knowledge, style descriptions and the ability to explain why one wine costs more than another.

The career changer

Someone moving from another industry into wine is not yet sure which job they want. WSET Level 3 is a strong neutral foundation: it is respected across the trade and keeps all doors open. Once a direction emerges, the candidate can add a service-based qualification or move towards the Diploma.

The enthusiast

A passionate amateur wants to understand wine deeply and enjoys structured study. WSET suits this profile best, because it does not require a professional service setting and offers a clear progression from Level 2 to Level 3 and beyond.

The sommelier who wants to move into buying

An experienced sommelier with service qualifications wants to become a buyer for a distributor. Adding WSET Level 3 or the Diploma strengthens their knowledge of production, quality assessment and the business side, which matters more in buying than table service does.

Combining the two paths

Holding both is common, and the two paths complement each other well. The most frequent combination is to start with WSET theory, which provides a structured map of the wine world, and then to add sommelier exams that test service and deductive tasting. The reverse order also works, especially for people already working on a restaurant floor, who may take an introductory sommelier course first and then deepen their knowledge with WSET Level 3.

Whatever the order, the theory overlaps substantially. The regions, grapes, winemaking techniques and laws you learn for one path are the same facts you need for the other. What differs is the format in which you show that knowledge: written explanations and analytical notes for WSET, oral and practical demonstrations for the Court. Training both formats makes you more versatile.

If you plan to take both, keep a single set of study notes organised by region and topic, then practise the exam formats separately. That way you learn each fact once and reuse it in every exam.

Common misconceptions

  • One is more prestigious than the other: both are highly regarded, but in different contexts; employers value the qualification that matches the job.
  • WSET is only for beginners: Levels 3 and 4 are demanding professional qualifications.
  • The Court is only about service: theory and blind tasting are equally important at every stage.
  • You must choose one path for life: many professionals add the other path later in their career.
  • Tasting skill transfers automatically: the foundation transfers, but each method has its own format and needs specific practice.

A short decision checklist

  • Will you work on a restaurant floor in the next few years? If yes, plan for a service-based qualification.
  • Do you need a qualification recognised across retail, import and education? If yes, WSET is a strong choice.
  • Do you prefer structured written study, or practical, oral demonstration? Choose the format that suits your learning style.
  • Is there a national sommelier course in your country that local employers value? Check what employers in your area ask for.
  • Can you practise service regularly in a realistic setting? Without it, service exams are hard to prepare.
  • Whatever you choose, are you building solid theory? Every wine exam rests on it.

A weekly routine that serves both paths

Whichever qualification you aim for, the weekly building blocks are remarkably similar, and organising them well saves months. A balanced week for a working candidate might look like this.

  • Three short theory sessions of twenty to thirty minutes, each on one region or topic, ending with a set of mixed questions.
  • One longer session to consolidate: redraw a regional map from memory, then explain the link between climate, grapes and style aloud or on paper.
  • Two tastings, even of a single wine, written up with a complete, disciplined note.
  • One session in the exam format of your chosen path: written answers for WSET, spoken descriptions and service drills for sommelier exams.
  • A short weekly review of every question you got wrong, so that weak points come back until they are fixed.

The proportions shift as the exam approaches. In the final month, increase the share of exam-format practice and full mock exams, and reduce new reading. What stays constant is regularity: short daily contact with the material beats occasional long sessions on every path.

Glossary

  • Analytical tasting: describing a wine methodically and evaluating its quality and ageing potential.
  • Deductive tasting: describing a wine methodically in order to identify its grape, origin and age.
  • Service exam: a practical assessment of table service, wine recommendations and guest handling.
  • Diploma: WSET's Level 4 qualification, a multi-unit professional course.
  • Master Sommelier: the highest title awarded by the Court of Master Sommeliers.
  • Master of Wine: a separate title awarded by the Institute of Masters of Wine, often pursued after the WSET Diploma.

Frequently asked questions

Is WSET or CMS more respected?

Both are highly respected; they simply measure different skills. WSET is the reference for wine knowledge across the trade, the Court for restaurant service.

Can WSET help me become a sommelier?

Yes: WSET theory and tasting give a strong base, and many sommeliers combine it with a service-focused qualification.

Which is harder, the WSET Diploma or Advanced Sommelier?

They test different things and both are demanding; choose based on the job you want rather than on difficulty.

Do I need WSET before taking sommelier exams?

It is not usually a formal requirement, but many candidates find it very helpful. WSET Level 2 or Level 3 gives a structured overview of grapes, regions and winemaking, which is the theory base that sommelier exams also test. Starting with WSET can make later sommelier study faster and less stressful, especially for candidates who do not yet work in a restaurant.

Is WSET Level 3 comparable to the Certified Sommelier exam?

They are often mentioned together because both are intermediate steps, but they measure different things. WSET Level 3 has a written theory unit and an analytical tasting unit, each needing at least 55%. The Certified Sommelier exam adds a practical service component and uses deductive tasting. The theory overlaps substantially, but the formats and skills assessed are different.

Can I study for the Court of Master Sommeliers without working in a restaurant?

You can study the theory and tasting anywhere, but the service component is much easier to prepare with regular practice in a real restaurant setting. Candidates outside hospitality often arrange practice with mentors or colleagues, or start with WSET while they gain floor experience. Check the current entry requirements and recommendations directly with the organisation before enrolling.

Which path is better if I live in Italy, France or Spain?

It depends on your goal and on what employers around you value. National sommelier courses are well known locally and focus on service and national wines, while WSET is internationally recognised and covers every region of the world with the same structure. Many professionals combine a national course with WSET to gain both local credibility and international breadth.

Does WSET teach blind tasting identification?

At Level 3, the tasting unit asks you to describe two wines blind and to reach conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking, not to name them. This analytical approach builds the observation skills that identification depends on. If you later prepare for deductive tasting exams, you will add style recognition on top of a disciplined descriptive method.
