WSET wine exam preparation: Levels 1, 2 and 3 compared

In short

WSET Wine Level 1 is a short introduction tested by 30 multiple-choice questions, Level 2 covers the grapes and regions behind everyday wines in 50 questions, and Level 3 adds written answers and a blind tasting of two wines. Choose the level that matches what you already know, then prepare with daily questions, flashcards and timed mock exams.

Updated 2026-11-18 · The Wine Goat

The three WSET wine levels share the same goal, understanding what is in the glass, but they ask very different things of you. Level 1 checks that you know the main styles and grapes, Level 2 that you can connect grapes, regions and labels, and Level 3 that you can explain why a wine tastes the way it does.

This page compares the three levels side by side, helps you pick the right one and explains how to prepare for each exam, with the tools The Wine Goat offers at every level.

How do the three WSET wine levels compare?

Each level adds depth and a new type of assessment: Level 1 and Level 2 use multiple-choice questions only, while Level 3 adds short written answers and a blind tasting. The study time grows from about 6 hours to about 84 hours.

Notice how the pass mark falls as the questions become harder. A 70% target at Level 1 sounds demanding, but the questions are simple; a 55% pass at Level 3 sounds generous, but you must reach it separately in the theory and in the tasting, and a strong result in one cannot compensate for the other.

WSET Wine Level 1: the first step

Level 1 is a short, beginner-friendly course on the main styles of wine, the principal grapes, storage and service, and food pairing. A few hours of focused practice are usually enough to feel ready.

The syllabus covers still, sparkling and fortified wines; how grape variety and climate shape style; the eight principal grapes (Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Riesling, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot Noir and Syrah); service temperatures and glasses; and how sweetness, acidity, salt and umami in food change the taste of a wine. With 90 seconds per question, time is rarely the issue: careless mistakes are, because 70% leaves room for only nine wrong answers.

Learn the food and wine interactions as a small table with arrows: sweet and umami food make wine taste more bitter and acidic; salt and acidity in food make it taste softer. Directions, not definitions, are what the questions test.

WSET Wine Level 2: grapes, regions and labels

Level 2 is the most popular wine qualification: it links the principal grapes and about 22 regionally important ones to more than 70 regions, and teaches how environment, winemaking and labels explain the wines you see on shelves.

The exam is 50 multiple-choice questions in one hour, with 55% to pass, 70% for merit and 85% for distinction. The questions reward connections: a region with its grape, a climate with its style, a label term with its meaning. Typical topics include oak and malolactic conversion, the traditional and tank methods for sparkling wine, Port and Sherry, and quality categories such as Crianza, Riserva, Kabinett or AOC/DOP.

How to prepare for Level 2

  • Build a grid for the eight principal grapes: climate, key regions, typical aromas, cool versus warm styles.
  • Learn regions as pairs with their grapes, then add the regionally important varieties country by country.
  • Turn labelling terms into flashcards and review them daily.
  • From three weeks before the exam, sit a full 50-question mock every week and aim for 70% to give yourself a margin.

WSET Wine Level 3: explain, write and taste

Level 3 asks you to explain causes and effects across the whole world of wine and to assess two wines blind. It is a significant step up and rewards regular study over two to three months.

The theory unit combines 50 multiple-choice questions with 4 short written answers, each split into parts where every mark rewards a correct, explained idea. The tasting unit asks for a full description of two wines with the systematic approach to tasting, including conclusions on quality and readiness for drinking; you are not asked to name the wine. You need at least 55% in each unit, and the units are marked separately.

How to prepare for Level 3

  • Study the vineyard and winemaking chapters thoroughly first: almost every regional question relies on them.
  • For each region, learn the chain climate → grape → winemaking → style → label.
  • Write one short answer every day or two and mark it against marking points: one idea per sentence, always with the "why".
  • Taste with the full systematic approach on every wine you drink, using only the exam vocabulary.
  • Sit timed theory mocks and practise two-wine blind flights in the final weeks.

Which wine level should you choose?

Start at Level 1 if wine is completely new, at Level 2 if you already know some grapes or work with wine, and at Level 3 after Level 2 or equivalent knowledge. Skipping Level 1 is common; skipping Level 2 is rarely a good idea.

  • You want a friendly introduction in a short time: Level 1.
  • You serve or sell wine, or simply want solid foundations: Level 2, with or without Level 1 first.
  • You hold Level 2 and want to understand the reasons behind styles and quality: Level 3.
  • You work in the trade and want the widely recognised professional standard: Level 3, and later the Diploma.

If you are unsure, try a few practice questions from each level. If Level 2 questions feel comfortable, you are probably ready to aim higher; if Level 1 questions already feel hard, start there and move on while the basics are fresh.

What comes after Level 3?

The next step is the Level 4 Diploma in Wines, a multi-unit professional qualification that requires Level 3 and takes many months of study. It is not part of this comparison, but it is worth knowing where the path leads.

The Diploma is organised in six units, D1 to D6, combining theory and tasting exams with a research assignment. Many people take Level 3 first simply to deepen their understanding and stop there; others use it as the foundation for a career in buying, education or wine writing. If the Diploma is your long-term goal, treat Level 3 as training in method: precise vocabulary, structured written answers and consistent tasting notes will all carry over. Common mistakes across the wine levels

The same few mistakes cost marks at every level. Most are about method rather than knowledge.

  • Confusing a grape with a place: Chablis is a region, Chardonnay is the grape.
  • Mixing up regions in the same country, such as Bordeaux Left and Right Bank or Northern and Southern Rhône.
  • Forgetting that the same grape tastes different in a cool and a warm climate.
  • Confusing sparkling methods: second fermentation in the bottle versus in a tank.
  • Leaving fortified wines, labelling terms and food pairing until the last week.
  • At Level 3, listing facts instead of explaining them, and using synonyms instead of the tasting terms the exam expects.

Whatever your level, review your mistakes before you add new material. A mistake you understand today is a question you will get right in the exam.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between WSET Wine Level 2 and Level 3?

Level 2 tests knowledge with 50 multiple-choice questions: which grape grows where, what a label term means, how a method works. Level 3 asks you to explain why, with 50 multiple-choice questions plus 4 short written answers, and adds a blind tasting of two wines. Level 3 also requires roughly three times the study time of Level 2.

Is WSET Wine Level 1 necessary before Level 2?

No. Level 2 has no entry requirement and is written for people with little prior knowledge, so many candidates start there directly. Level 1 is still useful if wine is completely new to you or if you want a quick, confidence-building first qualification before committing to a longer course.

How hard is the WSET Wine Level 3 exam?

It is a real step up. You must pass the theory and the tasting separately, each with at least 55%, and the written answers reward explained ideas rather than lists of facts. Candidates who study regularly over two to three months, write practice answers and taste with the systematic approach are well placed to do well.

How many questions are in the WSET wine exams?

Level 1 has 30 multiple-choice questions in 45 minutes and Level 2 has 50 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes. The Level 3 theory unit has 50 multiple-choice questions and 4 short written answers, and the tasting unit asks you to describe two wines served blind.
